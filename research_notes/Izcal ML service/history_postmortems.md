# Izcal ML service: history and postmortems

Compiled 21 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred (papers, specifications, maintainers' posts, release notes, issue trackers).
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only.

M-1 note: this file records dates, published numbers and stated reasons. No third-party source code was opened; no value is derived from one. Vendor documentation, release notes, blog posts, support pages, specifications, an FTC-adjacent law-firm write-up and press reporting were used. Where a row says what a product "does", that is evidence to reason from, not a shape to copy.

**Method notes**
- The shared web-search budget ran out during this strand (after roughly 25 searches). Everything after that point was read from known primary URLs directly; rows that rest on search-result summaries or recollection are graded Medium or Low and repeated under "Claims not verified".
- Chrome's built-in AI documentation, Chrome 148 release notes, the Chrome 138 and I/O 2026 posts, Google's 2019 and 2021 blog posts, Mozilla's 2024 Hacks post, Apple's two foundation-model posts and Apple's storage support page, Microsoft's 2025 and 2026 Edge posts, Google's MediaPipe and LiteRT.js posts, the W3C WebNN draft and Brave's October 2026 post were read as extracted text; quotes below come from that text.
- The brief's framing "Chrome's silent ~4 GB download criticism (2025)" does not match what the sources show: the model shipped from 2024, but the public consent backlash is dated **May 2026**. Both dates are recorded separately rather than forcing the 2025 framing.
- The brief's "Chrome Live Translate" is not a product name that any source found. The shipped capability is the **Translator API** (Chrome 138, June 2025); live caption translation is built by extensions on top of it. This is recorded as a finding, not smoothed over.

## 1. Before the LLM: OCR and speech as the first on-device ML

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Tesseract** was built at HP from 1985 to 1994, open-sourced in 2005 and sponsored by Google from 2006. It is the reference point for "OCR that runs locally on ordinary hardware": no accelerator, no service. | 20 years of HP development | [Tesseract DAS 2016 tutorial](https://tesseract-ocr.github.io/docs/das_tutorial2016/6ModernizationEfforts.pdf); [Tesseract history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract_(software)) | 1985-2006 | Medium |
| 1.2 | Tesseract placed in the top three engines for character accuracy in the 1995 UNLV OCR accuracy test. That quality, not novelty, is why it survived into the browser era as the default open OCR. | top 3 | [Tesseract history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract_(software)) | 1995 | Low |
| 1.3 | **Tesseract 4.0** replaced the legacy classifier with an LSTM engine (2018), supporting 116 languages and 37 scripts; the legacy engine remained selectable. Reported gain over the legacy engine was about 30%. | 116 languages, ~30% gain | [Tesseract 4.0 notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract_(software)); [DAS 2016 tutorial](https://tesseract-ocr.github.io/docs/das_tutorial2016/6ModernizationEfforts.pdf) | 2018 | Medium |
| 1.4 | Tesseract's known limits are documented and persistent: poor results without preprocessing (x-height at least 20 px, deskewing, border removal), weaker page-layout analysis than deep-learning competitors, and no GPU support. This is the technology mismatch that made camera-style OCR a separate product category. | — | [Tesseract history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract_(software)) | 2020s | Low |
| 1.5 | In November 2020 Brewster Kahle said Tesseract had "made a major step forward" and now matched proprietary OCR, reversing earlier Internet Archive evaluations. | — | [Tesseract history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesseract_(software)) | 2020-11 | Low |
| 1.6 | **macOS Vision** has shipped on-device text recognition since macOS 10.15 Catalina (2019), exposed to apps as `VNRecognizeTextRequest`; it is the engine behind Live Text. | — | [Apple Vision documentation](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/vision) | 2019 | Low |
| 1.7 | **Windows.Media.Ocr** has provided on-device OCR to Windows apps since the Windows 10 era, with language packs installed locally; it is the same class of bounded, small-model, OS-level primitive. | — | [Windows.Media.Ocr](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/uwp/api/windows.media.ocr) | 2016 | Low |
| 1.8 | **Windows 11 Live Captions** shipped in the 2022 Update (22H2) on 2022-09-20, generating captions on-device for any audio and working offline after a speech-model download; English was the only language at launch. | English only at launch | [Windows Insider build 25300](https://blogs.windows.com/windows-insider/2023/02/15/announcing-windows-11-insider-preview-build-25300/); [ZDNET](https://www.zdnet.com/article/windows-11-live-captions-snap-layout-and-widget-updates/) | 2022-09-20 | Medium |
| 1.9 | Windows expanded Live Captions to Chinese, French, German, Italian, Japanese and Portuguese in February 2023; the current list adds Korean and regional English variants. Up to five caption languages can be installed, and processing stays local. | 5 installed languages maximum | [Windows Insider build 25300](https://blogs.windows.com/windows-insider/2023/02/15/announcing-windows-11-insider-preview-build-25300/) | 2023-02-15 | Medium |

**Implications:** Bounded perception tasks (OCR, captioning) are the on-device ML that actually shipped for a decade before LLMs, at model sizes measured in megabytes and with no account, no server and no consent problem. Izcal's ML service should treat these as the core, not the fallback: an OCR engine and a captioner that run on CPU, offline and deterministically are more defensible than a general assistant. Tesseract's history also warns that layout analysis, not the character classifier, sets perceived quality; Izcal must measure whole-page structure, not only glyph accuracy.

## 2. The server-side era: Chrome's image descriptions (2019)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Chrome shipped **"Get Image Descriptions from Google"** for screen-reader users: when an image has no alt text, Chrome sends the image to a Google server running several ML models (text detection, object recognition, sentence captioning) and reads the result prefixed "Appears to be..." or "Appears to say...". It was opt-in via Settings or the context menu and English-only at launch. | — | [Google Accessibility blog](https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/accessibility/get-image-descriptions/) | 2019-10-10 | High |
| 2.2 | Google reported **more than 10 million image descriptions** generated during testing, with "hundreds of thousands being added every day". This is the first published usage number for browser-integrated ML and it was achieved by uploading page images, not by local inference. | 10M+ descriptions; hundreds of thousands/day | [Google Accessibility blog](https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/accessibility/get-image-descriptions/) | 2019-10-11 | High |
| 2.3 | The feature's privacy model is explicit: images are "sent securely to a Google server", not saved, and pages are not modified. Google itself said machine descriptions are worse than author-written alt text and returns nothing when confidence is low. | — | [Google Accessibility blog](https://blog.google/company-news/outreach-and-initiatives/accessibility/get-image-descriptions/) | 2019-10-10 | High |
| 2.4 | Seven years later the feature is still documented and still server-side: Chrome Enterprise's `AccessibilityImageLabelsEnabled` policy (supported since Chrome 84, still listed for Chrome 155) exists to turn it on or off, and Google's support page still describes sending images for description. No on-device successor was found. | policy through Chrome 155 | [Chrome support](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/9311597); [policy reference](https://admx.offsec.nl/policy/google/google/google-chrome/enable-get-image-descriptions-from-google/) | 2026 (page) | Medium |
| 2.5 | The fleet-level cost of this design was never published by Google. The only related cost data in this strand is Amazon's (section 8), which shows what per-request cloud inference does to a consumer product's economics at scale. | — | see 8.3 | 2026 | Low |

**Implications:** The 2019 design was right for its constraints (no usable on-device vision model, rare opt-in use, accessibility urgency) and still produced a privacy bill: every described image leaves the machine. Izcal should keep the user-facing capability but invert the data flow: local captioning by default, no image upload, and a visible "this ran on your device" record. The 10-million figure also shows the demand is real and recurring, which makes a small local captioner a better long-term bet than a server feature.

**Implications:** Chrome's line is the clearest negative lesson in this strand. A model can be on-device and still be unwanted: the consent failure, the re-download against the user's deletion, the removed privacy sentence, the desktop-only gate and the cloud AI Mode beside a local model add up to a trust deficit that a privacy-branded browser cannot afford. Izcal should make every model an explicit, listed, user-owned artifact with size, licence, purpose and a delete that stays deleted; never download on first run; and separate "runs locally" from "sends data to the vendor" in the UI and in the privacy text. The 22 GB / 16 GB RAM gate also shows that a general model excludes most devices, while a 197M task model is the shape that reaches them.

**Implications:** Firefox is the strongest existence proof for Izcal's direction: a 182M model under 200 MB and a 20 MiB translation model both shipped to hundreds of millions of users, offline, with the model served and versioned by the browser vendor, and with a separate inference process. The drift is equally instructive: when the product became a general assistant, Mozilla chose hosted 235B-class models because a local small model could not match them, and "local" survived only as BYOM and as local storage of memories. Izcal should therefore scope its service to tasks where a small model is competitive, publish the model's footprint and licence per task, and never describe hosted inference as local.

## 14. Cross-cutting implications for Izcal

- **On-device is a scope decision, not a slogan.** The tasks that worked locally are bounded perception and transformation tasks with models under ~200 MB. The tasks that moved to the cloud are open-ended assistants with models in the tens to hundreds of billions. Izcal should ship the first class and refuse to pretend the second class is local.
- **Off by default means no download without a decision.** The Chrome episode is the template of how not to do it: a multi-gigabyte file, no prompt, re-download after deletion, and a privacy sentence later removed. Every Izcal model should be an explicit, listed artifact with size, licence, purpose and a delete that persists.
- **MIT-only weights narrows the field.** Gemini Nano, Apple's AFM and Gemma are not MIT; Brave's GLM 5.3 Flash is MIT but cloud-hosted. Izcal's viable path is small MIT-licensed or in-house-trained task models, which also matches the size class that worked.
- **Own the inference stack, but anchor it to portable primitives.** WebNN is a single-implementation Candidate Recommendation Draft after years; WebGPU is the portable compute layer; a deterministic CPU reference path is the only way to guarantee reproducibility across platforms.
- **Footprint is a feature.** Chrome needs 22 GB free and 16 GB RAM; Apple Intelligence costs 8-14 GB; Izcal should set a per-model budget (tens of megabytes for perception, low hundreds for captioning) and publish it, because that is what made Live Caption and Firefox translation invisible successes.
- **Determinism is the differentiator regulators and users can check.** A local model with a fixed version, fixed weights and a fixed seed can be tested; a cloud endpoint cannot. That is the technical basis for any accessibility or privacy claim Izcal makes.
