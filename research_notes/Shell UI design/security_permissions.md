# Shell UI Evidence: Security Indicators, Warnings, Permissions, Disclosure, Identity

Compiled 14 Oct 2026. Primary sources were preferred: peer-reviewed papers, vendor blogs with data, and standards or engine docs.
Confidence: **High** means a primary source was read and the number was checked in the text. **Medium** means a primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim with no data shown. **Low** means a secondary source or a figure I could not verify.

## 1. Address bar and security indicators

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Most users misread the padlock. They take it to mean the whole site is "safe/trustworthy" rather than "the connection is encrypted". | n=1,880. 89% held misconceptions: 11% correct, 59% over-confident (connection security plus more), 30% misinformed. 74% read it as "the website is secure" and 51% as "safe to enter data". Only 23–44% mentioned the padlock unprompted when judging trust. With no icon, 50% would continue an order vs 74% at baseline. With the tune icon, 53% vs 74% agreed the site was secure. | 1.2 | Chrome replaced the lock with the neutral "tune" icon in Chrome 117. Rationale: the lock implies trust, nearly all phishing sites use HTTPS, and users never learned the lock was clickable. In the experiment, users opened site controls *more* and showed no confusion. HTTP is still marked "Not secure". On iOS the lock was removed entirely. | Over 95% of Chrome/Windows page loads were HTTPS. Only 14% of Alexa top 1M sites supported HTTPS in 2013. Only 11% understood the lock (from 1.1). | Chromium Blog, "An Update on the Lock Icon". https://blog.chromium.org/2023/05/an-update-on-lock-icon.html | 2 May 2023 | High |
| 1.3 | Positive-indicator redesign: users understood the HTTPS indicator but many did not understand the HTTP one. The team tested 40 icons and 7 strings and arrived at the "Not secure" label and red triangle that Chrome shipped. | 1,329 surveyed in situ. Then "thousands" of respondents rated the candidates. | Felt et al., "Rethinking Connection Security Indicators", SOUPS 2016. https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2016/technical-sessions/presentation/porter-felt | Jun 2016 | High |
| 1.4 | "Not secure" applied to all HTTP pages in Chrome 68. This followed staged steps: password and credit-card fields (Chrome 56), then Incognito (Chrome 62). | At announcement, about 68% of Android/Windows and 78% of ChromeOS/Mac traffic was HTTPS. 81 of the top 100 sites defaulted to HTTPS. | Google post (Schechter) via press, e.g. https://blog.cloudflare.com/https-or-bust-chromes-plan-to-label-sites-as-not-secure/ | Feb/Jul 2018 | Medium |
| 1.5 | HTTPS-by-default: "Always Use Secure Connections" turns on by default for public sites in Chrome 154 (Oct 2026), after Chrome 147 for Enhanced Safe Browsing users. Chrome warns before the first visit to an HTTP public site and does not re-warn for frequently visited ones. Private networks are exempt. | Chrome 141 test: warnings on <3% of navigations. Median user saw <1 warning/week, and >95% saw <3/week. | Google Security Blog, "HTTPS by default". https://security.googleblog.com/2025/10 (also https://blog.google/security/https-by-defau/) | 28 Oct 2025 | Medium (rollout and percentages via SecurityWeek coverage) |
| 1.6 | EV indicator removal: in a large field experiment, removing the EV UI did not change most behavioural metrics. Chrome 77 moved EV into Page Info, and Firefox 70 followed. | Field experiment "orders of magnitude larger" than prior work. Per-origin analysis found only 1 origin significant after Bonferroni correction. | Thompson et al., "The Web's Identity Crisis", USENIX Security 2019. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/thompson. Chromium doc: https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/ev-to-page-info.md | Aug 2019 | High |
| 1.7 | Users cannot reliably parse URLs. | Hosts identified correctly in an average of 58% of URLs: 93% of real URLs but only 40% of obfuscated or lookalike URLs. | 1.8 | Chrome's "simplified domain" (registrable domain only, path hidden) field trial was abandoned because it "didn't move relevant security metrics". Full URLs returned, though scheme and "www" stay elided. **No metric values were published.** | none published | Chromium commit "Delete simplified domain experiment". https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/180e57971%5E%21. Coverage: https://portswigger.net/daily-swig/google-abandons-plans-to-simplify-urls-in-chrome-following-real-world-testing | Experiment Aug 2020 (M86), ended Jun 2021 | High (outcome) / n/a (numbers) |
| 1.9 | URL formatting alone helps little. Showing only the "who" part of the URL vs the full URL made no significant difference in phishing detection. | n=200. Detection 71% (unmodified) to 76% (domain only), not significant. | Mossano et al. (KIT), via https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000163039/151499531 | ~2023 | Medium |
| 1.10 | IDN homograph policy (Chromium): show punycode when a label mixes scripts in a confusable way (UTS #39), for whole-script confusables on TLDs not associated with that script (e.g., Cyrillic "аррӏе.com"), for digit lookalikes, and for skeleton matches against top or visited domains. Chromium's stated principle is to avoid punycoding too broadly because it harms non-Latin users. Lookalike domains also get a local, on-device "Did you mean…?" interstitial or a smaller safety tip, depending on confidence. | n/a (policy) | https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md ; https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/lookalikes/lookalike-domains.md | living docs | High (policy); no published effectiveness data |

**Implications:** Do not use a passive positive "secure" indicator. Use a neutral, clickable site-controls entry point and keep strong negative signalling for HTTP. Put precise connection and identity detail in secondary UI. Do not rely on URL styling such as eTLD+1 bolding to stop phishing. Measured gains are about zero, so put the effort into active defences: lookalike interstitials, Safe Browsing, and credential binding (section 5). eTLD+1 emphasis is cheap and harmless, so it is fine to keep, but do not hide the path, because the evidence for doing so is null.

## 2. Warnings (interstitials)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Field click-through rates (CTR) vary hugely with design. | 25M+ warning impressions. Malware and phishing CTR 9–23%. Firefox SSL CTR 33.0% vs Chrome SSL 70.2%. Technically advanced users clicked through more. | Akhawe & Felt, "Alice in Warningland", USENIX Security 2013. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity13/technical-sessions/presentation/akhawe | Aug 2013 | High |
| 2.2 | Appearance explains part of the Firefox–Chrome gap. Imagery (a watching-eyes figure, a "stop" person) and an extra click did not. | 130,754 impressions, 6 conditions. Default Chrome 67.9% vs mock-Firefox 56.1%. Appearance accounts for about 12 of the 37-point gap, and Firefox's extra dialog about 8 more. 98% of users who took the first proceed step also took the second. Removing the human figure had no effect (56.1% vs 55.9%). | Felt, Reeder, Almuhimedi, Consolvo, "Experimenting at Scale with Google Chrome's SSL Warning", CHI 2014. https://research.google/pubs/experimenting-at-scale-with-google-chromes-ssl-warning/ | Apr 2014 | High |
| 2.3 | **Opinionated design** is the strongest documented lever. Make the safe choice a prominent button ("Back to safety"), hide the proceed path behind "Advanced", and use red full-page styling. Comprehension stayed low for every text tested, including the new one. | Field adherence roughly +30 percentage points. After the Chrome 37 launch, SSL adherence rose from 37% to 62%. Extra hurdles add 2–15%, depending on complexity. | Felt et al., "Improving SSL Warnings: Comprehension and Adherence", CHI 2015. https://research.google/pubs/improving-ssl-warnings-comprehension-and-adherence/ | Apr 2015 | High |
| 2.4 | Habituation is real at the neural level: visual-processing response drops sharply after the second exposure. "Polymorphic" warnings that vary their appearance resist habituation. Results come from fMRI, mouse-tracking, and a 5-day longitudinal field study. | n=25 (fMRI). Effects are statistically significant but the papers report no field CTR. | Anderson et al., CHI 2015 (https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_323208); Vance et al., MISQ 2018 (https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html) | 2015–2018 | Medium |
| 2.5 | In the field, no single dominant failure (habituation included) explains non-adherence to modern warnings. Reasons are many small, context-specific misunderstandings, such as "I trust this site" or "it's my own server". | >6,000 Chrome and Firefox users surveyed in situ. | Reeder et al., "An Experience Sampling Study of User Reactions to Browser Warnings in the Field", CHI 2018. https://research.google/pubs/pub46632/ | Apr 2018 | High (abstract) |
| 2.6 | Frequency control: HTTPS-First warnings are tuned to be rare (<3% of navigations, median <1/week) and suppressed for frequently visited sites. This is an explicit anti-habituation budget. | see 1.5 | see 1.5 | Oct 2025 | Medium |

**Implications:** Use full-page, opinionated interstitials with a single prominent safe action and the bypass behind a disclosure. Keep warning volume very low (false-positive budget), and consider polymorphic variation for repeated warnings. Do not expect better text to fix comprehension. Context-specific copy (by error type) helps at the margin.

## 3. Permission prompts

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Notifications dominate prompt volume and have the worst grant rate. Chrome's quiet UI (an address-bar chip instead of a bubble) is auto-enabled for users who habitually deny and for sites with low accept rates. A crowd-sourced blocklist covers abusive sites. | Notifications are 74% of all Chrome permission prompts. Grant rate 12% on desktop and 23% on Android. A/B test with 40M+ users, 100M+ prompts, 70k+ sites: up to 30% fewer unnecessary actions with <5% change in grant rate. (The Google Research page states 10%/21%. Use the USENIX figures.) | Bilogrevic et al., "Shhh...be quiet!", USENIX Security 2021. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/bilogrevic ; Chromium Blog https://blog.chromium.org/2020/01/introducing-quieter-permission-ui-for.html | 2021 (UI shipped in Chrome 80, Feb 2020) | High |
| 3.2 | Firefox telemetry: prompts not tied to a user gesture are overwhelmingly rejected. Requiring a gesture raises first-time accept rates sharply. Repeat prompting rarely converts. Result: "Never" replaced "Not Now" in Firefox 70, and a gesture became required for notification prompts in Firefox 72. Without a gesture, Firefox shows only an address-bar icon. | About 99% of notification prompts unaccepted and 48% actively denied. One month of Firefox 63: 1.45B prompts, 23.66M accepted (about 1:60). About 85% of accepts came without a prior "Not Now". With a gesture: 52% first-time allow (Nightly), 24% (existing users, Release), 56% (new users). | Mozilla Future Releases blog, "Restricting Notification Permission Prompts in Firefox". https://blog.mozilla.org/futurereleases/2019/11/04/restricting-notification-permission-prompts-in-firefox/ | 4 Nov 2019 | High |
| 3.3 | Safari has required a user gesture for push permission prompts since 12.1 on macOS (later extended to iOS web push). | n/a | Safari 12.1 release notes. https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/safari-release-notes/safari-12_1-release-notes.md | Mar 2019 | High |
| 3.4 | One-time grants: Chrome 116 added "Allow this time" for geolocation, camera, and mic. It expires on tab close, after 16 h, or after 5 min in the background (except capture). Safari 16 makes geolocation one-time by default, with an opt-in 24 h checkbox. Firefox 115 makes geolocation, camera, and mic one-time by default, with a "remember" checkbox. Firefox grants "the smallest atomic unit of access". **No usage or uptake numbers published.** | none | https://developer.chrome.com/blog/one-time-permissions ; Mozilla rationale: https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-device-apis-log/2021Apr/0020.html | Aug 2023 | High (behaviour) |
| 3.5 | Auto-revocation: Safety Check revokes camera, location, and similar permissions on unused sites (2023–24). Since Oct 2025 it also revokes notification permission on low-engagement, high-volume sites (installed PWAs excluded). Users are told and can re-grant via Safety Check, and the feature can be turned off. Google reports a "significant reduction" in notifications with "minimal change" in total clicks, and that low-volume senders got *more* clicks. | <1% of notifications receive any interaction. No effect sizes published. | Chromium/Google blog, "Reducing notification overload". https://blog.google/chromium/automatic-notification-permission/ | 10 Oct 2025 | High (claim) / Low (no numbers) |
| 3.6 | Layered notification-abuse defences (2026): auto-revoke, revocation after repeated "suspicious notification" warnings, server-side rate limits, and a reworked Android grant flow. | Over 7B fewer notifications/day on Android in Q1 2026. Disruptive domains capped at 1,000 msgs/min (HTTP 429). | Google Security blog, "The multi-layered defenses that harden Chrome against abusive notifications". https://blog.google/security/the-multi-layered-defenses-that-harden-chrome-against-abusive-notifications/ | 11 Aug 2026 | High |
| 3.7 | ML quieting: Chrome uses server-side and later on-device models (Web Permission Predictions; a Gemini Nano "PermissionsAI" experiment for notifications and geolocation) to route low-likelihood requests to the quiet UI. | About 5% average grant-rate change (NDSS 2024 slides, unverified). No published results for the Gemini variant. | https://www.ndss-symposium.org/wp-content/uploads/2024-108-slides.pdf ; https://windowsreport.com/google-chrome-to-use-ai-for-quieter-browser-permission-notifications/ | 2024–2025 | Low-Medium |
| 3.8 | Page-Embedded Permission Control: the generic `<permission>` element ran as an origin trial in Chrome 126–143. Vendor feedback (WebKit, Mozilla) said a generic element added complexity, so it was redesigned into capability-specific elements. `<geolocation>` shipped in Chrome 144, and `<usermedia>` is in its own origin trial. Benefits: a strong user-intent signal (avoids quiet blocks) and a recovery path from a "previously blocked" state. | Partner results only, no accept-rate or controlled effect sizes: **Zoom −46.9%** camera/mic capture errors, **Immobiliare.it +20%** successful geolocation flows, **ZapImóveis 54.4%** recovery from a previously blocked state. | Chrome for Developers, "Introducing the `<geolocation>` HTML element". https://developer.chrome.com/blog/geolocation-html-element ; origin trial: https://developer.chrome.com/blog/permission-element-origin-trial ; https://developer.chrome.com/blog/enhancements-to-permission-element | 13 Jan 2026 (OT from mid-2024) | High (numbers are self-reported by partners) |

**Implications:** Require a user gesture for any prompt. Default noisy permission types (notifications) to a quiet chip, and escalate to a bubble only on a strong intent signal (embedded control or click). Offer one-time grants by default for location, camera, and mic. Auto-revoke unused grants and *tell the user*. Never re-prompt after a deny. Support `<geolocation>`-style in-content controls, because they give a high-confidence intent signal and a recovery UI for previously blocked users.

## 4. Honest labelling and disclosure

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Private-mode splash screens fail to correct most misconceptions, and some *increase* them (about ads, ISP/employer/government tracking, and download lists). Users expected protection from geolocation, ads, viruses, and site or network tracking. Vague promises such as Opera's "your secrets are safe" and Firefox's "browse like no one's watching" were singled out as harmful. Wording mattered: concrete local-only statements did better. | n=460, 13 disclosures (12 real plus control), 20 scenarios. Basic local facts were well understood (e.g., 89.8–99.3% correct on history and cache items). | Wu et al., "Your Secrets Are Safe", WWW 2018. https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2018.wwwconf_conference-2018.21/ (slides: https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-privacy/2018JulSep/att-0028/privatebrowsing.pdf) | Apr 2018 | High (qualitative), Medium (numbers from slides) |
| 4.2 | Real-world private-browsing use is rare and practical. Some users overestimate protections, e.g., believing credit cards are safer or that social media is hidden from an employer. | 450+ SBO participants. Private browsing was about 4% of 167,128 sessions. | Habib et al., "Away From Prying Eyes", SOUPS 2018. https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2018/presentation/habib-prying | Aug 2018 | High |
| 4.3 | Brave's new-tab disclosures (Private and Private with Tor) did not improve comprehension. Help Center text did. Too many modes increased confusion. | n=283 | Fehlhaber et al., "When Brave Hurts Privacy", IEEE S&P 2020 poster. https://www.ieee-security.org/TC/SP2020/poster-abstracts/hotcrp_sp20posters-final19.pdf | May 2020 | Medium (poster) |
| 4.4 | Legal pressure on wording: after *Brown v. Google*, Chrome's Incognito text changed to "Others who use this device won't see your activity, so you can browse **more** privately" plus "This won't change how data is collected by websites you visit… including Google." | n/a | Coverage: https://www.forrester.com/blogs/google-chrome-settlement-shows-the-power-of-privacy-minded-consumers ; https://moneylife.in/article/google-updates-chromes-incognito-disclaimer-after-settling-us5bn-lawsuit/73159.html | Jan–Apr 2024 | Medium |
| 4.5 | Firefox tracking-protection shield: in a 2015 diary study only 1 of 12 noticed the shield unprompted, though users understood the toggle once shown. The "Content Blocking" label was misread (as adult content or pop-ups), so it was renamed "Enhanced Tracking Protection". The panel was redesigned to answer "is it on, is it working, how do I turn it off for this site". | n=12 (diary), plus qualitative testing | Mozilla UX blog. https://blog.mozilla.org/ux/2015/07/user-study-of-tracking-protection-in-firefox-nightly/ ; https://blog.mozilla.org/ux/?p=4429 | 2015; 2019 | Medium (small n) |
| 4.6 | **Gap:** no peer-reviewed comprehension study of Brave Shields or of the current ETP shield was found. | — | — | — | — |

**Implications:** Avoid sweeping promises ("private", "safe", "anonymous"). State *who* is and is not protected against, e.g., "People who use this device won't see…" / "Your ISP, employer, and the sites you visit can still see…". Prefer concrete, scoped verbs. Make the protection indicator clickable to a panel that answers on/working/off-for-this-site. Test icon recognition rather than assuming it.

## 5. Phishing-resistant identity UI

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Passkeys sign in faster and more reliably than passwords (Google data). | Success: 63.8% for local passkeys vs 13.8% for passwords (about 4x). Average time 14.9 s vs 30.4 s. Mar–Apr 2023 data, metric not precisely defined. | Google Security Blog, "Making authentication faster than ever". https://security.googleblog.com/2023/05/making-authentication-faster-than-ever.html | May 2023 | Medium-High |
| 5.2 | Cross-industry Passkey Index (Amazon, Google, Microsoft, PayPal, TikTok, and others). | 93% passkey sign-in success vs 63% for other methods. 8.5 s vs 31.2 s (−73%). 36% of accounts have a passkey enrolled, and 26% of sign-ins use one. −81% login-related help-desk incidents. Self-reported, not audited. | FIDO Alliance Passkey Index. https://fidoalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/FIDO-Passkey-Index-October-2025.pdf | Oct 2025 | Medium |
| 5.3 | Enterprise deployment barriers: account recovery, friction, technical issues, regulation, and security culture. | 28 interviews | Lassak et al., "Why Aren't We Using Passkeys?", USENIX Security 2024. https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/lassak | Aug 2024 | High |
| 5.4 | Passkey website UX is inconsistent. Many sites show problematic patterns that create usability and security issues. | 111 sites audited | Ramat et al., SOUPS 2025 poster. https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/ramat-poster | Aug 2025 | Low-Medium (poster) |
| 5.5 | Password-manager autofill is origin-bound, so it does not fill on a lookalike domain. This is the mechanism-level anti-phishing benefit. Its weaknesses: mobile autofill frameworks and WebViews can be used as confused deputies, and desktop managers have unsafe defaults (autofill without interaction, clickjacking). Users of built-in managers are convenience-driven. | 13 managers evaluated; 30 interviews | Oesch & Ruoti, USENIX Security 2020 (https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/oesch); Oesch et al., ACSAC 2021 (https://acsac.org/2021/program/final/s129.html); Pearman et al., SOUPS 2019 (https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2019/presentation/pearman) | 2019–2021 | High (mechanism) / **no field study found quantifying phishing prevented by autofill** |

**Implications:** Make passkeys and origin-bound autofill the primary identity UI. Require a user gesture to fill (no fill-on-load). When a saved credential *doesn't* offer to fill, show a clear "this isn't the site you saved a password for" cue. That turns autofill absence into a usable phishing signal, though the effect is unmeasured, so treat it as a hypothesis. Plan recovery UX for passkeys up front.

## 6. Download warnings

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | The download bubble (tray anchored by the toolbar) replaced the bottom shelf. Part of the rationale was a clearer separation of trusted browser UI from web content, plus room for richer warnings. Early-experiment feedback changed how often the tray auto-opens, and users can disable auto-open. | none | Chromium Blog, "Redesigning Chrome downloads". https://blog.chromium.org/2023/08/redesigning-chrome-downloads-to-keep.html | 3 Aug 2023 | High (qualitative) |
| 6.2 | Two-tier warning taxonomy differentiated by icon, colour, and text: **Suspicious** (grey triangle, lower confidence) and **Dangerous** (red, high confidence). Google reports "fewer warnings bypassed, warnings heeded more quickly". **No numbers published.** Deep scans (automatic for Enhanced Protection users) flag malware at more than 50x the base rate. Encrypted archives prompt for the password: Enhanced Protection sends it for scanning, while Standard Protection checks only metadata locally. | >50x flag rate for deep-scanned files | Google Security Blog, "Building security into the redesigned Chrome downloads experience". https://security.googleblog.com/2024/07/building-security-into-redesigned.html | 24 Jul 2024 | High (claims) / no effect sizes |

**Implications:** Use graded warnings with distinct visuals per confidence level. Anchor download UI to trusted chrome, not the content area. Make "delete" the primary action on dangerous files. Offer deep scan when protection mode allows it.

## 7. Containers, identity, and profiles

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | **No published comprehension study of Firefox Multi-Account Containers' colour coding was found.** Mozilla's Test Pilot (2017) set out to learn whether "the security model is intelligible" and tested privacy-framed vs security-framed onboarding (Shield study), but no results were published. Colours were chosen for light/dark-theme visibility. User feedback asks for stronger tab-edge colour bars. | none | https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1373676 ; https://hacks.mozilla.org/2017/10/containers-for-add-on-developers/ ; https://connect.mozilla.org/t5/ideas/colors-for-containers/m-p/4134 | 2017 | Low (absence of evidence) |
| 7.2 | Chrome profile picker shown at launch (2020–21) to avoid opening the wrong person's profile. Informed by a 2-month diary study plus interviews of personal and work users. Later experimentation added "Open all profiles" for setups with fewer than 5 profiles. **No outcome metrics published.** | n/a | https://safety.google/stories/chrome-profile/ ; https://wwwhatsnew.com/2021/03/02/chrome-lanza-su-nueva-experiencia-de-seleccion-de-perfiles/ | 2021 | Low-Medium |
| 7.3 | Indirect evidence: colour and icon cues alone are weak identity signals (see 1.6, 1.7, 4.5, and the 2.4 habituation results). Text labels improve icon comprehension. | — | — | — | Inference |

**Implications:** Treat container and profile identity as safety-relevant chrome. Use a colour *plus a text label* in the toolbar or address bar, carry it into the tab, and repeat it in permission and credential prompts (e.g., "Work container – save password?"). Run your own comprehension tests, because there is no published baseline.

## Key gaps (not published as of Oct 2026)
- Effect sizes for Chrome's simplified-domain trial, download-bubble warnings, one-time permission uptake, notification auto-revoke, and PermissionsAI.
- Controlled accept/deny effect sizes for `<permission>` / `<geolocation>` (only partner anecdotes exist).
- Comprehension studies of Brave Shields, the current Firefox ETP shield, and container colour coding.
- Field measurement of phishing prevented by origin-bound autofill.
