# Izcal resource footprint: history and postmortems of browser resource use

Compiled 2026-10-19. Primary sources were preferred (papers, Chromium/Mozilla/WebKit/Microsoft blogs and design docs, release notes).
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only.

**Method note.** This strand follows phase 2 of `.claude/skills/first-principles-spec/SKILL.md`. The web-search budget is shared, so the most design-relevant questions came first (process model cost, site isolation, lifecycle interventions, measurement). After that, known primary pages were fetched directly. Several engine source files were read while compiling this note. Under HGBA-2 M-1 part 3 (2.3.1) engine source is never evidence, so the rows that rested on them were re-sourced, turned into black-box measurements or removed in the 0.3.1 pass ([source_evidence_pass.md](source_evidence_pass.md)). Vendor savings claims ("up to N%") are graded Medium unless the method is published. Items without a primary source are listed under **Gaps**.

## 1. The single-process era and its leaks (1996–2014)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Why single-process was right then.** Chrome's own launch post says that when most browsers were designed, "web pages were simple and had little or no active code in them," so "it made sense for the browser to render all the pages you visited in the same process, to keep resource usage low." | — | [Reis, "Multi-process Architecture," Chromium Blog](https://blog.chromium.org/2008/09/multi-process-architecture.html) | 2008-09-11 | High |
| 1.2 | **Opera Presto** ran on very small devices. The Nintendo DS Browser (Opera 8.5 port) shipped with a Slot-2 **Memory Expansion Pak adding 8 MB, for 12 MB total**; it was the only licensed DS software to use it. Opera Mini went further by moving layout to a **server-side Presto** and sending pre-rendered, compressed pages (claimed ~90% compression) to Java ME phones. The constraint was the device, and the answer was to move work off it. | 4 MB + 8 MB RAM; ~90% compression (claim) | [Wikipedia: Nintendo DS Browser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_DS_Browser); [Wikipedia: Opera Mini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera_Mini) | 2006; 2005– | Low |
| 1.3 | **Presto's end was not about footprint.** Opera's stated reason: "It makes more sense to have our experts working with the open source communities to further improve WebKit and Chromium, rather than developing our own rendering engine further," with the shift freeing "more of our resources" for features. Engine cost and web compatibility, not memory, ended the small engine. | 300 M users at the time | [Opera press release, "Opera gears up at 300 million users"](https://press.opera.com/2013/02/13/opera-gears-up-at-300-million-users/) | 2013-02-13 | High |
| 1.4 | **Firefox 4 regressed and MemShrink started.** Nethercote: Firefox 4 regressed "partly due to a large number of new features (not all of which were maximally efficient in their first iteration), and partly due to some over-aggressive tuning of heuristics relating to JavaScript garbage collection and image decoding." MemShrink's rules: reduce memory, but "changes that reduce memory consumption but make Firefox slower are not desirable"; the stated drivers were OOM crashes on **32-bit Windows (2–4 GB virtual address space)** and on swapless mobile devices. | — | [Nethercote, "Firefox 7 is lean and fast"](https://blog.mozilla.org/nnethercote/2011/08/09/firefox-7-is-lean-and-fast-2/); [MozillaWiki: Performance/MemShrink](https://wiki.mozilla.org/Performance/MemShrink) | 2011-08-09 | High |
| 1.5 | **Firefox 7 result.** "Often 20% to 30% less, and sometimes as much as 50% less" than Firefox 6. The big win was memory *given back*, not peak. | −20–30% typical; −52% post-close | [Firefox 7 is lean and fast](https://blog.mozilla.org/nnethercote/2011/08/09/firefox-7-is-lean-and-fast-2/) | 2011-08-09 | High |
| 1.6 | **Leaks were found only once measurement existed.** Splitting the JS heap into compartments (Firefox 4, March 2011) and per-compartment reporting in `about:memory` (Firefox 7, September 2011) made "zombie compartments" visible; "it only took a few months to realize the magnitude of the problems." Leaks were found in Adblock Plus, Video DownloadHelper, GreaseMonkey, Firebug, McAfee SiteAdvisor and Yahoo! Toolbar; zombie compartments were "90% or more of the add-on leaks." | 90%+ of add-on leaks | [Nethercote, "Firefox 15 plugs the add-on leaks"](https://blog.mozilla.org/nnethercote/2012/07/19/firefox-15-plugs-the-add-on-leaks/) | 2012-07-19 | High |
| 1.7 | **The fix was structural, not per-add-on.** Kyle Huey's patch detected references from chrome (add-on) code into a closed page's DOM and **cut them**. Telemetry's mean ghost-window count fell "from typically between three and four to typically less than one"; the worst 5% went from 15–20 ghost windows to about two. "Leaks in add-ons are not Mozilla's fault, but they are Mozilla's problem." | ghost windows 3–4 → <1 | [Firefox 15 plugs the add-on leaks](https://blog.mozilla.org/nnethercote/2012/07/19/firefox-15-plugs-the-add-on-leaks/) | 2012-07-19 | High |
| 1.8 | **MemShrink wound down by success and priorities, then was needed again.** Rahm (2017): "we did a pretty good job of getting memory under control and with limited resources there were more important tasks." Mozilla also shipped Firefox OS on a **128 MB phone (Tarako)**. With multiple content processes, "we're going to see the need to ramp up MemShrink again"; the JS engine was "probably our biggest target." | 128 MB device | [Rahm, "MemShrink's 6th Birthday"](https://erahm.org/2017/05/29/memshrinks-6th-birthday/) | 2017-05-29 | High |

**Implications:** In the single-process era the largest wins came from **returning memory after a page is gone** (fragmentation, leaks, zombie compartments) and from **measurement that attributes memory to a page**. Izcal should treat "memory returned after close/navigation" as a first-class budget, and make extension-held references to dead documents structurally impossible or automatically severed.

## 2. Chrome's multi-process model and process limits (2008–2018)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Chrome 2008: one browser process, sandboxed renderers, one process per plug-in type. Default **process-per-site-instance**: related same-site tabs share a renderer. Renderer count was capped: "we place a limit on the number of renderer processes that we create (20 in most cases). Once we hit this limit, we'll start re-using the existing renderer processes," so "the same renderer process could be used for more than one web site." Cross-site iframes stayed in-process. | limit 20 | [Multi-process Architecture](https://blog.chromium.org/2008/09/multi-process-architecture.html) | 2008-09-11 | High |
| 2.2 | **Historical modes and why they lost.** Process-per-site (all instances of a site in one process) "generally led to poor usability when a single process was used for too many tabs"; it survives only for the New Tab Page and extensions. Process-per-tab did not swap on cross-site navigation. Single-process "is generally not a safe or robust process model"; kept for old low-resource Android WebView and testing. | — | [Chromium docs: Process Model and Site Isolation](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/process_model_and_site_isolation.md) | read 2026-10-19 | High |
| 2.3 | **The desktop process limit is a function of memory.** Chromium sets a "soft" process limit "based on the memory available on a given client"; over it, Chrome randomly reuses *same-site* processes; it can never merge sites. (The formula, its per-process constant and worked values, read from engine source, were removed (0.3.1 source-evidence pass, HGBA-2 M-1 part 3); Chrome's limit per lab device is observed black-box at FP-M0, footprint open question 17.) | memory-based | [process model doc, "Soft Process Limit"](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/process_model_and_site_isolation.md#process-reuse) | read 2026-10-19 | High |
| 2.4 | **Android has no soft limit.** "Chromium on Android does not set this soft process limit, and instead relies on the OS to discard processes." (A source comment on an earlier Android limit and a feature-flagged limit were removed (0.3.1 source-evidence pass, HGBA-2 M-1 part 3).) | — | [process model doc, "Soft Process Limit"](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/process_model_and_site_isolation.md#process-reuse) | read 2026-10-19 | High |
| 2.5 | **Measuring multi-process memory honestly was itself a design problem.** Chromium's backgrounder: summing working sets double-counts shared DLLs ("10-50MB larger than it really is"); Chromium reports "Private Working Set + Shareable Working Set − Shared Working Set," and applied the same metric to IE8's multi-process mode. | 10–50 MB double-count | [Chromium: Memory Usage Backgrounder](https://www.chromium.org/developers/memory-usage-backgrounder/) | undated (c. 2012) | High |

**Implications:** A process limit is a memory policy disguised as a count. A memory-based limit implies a per-process budget that the whole policy rests on, stated or not. Izcal should make that budget explicit and measured, and should keep the rule "soft limits merge only same-site" (I-1).

## 3. Site Isolation (2018) and its measured cost

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Chrome 67 enabled Site Isolation for 99% of desktop users (1% holdback) after Spectre: "about a 10-13% total memory overhead in real workloads due to the larger number of processes." | 10–13% | [Google Security Blog, "Mitigating Spectre with Site Isolation in Chrome"](https://security.googleblog.com/2018/07/mitigating-spectre-with-site-isolation.html) | 2018-07-11 | High |
| 3.2 | **The paper's field data.** Unique sites open: 6.0 at p50, 41.9 at p99. Renderer processes rose **4.4 → 6.2 (+43.5%) at p50** and **35.0 → 52.7 (+50.6%) at p99**. Private memory *per renderer* fell 51.5% (87.2 → 42.3 MB) at p50 and 28.6% (714.2 → 509.7 MB) at p99. **Total** memory rose 12.6% at p25 and 8.6% at p99; summary "9-13%." | +50% processes → +9–13% memory | [Reis, Moshchuk, Oskov, "Site Isolation: Process Separation for Web Sites within the Browser," USENIX Security 2019](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |
| 3.3 | **Why the cost was low:** "Site Isolation is effectively dividing an existing workload across more processes, so each renderer process is correspondingly smaller and shorter lived." The cost is the **fixed per-process overhead**, not the workload. | — | [sec19-reis.pdf §5](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |
| 3.4 | **Lab worst cases were far higher.** Single-page microbenchmarks: up to **89% overhead (wowprogress.com, 10 processes)**; seatguru.com 31% vs amazon.com 5% with the same five processes; a single-site page still paid **4% for the spare process**. Heavier pages amortize the fixed cost. | 4–89% | [sec19-reis.pdf §5.2](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |
| 3.5 | **The optimizations that made it shippable:** (a) consolidate same-site **iframes** into existing same-site processes always; (b) consolidate same-site **main frames** only above the soft limit, because "using a single process for all of them leads to bloated processes that perform poorly"; (c) keep data: URLs, sandboxed same-site iframes and cross-site *images* in-process; isolate by **site, not origin**; (d) a **spare warmed renderer**, skipped on low-memory devices, under pressure, or over the soft limit. "Site Isolation cannot support a hard process limit." | — | [sec19-reis.pdf §4.1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |
| 3.6 | **Other costs:** CPU in the browser process +8.2% at p99 (IPC and coordination); per-renderer CPU fell 33.5%. Page-load latency +≤2.25%. The rollout cost about **450,000 changed lines in 9,000 files**. | — | [sec19-reis.pdf §4.3, §5](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |
| 3.7 | "The Chrome team was willing to accept 9-13% memory overhead for the security benefits." The trade-off was explicit and data-backed, not assumed. | — | [sec19-reis.pdf §5.1](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec19-reis.pdf) | 2019-08 | High |

**Implications:** The measured lesson is that isolation's cost is **(number of processes) × (fixed per-process overhead)**, not the workload. That makes per-process fixed overhead the single most leveraged number in Izcal's footprint design. A small, mostly-shared process image makes I-1 almost free; a 40 MB one makes it expensive.

**Implications:** Every vendor weakened isolation on small devices because per-process overhead made full isolation unaffordable there. Izcal should also give the OS an explicit kill ranking (visibility, viewport, user state) rather than let it guess.

## 5. Firefox: Electrolysis (e10s) to Fission

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | **e10s measured cost before shipping.** Rahm's test (30 tabs, 0–8 content processes): "the more content processes we use, the more memory we use," but not 1:1; 8 processes roughly doubled TabsOpenSettled memory. | 5.8 | **Unloading under pressure (Firefox 93).** Earlier tab-unloading attempts failed to balance memory against reload annoyance, so Mozilla narrowed it to "if the browser is about to crash": Windows low-memory notification (~6% physical free), then **commit-space** checks; unload in **LRU** order; `about:unloads` shows the order. | 5.9 | Cross-browser snapshot (ATSY, 30 pages, 2017): Chrome 62 used ~1.4× Firefox 57 on Windows 64-bit and ~2× on Linux; Edge highest on Windows; Safari TP 40 "by far the worst" on macOS. **Implications:** Mozilla shows that per-process overhead is reducible by deliberate, budgeted work (34 → 19.5 MB in ~5 quarters), and that the JS engine's per-process baseline is the largest single item. It also shows two process-model alternatives Izcal must choose between: one process per site (Firefox) versus per site-instance with a soft limit (Chrome). Firefox 93 adds a third lesson: **reclaim must not need memory** (no GC pass at the edge of OOM). Izcal's emergency reclaim path must be allocation-free.

## 6. Safari / WebKit: memory pressure, suspension and energy

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | **WebKit's in-process policy ladder.** Each web process polls its memory use (every **30 s** by default) and enters a conservative policy that releases non-critical memory, then a strict policy, at fractions of a memory limit (**0.33 / 0.5** by default); an optional kill threshold sits above them (0, the default, never kills). (Platform fractions and log strings read from engine source were removed (0.3.1 source-evidence pass, HGBA-2 M-1 part 3).) | 30 s poll; 0.33 / 0.5 | [WebKitGTK MemoryPressureSettings](https://webkitgtk.org/reference/webkit2gtk/stable/struct.MemoryPressureSettings.html) | read 2026-10-19 | High (WebKitGTK port documentation) |
| 6.2 | *Kill thresholds.* WebKitGTK documents the kill threshold as a configurable fraction of the memory limit, above the strict threshold. Kill thresholds are runaway-page backstops, not budgets. (Per-tab threshold values read from engine source were removed (0.3.1 source-evidence pass, HGBA-2 M-1 part 3).) | — | [WebKitGTK kill threshold](https://webkitgtk.org/reference/webkit2gtk/stable/method.MemoryPressureSettings.set_kill_threshold.html) | read 2026-10-19 | Medium |
| 6.3 | **Background = zero CPU.** WebKit: inactive pages get CSS/SVG animations suspended and timers throttled; "on iOS, tabs are completely suspended when possible"; "on macOS, tabs participate in App Nap." Guidance: "drive idle power usage towards zero" and coalesce timers, because "lots of uncoordinated timers which trigger frequent CPU wake-ups are much worse." | — | [Poulain & Fraser, "How Web Content Can Affect Power Usage," WebKit blog](https://webkit.org/blog/8970/how-web-content-can-affect-power-usage/) | 2019-08-27 | High |
| 6.4 | **App Nap (OS X 10.9, 2013)** applies priority reduction, timer throttling and I/O throttling to apps not doing user-visible work. Apple notes these "do not necessarily save energy" by themselves; apps must reach idle. Timer **tolerance** lets the OS coalesce wakeups. | — | [Apple Energy Efficiency Guide for Mac Apps: App Nap](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Performance/Conceptual/power_efficiency_guidelines_osx/AppNap.html); [Timers](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/Performance/Conceptual/power_efficiency_guidelines_osx/Timers.html) | 2013– | High |
| 6.5 | Safari's process count has been lower than Chrome's (Rahm in 2017 saw Safari TP load all test pages into one content process). WebKit's own site-isolation work was not researched here. | — | [process model doc](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/process_model_and_site_isolation.md#no-site-isolation); [Rahm 2017](https://erahm.org/2017/09/25/firefox-memory-usage-in-the-quantum-era/) | 2017; read 2026-10-19 | Medium |

**Implications:** WebKit's design is **graded self-shrinking inside each process before any kill**, plus OS suspension of whole processes. Izcal can do better than a 30 s poll with a broker-driven push of pressure levels (HGBA-2 §4.6), and should keep the "shrink, then die" order. On iOS-like platforms, full process suspension is the energy floor that throttling only approximates.

| 7.5 | **Chrome 108 Memory Saver and Energy Saver:** "up to 40% and 10GB less memory." The developer guidance: a hidden page may be discarded with no further events, so save state in `visibilitychange`. | "up to 40%, 10 GB" | [Google, "New Chrome features to save battery"](https://blog.google/products/chrome/new-chrome-features-to-save-battery-and-make-browsing-smoother/); [Walton, "Memory and Energy Saver modes"](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/memory-and-energy-saver-mode) | 2022-12-08 | Medium (no method) |
| 7.6 | Memory Saver later gained Moderate/Balanced/Maximum modes (2024), which change discard inactivity windows; a third-party 32-tab test reported −21% (Balanced) and −38% (Maximum) after 20 idle minutes and **no discards below ~3 GB** used. | −21% / −38% | [OMG Ubuntu](https://www.omgubuntu.co.uk/2024/10/google-chrome-now-offers-more-control-over-memory-usage); [Supercharge review](https://www.superchargebrowser.com/library/chrome-native-memory-saver-review/) | 2024-10; 2026 | Low |
| 7.7 | **Edge sleeping tabs (Edge 88 beta):** across ~40,000 devices, average 32% lower memory (1,675 → 1,146 MB) and 37% less CPU per sleeping tab; option to sleep after 5 minutes; heuristics skip sites that would break. Earlier internal figures: 26% / 29%. | −32% memory; −37% CPU | [Edge blog, "Sleeping Tabs: Delivering better browser performance"](https://blogs.windows.com/msedgedev/2020/12/09/sleeping-tabs-beta-performance/) | 2020-12-09 | Medium |
| 7.8 | **Edge 100:** "each sleeping tab saves 85% of memory and 99% CPU" on average; pages sharing a browsing instance may now sleep (+8% tabs sleeping). **Edge 105:** "sleep before discarding": sleeping "saves 83% of its memory on average," and unlike discards, "sleeping tabs resume without reloading"; high-resource tabs sleep automatically near the memory limit; "In September 2022, we slept 1.38 billion tabs." | 83–85% memory; 99% CPU; 1.38 B tabs/month | [Edge 100 post](https://blogs.windows.com/msedgedev/2022/04/07/sleeping-tabs-edge-100-improvements/) (snippet); [Edge 105 post](https://blogs.windows.com/msedgedev/2022/12/06/sleeping-tabs-edge-105-sleep-before-discarding/) | 2022-04-07; 2022-12-06 | Medium |
| 7.9 | Vivaldi, Opera and Brave expose tab hibernation/snoozing built on Chromium's discard machinery; no primary measurements were found. | — | not found | — | Low |

**Implications:** The interventions form a ladder (throttle → freeze → sleep/purge → discard), and each rung was added because the previous one was not enough. The vendors' numbers disagree by 2–3× depending on what is divided by what (per tab vs whole browser, average vs median), so Izcal's spec must define the denominator. The deeper lesson: these are **retrofits on engines whose pages run by default**. Timers alone were >40% of background work. Izcal can make "hidden ⇒ no wakeups unless the page holds a declared, user-visible reason" the default, rather than a heuristic added a decade later.

## 8. Allocator and engine-level memory work

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | **PartitionAlloc-Everywhere (Chrome 89):** intercepting malloc on Android and 64-bit Windows: "up to 22% in the browser process, 8% in the renderer, and 3% in the GPU," and up to 9% better responsiveness. Also: discarding foreground-tab memory not in use (e.g., off-screen big images) reclaims "up to 100MiB per tab, which is more than 20% on some popular sites"; macOS background-tab shrinking "up to 8%... more than 1GiB in some cases"; background throttling up to 65% better Apple Energy Impact. | 22/8/3%; 100 MiB/tab | [Chromium Blog M89](https://blog.chromium.org/2021/03/advanced-memory-management-and-more.html) | 2021-03-11 | Medium (vendor "up to") |
| 8.2 | **Windows Segment Heap episode.** Edge on Windows 10 2004 reported up to 27% lower memory using the segment heap. Chrome adopted it, then backed out after Intel and Bruce Dawson measured CPU regressions on Speedometer 2.0, WebXPRT3 and JetStream2. A general-purpose OS heap traded memory for CPU. | up to −27% (Edge) | [Thurrott](https://www.thurrott.com/cloud/web-browsers/microsoft-edge/236593/edge-memory-usage-improvements-are-coming-to-chrome-too); [Windows Central](https://windowscentral.com/google-roadblocks-ram-reducing-feature-chrome-windows-10) | 2020 | Low |

**Implications:** Allocator choice alone moved browser-process memory by up to 22%, and laziness (allocate metadata only when code proves hot) beat a separate "lite" mode. Both belong in the per-component budgets this spec sets. The segment-heap reversal shows that a memory win must be gated on a CPU benchmark, as CS-PF already intends.

## 11. Electron and app runtimes

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11.1 | **Slack desktop:** each signed-in workspace "was in fact running a stand-alone copy of the web client inside a separate Electron process, which meant that Slack used more memory than users expected." Fixing it "meant changing a fundamental assumption" (one workspace per process) and a rewrite to React + Redux with a lazily loaded, incomplete data model; one Redux store per workspace replaced one process per workspace. Reported result: **−50% memory, +33% load time**. | −50% (claim) | [Slack Engineering, "When a rewrite isn't: rebuilding Slack on the desktop"](https://slack.engineering/rebuilding-slack-on-the-desktop/) | 2019-07-22 | High (cause) / Medium (numbers) |
| 11.2 | **Microsoft Teams 2.0** moved from Electron + AngularJS (chosen in 2015 for rapid cross-platform delivery) to **WebView2 + React**, data processing in a worker, a partial data model, push instead of polling, and a native video compositor. **Implications:** Electron's footprint problem had two parts: **a private runtime copy per app** (code pages and disk not shared) and **apps that multiplied processes and loaded eagerly**. ## 13. Lessons learned (history strand)

| Project | What happened | Lesson | How Izcal applies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firefox 4–7, MemShrink | Regression from features and GC/image heuristics; the fix was mostly fragmentation and post-close reclaim | Peak is not the metric; memory returned after close is | Budget "resident after close/navigation" per test; CI gate |
| Firefox 15 zombie compartments | Leaks found only after per-compartment reporting; fixed by cutting dead references centrally | Measurement precedes reduction; fix structurally | Attribution from day one; extension references to dead documents severed by design |
| Chrome 2008 limit (20) | Limit forced cross-site sharing | A hard process limit conflicts with isolation | Never merge sites; soft limit merges same-site only (I-1) |
| Chrome Site Isolation | +50% processes, +9–13% memory; up to 89% on iframe-heavy pages | Cost = processes × fixed overhead | Make per-process fixed overhead the headline budget |
| Android ≥2 GB / login-only isolation | Isolation weakened where overhead didn't fit | Fixed overhead decides whether security is affordable | Target an overhead low enough to keep I-1 on 4 GB devices and phones; measure |
| Fission MemShrink | Content process 34 → 19.5 MB in ~5 quarters | Per-process overhead yields to budgeted, tagged work | Per-process budgets with `[overhead:N]`-style accounting |
| Firefox 93 unloading | Fewer crashes; OOMs rose, possibly because unload needs a GC | Reclaim must not need memory | Allocation-free emergency path; act before the edge |
| WebKit pressure ladder | Shrink in-process, kill only if shrinking fails | Graded response, kill last | Broker-pushed pressure levels with defined per-level actions |
| Chrome 57/87/88 throttling | Timers >40% of background work; up to 5× CPU cut | Wakeups are the energy cost | Hidden pages: zero wakeups by default unless a declared, user-visible reason |
| Edge sleeping tabs | 32% vs 83–85% savings, depending on definition | Numbers without a denominator mislead | Define per-tab vs whole-browser, mean vs median, in every target |
| PartitionAlloc / Segment Heap | Allocator moved memory 22%; OS heap cost CPU | Allocator is a top-level design decision | Budget allocator overhead; gate memory wins on CPU |
| V8 Lite → lazy V8 | Lazy allocation got most savings with no slowdown | Laziness beats a separate lite mode | Feed to JS engine spec: lazy metadata as default |
| Slack, Teams | Process per workspace; private runtime copies | Share runtime pages; lazy partial data | Shared read-only images across processes and embedders |
| Brave | Blocking cut 33–66% | Third-party content dominates | Treat blocking as a footprint control, measured in the corpus |
