## 1. QUIC/HTTP/3 vs TCP/TLS/HTTP/2: measured performance

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Google QUIC at scale.** QUIC reduced Google Search latency and YouTube rebuffers versus TLS/TCP in a holdback experiment. Gains grow with RTT and at high percentiles. **0-RTT** carried most of the search gain: ~88% of desktop QUIC connections were 0-RTT; the mobile app reached only 65–68%, so mobile gains were smaller. | ~7% of Internet traffic was QUIC. Search latency −8.0% desktop, −3.6% mobile; YouTube rebuffer rate −18.0% desktop, −15.3% mobile | [Langley et al., The QUIC Transport Protocol, SIGCOMM 2017](https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/46403.pdf) (text extracted from the PDF) | 2017-08 | High |
| 1.2 | **Where QUIC lost (2017).** Pre-warmed connections get no 0-RTT benefit. On >100 Mbps and/or few-ms RTT links QUIC "may perform worse than TCP", attributed to **client CPU limits and client-side scheduler inefficiencies**. Mobile gains were smaller partly because phones are CPU-bound when bandwidth is plentiful. | — | Same paper, §6.8 | 2017-08 | High |
| 1.3 | **"QUIC is not Quick Enough over Fast Internet."** On a 1 Gbps testbed, Chrome's HTTP/3 reached ~478 Mbps vs ~900+ for HTTP/2 (**−45.2%**); on a 1.6 GHz CPU HTTP/3 fell to ~200 Mbps. The gap appears above roughly **500–600 Mbps** (the paper is internally inconsistent on which threshold applies to which client). Same in Edge, Firefox, Opera; desktop and Pixel 5 on 5G. **Root cause is the receiver**: 744K UDP packets vs 53–58K TCP (GRO/LRO coalesce TCP but did not coalesce QUIC's UDP in Chrome), 231K vs 15K `netif_receive_skb` calls, 17K vs 4K syscalls, and **user-space ACK generation** (2.97 s of Chromium's 8.5 s network-thread time; TCP's ACKs cost the app nothing). Web pages: +3.0% mean page load time for QUIC over 100 sites (max +74.9%), but only 16 used H3. Video bitrate up to −9.8%. Mitigations proposed: UDP GRO, recvmmsg, delayed/less frequent ACKs. | 1 GB download desktop: 9.32 s (H2) vs 18.60 s (H3); CPU 77.5% vs 96.9%. Chrome network thread: decrypt 0.66 s, frame parse 3.47 s, ACK/response generation 2.97 s | [Zhang et al., WWW 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09423v2) (HTML full text) | 2024-05 (v2 2024-09-30) | High |
| 1.4 | **Cloudflare H3 vs H2 (2020).** TTFB 176 vs 201 ms (−12.4%); 15 KB page 443 vs 458 ms; 1 MB page 2.33 vs 2.30 s; real blog traffic H3 trailed H2 by 1–4%. Cloudflare blamed the **different congestion controllers** (H3 CUBIC, H2 BBRv1), not the protocol. | Synthetic tests, method thin | [Cloudflare: Comparing HTTP/3 vs HTTP/2](https://blog.cloudflare.com/http-3-vs-http-2/) | 2020-04-14 | Medium |
| 1.5 | **Meta (2020).** >75% of Facebook traffic on QUIC. GraphQL: request errors −6%, tail latency −20%, header size −5%. Video: MTBR up to +22%, video errors −8%, stall rate −20%. BBR is their primary CC. | Mobile apps, not a browser | [Meta: How Facebook is bringing QUIC to billions](https://engineering.fb.com/2020/10/21/networking-traffic/how-facebook-is-bringing-quic-to-billions/) | 2020-10-21 | Medium |
| 1.6 | **H3 share, Cloudflare 2025.** Requests: HTTP/2 50%, HTTP/1.x 29%, **HTTP/3 21%**, nearly unchanged from 2024. 15 countries sent >1/3 of requests over H3; 7 were <10% because of HTTP/1.x bot traffic. Dual-stack requests over IPv6: 29%. Post-quantum share of human traffic 29% → 52% during 2025. | All Cloudflare traffic incl. bots | [Cloudflare Radar 2025 Year in Review](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review) | 2025-12-15 | High |
| 1.7 | **H3 share, HTTP Archive 2024.** Home-page documents over H3: 7% desktop, 9% mobile (first-load crawl, so undercounts; author estimates closer to 30%). ~85% of requests are H2+. 26–28% of sites announce H3 by Alt-Svc; ~12% of pages have an HTTPS DNS record, 9–10% with `h3`. **99% of observed H3 page loads were triggered by HTTPS DNS records**, not Alt-Svc. | 2024 crawl | [Web Almanac 2024: HTTP](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/http) | 2024-12-10 | High |
| 1.8 | **Firefox.** About 20% of Firefox's HTTP traffic uses HTTP/3. | Firefox telemetry, 2025 | [Inden: Fast UDP I/O in Firefox](https://max-inden.de/post/fast-udp-io-in-firefox/) | 2025-09-14 | High |
| 1.9 | No 2025 HTTP Archive HTTP chapter was found (404 on 2026-10-19). | — | almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/http | 2026-10 | — |

**Implications:** H3 wins on lossy, long-RTT and mobile paths through 0-RTT/1-RTT setup and no transport head-of-line blocking, and it loses on fast, short-RTT links when the **receiver** is CPU-bound. A browser is the receiver. Izcal's QUIC receive path (batch receive, GRO, cheap ACK generation, in-place decryption) therefore decides whether H3 helps or hurts on fibre and Wi-Fi 6/7, and Izcal must benchmark its own H3 against its own H2 at 1 Gbps on a low-end CPU, not only on lossy links.

## 2. CPU cost of user-space QUIC and OS I/O

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Fixes (hand-optimised ChaCha20, PACKET_RX_RING memory-mapped receive, cache-efficient data structures) brought it to ~**2×**. | 2.10 | **io_uring** was the target of many Linux kernel exploits submitted to Google's kCTF, and Google restricted it (ChromeOS, Android apps, production). | 2.11 | MsQuic supports **XDP** (off by default) and Windows datapaths; its platform doc lists TLS requirements only (Schannel TLS 1.3 needs Windows 11/Server 2022). **Implications:** Build the UDP datapath as Izcal's own per-OS layer with **capability probing at runtime and kill switches per feature** (GSO, GRO, USO, URO, mmsg, ECN), because offload support is driver-dependent and Firefox had to roll back Windows offloads. Linux GSO+GRO is the proven win; macOS needs a batching strategy without GSO; Windows offloads must start disabled behind telemetry-free self-checks (e.g. detect missing segment size and fall back). Kernel bypass is irrelevant for a client. Since crypto becomes the next bottleneck, AES-GCM/ChaCha via CPU instructions and **in-place decryption into a shared receive buffer** are design requirements, and io_uring should not be a dependency of the sandboxed network process (it is a large kernel attack surface).

| 7.1 | **Chrome idle timers.** QUIC idle connection timeout **30 s** (300 s under a field trial); idle-session migration window 30 s; "retransmittable-on-wire" PING after 200 ms when early migration is on. H2 sends a "preface PING" before writing if idle ≥10 s. | — | Chromium [quic_context.h](https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:net/quic/quic_context.h), [client_socket_pool.cc](https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:net/socket/client_socket_pool.cc), [spdy_session.h](https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:net/spdy/spdy_session.h) (source read) | 2026-10 | High |
| 7.2 | **Radio-aware PING batching.** Chrome's H2 session checks liveness "in an energy efficient manner": if the radio is in standby it defers the PING until the radio wakes. Only implemented on Android. | — | Chromium spdy_session.h (source read) | 2026-10 | High |
| 7.3 | **Firefox has no separate network process yet**: "Network is not yet isolated in its own process"; a socket process implementation is work in progress. | — | [Inden, Networking challenges, MIR³ 2025](https://www.ce.cit.tum.de/fileadmin/w00cgn/cm/mir-2025/talks/2025-inden-talk-firefox-networking-challenges.pdf) | 2025 | High |
| 7.4 | **Chrome network service memory** (MB, desktop or Android) has no public figure found. Chrome can run the network service in-process (`IsInProcessNetworkService`); recalled that Android uses in-process on low-memory devices — not verified. | — | [Chromium network_service_util.cc](https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:content/public/browser/network_service_util.cc) | 2026-10 | Low |
| 7.5 | **Cellular tail energy**: LTE keeps the radio in a high-power state for seconds after the last packet (≈11.6 s tail in 2012 measurements), so small periodic packets (keepalives, PINGs, beacons) cost far more than their bytes. Paper not re-read this session. | — | Huang et al., MobiSys 2012 (recalled) | 2012 | Low |
| 7.6 | **Receive buffers**: TUM raised the UDP receive buffer 32× (208 KiB default) to avoid client loss at multi-Gbit/s; Firefox uses one shared 64 KiB receive buffer for all QUIC connections. The memory cost of fast receive is a buffer-sizing choice, not inherent. | — | NOMS 2026; Inden 2025 | 2026; 2025 | High |

**Implications:** Every keepalive, PTO, idle timeout and PING in Izcal must be a **substrate-scheduled, coalescable timer** with a known owner, so "hidden pages get no wakeups" is enforceable and testable in the simulator (count wakeups per idle minute). Idle QUIC connections should simply expire (30 s class) rather than be kept alive by PINGs. Set the network-service memory budget from Izcal's own measurement; there is no public baseline to copy.
