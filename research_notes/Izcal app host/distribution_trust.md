**Implications:** the five models form a spectrum of what the store gate actually is — Apple (human policy review at industrial scale), Play (automation plus post-install scanning), Microsoft (fast automated certification), F-Droid/Flathub (source builds plus reproducibility and volunteer review). None of them reviews *behaviour over time*; the incidents that matter all happened after the gate. Izcal's "review is latency reduction, containment is the capability model" (EX-D10) matches the evidence: the gate should be cheap, published and fast; the real safety boundary is the sandbox plus runtime grants.

Note the contrast with Izcal's rule: Android's rotation is a *silent, cryptographic* ownership change, whereas Izcal makes **ownership transfer a review event** (extensions spec 6.12). | — | [Android, APK Signature Scheme v3](https://source.android.com/docs/security/features/apksigning/v3) | current | High (fetched) |

| 2.12 | The extension-domain record of key/identity abuse matches: The Great Suspender (sold Feb 2021, new owner pushed a malicious update to ~2M users) and the 2025 trojanized-extension wave (16 extensions, ~3.2M users, bought from their authors) are ownership-transfer failures, which is why Izcal makes transfer a review event (extensions spec 6.12, EX-D10). | ~2M / 3.2M users | [distribution_signing note §1](../Izcal%20extensions/distribution_signing.md) (The Verge/BleepingComputer rows) | 2021-02 / 2025 | Medium (cross-ref to sibling note) |

**Implications:** the two-signature scheme (author key for identity, platform signature for trust) that EX-D10 already adopts for extensions is exactly the shape the field converged on — Chrome's 2025 "Verified uploads" (§3.5) independently reinvented author-key gating, and Android's v3 proof-of-rotation is the prior art for key rotation *without* review. Izcal's deliberate difference — rotation and transfer are review events, not silent cryptographic facts — is the direct lesson of 2.7–2.10 and 2.12, where a valid signature was the adversary's camouflage. For the app host this must hold identically: platform re-signing after review, author key for identity, transfer = full re-review.

## 3. Update channels as the attack surface

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | The "trusted updater becomes the attack" pattern: every supply-chain incident in §2 reached victims **through the legitimate, signed update channel** (CCleaner's installer, ASUS Live Update, Orion updates, 3CX's desktop-app updater), so install-time review and signature checks were structurally blind to it. The extension analogue is identical: Copyfish (2017, phished account, 30k+ users), the Dec 2024 wave (35+ extensions via phished developer accounts, **2.6M users**, 20 credential-stealers), and the Feb–Mar 2025 wave (16 hijacked extensions, ~**3.2M users**). | 2.6M + 3.2M users | Talos/Kaspersky/CISA/Group-IB rows above; [Chrome Web Store wave coverage](https://www.aboutchromebooks.com/chrome-extension-ecosystem/) | 2017–2025 | High (pattern) / Medium (wave numbers) |
| 3.2 | Staged rollouts exist in every platform but are designed for *quality*, not adversaries: Google Play staged rollouts release to a chosen percentage, never auto-increase, and can be halted (halting stops further users; current recipients stay). Purpose: gradual exposure, crash monitoring, feedback. A malicious update sails through staging because staging never re-checks behaviour. | — | [Google Play, staged rollouts](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/6346149) | current | High (fetched) |
| 3.3 | Update pinning and anti-rollback are the cryptographic fixes, not the policy ones: TUF refuses anything older than what the client has seen and pins hashes/versions in signed metadata (§2.6); Go's checksum database (sum.golang.org) is a transparent, tamper-evident log that pins module content hashes with witnesses. | — | [TUF security](https://theupdateframework.io/security/); [Russ Cox, Transparent Logs](https://research.swtch.com/tlog) | current / 2019 | High (TUF) / Medium (Go log, not fetched) |
| 3.4 | Reproducible builds and binary transparency as the honest answer: F-Droid's rebuild-and-compare (§1.8) verifies that the distributed binary matches the published source — the only model that catches a *build-environment* attacker like CCleaner/SolarWinds before distribution; Sigstore's Rekor (§2.5) gives post-hoc evidence of what was signed when. Neither requires trusting review capacity. | — | [F-Droid reproducible builds](https://f-droid.org/docs/Reproducible_Builds/); [Sigstore overview](https://docs.sigstore.dev/about/overview/) | current | High (both fetched) |
| 3.5 | What actually stopped the recorded attacks: CCleaner — Talos behavioural detection + sinkholing the DGA (2017); ASUS — Kaspersky's telemetry network spotting a signed but anomalous updater (2019); SolarWinds — FireEye's own IR + Microsoft's DLL analysis (2020); 3CX — endpoint behavioural detection (CrowdStrike/SentinelOne, 2023). **None was stopped by a signature, a store gate, or notarization.** Every detection relied on telemetry or deep inspection of behaviour at runtime. | — | rows 2.7–2.10 | 2017–2023 | High (synthesis over High rows) |
| 3.6 | Chrome's post-2024 answer to update-channel hijack: **Verified uploads** (7 May 2025) — a developer registers an RSA public key; uploads must be signed with the matching private key or they are rejected, then Google re-signs with its own key so extension IDs are unchanged. Opt-in, covers future uploads, and does **not** protect against theft of the developer's private key or account compromise itself. Companion measure (28 Jan 2025): enterprise risk-score reports for extensions. | — | [Chrome for Developers, Verified uploads](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/verified-uploads-cws) | 2025-05-07 | High (fetched) |
| 3.7 | Android's post-install measures are the deepest of any platform: Play Protect real-time code-level scanning found **13M new malicious apps outside Play** in 2024; untrusted-APK blocking prevented **36M installation attempts** (200,000 unique apps, 10M devices); Play Protect now auto-revokes permissions from potentially harmful apps. | 13M apps; 36M attempts | [Google, Play ecosystem 2024 post](https://blog.google/security/how-we-kept-google-play-android-app-ecosystem-safe-2024/) | 2025-01 | Medium (blog via search summary) |

**Implications:** the update grid — not the install gate — is where trust is actually exercised, which vindicates EX-D10's shape (uniform update grid, re-review before re-signing, witnessed TUF root, staged capability growth under G175) and adds three specifics for the app host: (a) updates must be **pinned and anti-rollback** (TUF semantics), not just signed; (b) staged rollout is worth having but is not a security control — capability-growth staging (G175) is the adversarial-aware version of it; (c) Izcal's no-install-telemetry rule (EX-D12) foregoes exactly the detection channel that stopped CCleaner, ASUS and 3CX, so the substitute must be explicit: local audit records, a public decision log, and review-triggering signals (ownership change, unusual capability deltas) rather than server-side telemetry.

**Implications:** for Izcal's app host (whose package form is Wasm components per JS-D10), the OCI route via wkg-style tooling is the ecosystem standard worth matching, and WARG's collapse is the cautionary tale against inventing a bespoke registry protocol when a general one exists. WARG's one load-bearing idea — maintainer-signed release logs — survives in Izcal's decision-log + two-signature design. The npm record shows names are the attack surface: Izcal's identity-derives-from-the-author-key rule (6.4) plus content hashes as the install identity is the correct inversion. Registry abuse is cheap and automated (150k-package campaigns), so the app host's directory needs machine-checkable name rules and digest-based addressing from day one, with review reserved for behavioural risk (tier 3, native messaging, sensitive classes), never for name hygiene.

## Implications for Izcal

1. **Review is triage, not a gate.** Apple's 500 reviewers, Google's 92%-AI-assisted pipeline and Microsoft's 3-day automated certification all say the same thing: no operator reviews behaviour over time, and every major incident arrived after the gate, through the update channel. Izcal's published decision log plus containment (capabilities, grants, audit) is the right inversion — the record *is* the product a small vendor can offer that the giants cannot.
2. **Signatures prove possession, continuity and integrity — nothing else.** Authenticode, notarization, APK v2/v3 and Sigstore all state this, and CCleaner, ASUS, SolarWinds and 3CX all had valid signatures from the legitimate publisher. The two-signature scheme (author key for identity, platform signature for trust) is the converged shape — Chrome's 2025 Verified uploads reached it independently. Izcal's departures are both evidence-backed: ownership transfer as a review event (against Android's silent v3 rotation), and review decisions in a public log.
3. **The update channel is the attack surface, and its defences are cryptographic, not procedural.** TUF semantics (anti-rollback, thresholds, offline roots, whole-repository verification), digest pinning, and — where feasible for a component host — F-Droid-style rebuild verification are the controls that actually bind. Staged rollouts help quality, not adversaries; G175's capability-growth staging is the adversarial-aware analogue and should be described as such.
4. **The no-telemetry stance has a real cost that must be paid elsewhere.** Every recorded detection (CCleaner, ASUS, 3CX) came from behavioural telemetry Izcal declines to collect (EX-D12). The substitutes must be named as commitments: local audit records the user can read, review-triggering signals (ownership change, capability deltas, unusual update patterns), the public decision log, and a remote kill whose use is disclosed.
5. **The Wasm registry landscape says: use OCI, don't invent a protocol, and don't trust names.** WARG's deprecation is the cautionary tale; wkg/OCI is the surviving standard for components; npm shows name confusion is industrial-scale abuse (1.2M+ cumulative malicious packages, 209-day median detection). Identity from the author key plus digest-based addressing is correct; name hygiene must be machine-enforced.
6. **Economics force tiering.** At 2–4 review staff, manual review of everything is impossible (~15–45 min per submission at plausible volumes). The design follows the evidence: automation triage, human review for escalated capabilities (tier 3, native messaging, sensitive classes), curation/known-publisher tiers, and the sandbox plus runtime grants as the real boundary — with the decision log publishing what was tested per decision so the tiering itself is auditable.
7. **Local-first sources are managed abuse, not eliminated.** The 2019 enterprise-certificate revocations show both the abuse and the blunt cost of enforcement; the right shape is Izcal's: separate, labelled trust tiers that never mix, per-session/per-container developer mode, and enterprise grants visible in the user UI.
