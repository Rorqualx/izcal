# Izcal shell: security and robustness

Compiled 21 October 2026. This strand covers the security and robustness of the shell UI: permission-prompt behaviour, warning effectiveness, UI redress and spoofing, shell compromise, downloads and external protocols, consent usability, and session/state recovery. Primary sources were preferred: peer-reviewed papers, vendor research posts, standards and vendor documentation, and CVE records. No project's source code, headers, commits, diffs or code reviews were read or cited (M-1 part 3); Chromium material here is limited to published prose documentation, issue-tracker descriptions and severity guidance.

Companion evidence: `research_notes/Shell UI design/security_permissions.md` (14 Oct 2026) covers indicators, warning design, permission grants, disclosure, phishing-resistant identity and download warnings. Rows marked "prior note" rely on that note's checked figures; I did not re-read the primary where the vendor page blocked fetching. `tabs_onboarding_a11y.md` covers recovery-mode history and accessibility mechanics.

Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text. **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data. **Low** = secondary source only. **Gap** = no published evidence found. **Inference** = my conclusion from the cited rows, not a published finding. **S** after a confidence means a single primary source with no independent replication found; most field studies and vendor measurements below are S.

The design constraints this note must inform are in `reports/Izcal security scope.md` sections 6.2, 6.3, 6.6a, 6.14 and 6.14a (CON-R1 to CON-R5, SH-R1 to SH-R9), and the owner-approved surface design is `reports/HGBA shell UI design.md`.

## 1. Permission prompts: volume, comprehension and behaviour

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Notifications dominate prompt volume and have the worst grant rate.** Chrome telemetry over seven days shows notification prompts are three-quarters of all permission prompts and the least likely to be granted. | 74% of all prompts. Grant rate 10% on desktop and 21% on Android per prompt; per-client averages 12% desktop and 23% Android. | 1.8 | **The stated rationale for one-time grants is that users are not ready to make a persistent decision.** User testing also preferred the mobile-style stacked layout. | No uptake numbers published. | Chrome for Developers, "One-time permissions", https://developer.chrome.com/blog/one-time-permissions | 2023-08 | Medium (qualitative) |

| 1.13 | **A page-embedded permission control is a strong intent signal and repairs the blocked state.** Capability-specific elements replaced a generic permission element after vendor feedback. | Self-reported partner results: Zoom −46.9% camera and microphone capture errors; Immobiliare.it +20% successful geolocation flows; ZapImóveis 54.4% recovery from a previously blocked state. `<geolocation>` shipped in Chrome 144. | Chrome for Developers, "Introducing the `<geolocation>` HTML element", https://developer.chrome.com/blog/geolocation-html-element | 2026-01-13 | High (partner-reported, unaudited) |
| 1.14 | **A denial is sticky and a dismissal is nearly a denial.** Chrome records one grant or deny decision for all future visits to a site; three consecutive dismissals are treated as a deny. | 80% of desktop clients and 70% of Android clients never granted a notification prompt over the 10-day study. 36% of Android clients never denied one; 64% denied at least once. | Bilogrevic et al., USENIX Security 2021, https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-bilogrevic.pdf | 2021-08 | High · S |
| 1.15 | **Prompt volume per user is high and attention is low.** Most desktop clients see repeated prompts but do not act on them. | 59% of desktop clients saw two or more prompts; only 39% interacted with any. 19% of desktop clients never clicked a prompt, versus 3% of mobile clients. | Bilogrevic et al., USENIX Security 2021, https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-bilogrevic.pdf | 2021-08 | High · S |
| 1.16 | **Interruption timing has a measurable cognitive cost.** A notification shown during a primary task is worse than one shown between tasks. | +106% annoyance, roughly twice the errors and twice the anxiety when the interruption occurs during the task rather than between tasks (Bailey and Konstan, as summarised in the USENIX 2021 related work). | Bilogrevic et al., USENIX Security 2021, related work, https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec21-bilogrevic.pdf | 2021-08 | Medium (secondary reading) |
| 1.17 | **Prompt comprehension is not established.** No peer-reviewed comprehension study of browser permission-prompt wording was found; the large 2024 study measures sentiment and decision context, not understanding. | — | — | — | Gap |

**Implications:** Require a gesture for every prompt, default notification requests to a quiet chip, and make one-time grants the first option for location, camera and microphone. Never re-prompt after a deny; auto-revoke unused grants and say so. Keep prompt volume inside an explicit budget, because most prompts are dismissed and repeated prompts train dismissal. Do not assume prompt text is understood: measure decisions and outcomes, and prefer page-embedded capability controls for high-intent requests.

## 2. Warnings and interstitials: adherence, comprehension and habituation

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **Warning click-through varies enormously by browser and warning type.** Field telemetry from Firefox and Chrome. | 25M+ impressions. Firefox malware and phishing warnings continued about 10% of the time; Chrome about 25%; Firefox SSL about 33%; Chrome SSL 70.2%. | Akhawe and Felt, "Alice in Warningland", USENIX Security 2013, https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity13/technical-sessions/presentation/akhawe | 2013-08 | High · S |
| 2.2 | **Appearance explains part of the gap; more clicks do not fix comprehension.** A six-condition field experiment on Chrome's SSL warning. | 130,754 impressions. Default Chrome 67.9% click-through versus mock-Firefox 56.1%. Appearance accounts for about 12 of the 37-point gap and Firefox's extra dialog about 8 more. Removing the human figure had no effect (56.1% vs 55.9%). 98% of users who took the first proceed step took the second. | Felt et al., "Experimenting at Scale with Google Chrome's SSL Warning", CHI 2014, https://research.google/pubs/experimenting-at-scale-with-google-chromes-ssl-warning/ | 2014-04 | High (prior note) · S |
| 2.3 | **"Opinionated" design is the strongest documented lever, but comprehension stays low.** The safe action is the prominent button, the proceed path sits behind "Advanced", and the page uses red full-page styling. | Adherence rose from 37% to 62% in the published account (the abstract says "nearly 30% more total users chose to remain safe"). The team "failed at our goal of a well-understood warning" for every text tested. | Felt et al., "Improving SSL Warnings: Comprehension and Adherence", CHI 2015, https://research.google/pubs/improving-ssl-warnings-comprehension-and-adherence/ | 2015-04 | Medium (headline figures conflict between abstract and published account) · S |
| 2.4 | **Habituation is measurable at the neural level and begins at the second exposure.** Polymorphic warnings that vary appearance resist it. | n=25 fMRI; statistically significant drop in visual-processing response after the second exposure. A five-day longitudinal field study and mouse-tracking corroborate. No field click-through rate published. | Anderson et al., CHI 2015, https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_323208 ; Vance et al., MISQ 2018, https://misq.org/tuning-out-security-warnings-a-longitudinal-examination-of-habituation-through-fmri-eye-tracking-and-field-experiments.html | 2015–2018 | Medium |
| 2.5 | **No single failure mode explains non-adherence in the field.** Reasons are many small, context-specific judgements, such as "I trust this site" or "it's my own server". | More than 6,000 Chrome and Firefox users surveyed in situ. | Reeder et al., "An Experience Sampling Study of User Reactions to Browser Warnings in the Field", CHI 2018, https://research.google/pubs/pub46632/ | 2018-04 | High (prior note) · S |
| 2.6 | **Warning effectiveness depends on who deploys it and what it protects.** A social platform's link-shim warnings saw high adherence, unlike browser interstitials; most warned destinations were not on the browser's blocklist. | 6 billion shimmed clicks and 328M warnings over one month (14 Aug–16 Sep 2019). User adherence around 80%. Only 3% of warned sites were ever in Google's Safe Browsing blocklist. Nearly 4% of investigated clients had no browser privacy features, and 7–32% had limited ones. | Li, "Shim Shimmeny", USENIX Security 2020, https://www.usenix.org/system/files/sec20fall_li-frank_prepub.pdf | 2020-08 | High · S |
| 2.7 | **Users ignore contextual warnings but notice interstitials.** Interstitial designs changed information-seeking behaviour. | The paper summarises modern browser warning click-through as roughly 10–25% (USENIX 2021 slides); the paper itself gives no numeric browser-warning table. Eight interstitial designs were tested with a significant effect on information seeking. | Kaiser et al., "Adapting Security Warnings to Counter Online Disinformation", USENIX Security 2021, https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/kaiser | 2021-08 | Medium |
| 2.8 | **Warning frequency is an explicit anti-habituation budget.** Chrome's HTTPS-first warnings were tuned to be rare and are suppressed for frequently visited sites. | Warnings appeared on under 3% of navigations. Median user saw fewer than one warning per week; more than 95% saw fewer than three per week. Private networks are exempt. | Google Security Blog, "HTTPS by default", https://blog.google/security/https-by-defau/ (rollout details via prior note 1.5) | 2025-10-28 | Medium · S |
| 2.9 | **Better text is not a fix for comprehension.** Across the SSL warning redesign, every tested wording failed to produce a well-understood warning. | No text variant reached the team's comprehension goal (CHI 2015). | Felt et al., CHI 2015, https://research.google/pubs/improving-ssl-warnings-comprehension-and-adherence/ | 2015-04 | High (qualitative) · S |
| 2.10 | **Warning fatigue is a design constraint, not a user failing.** Repeated exposure reduces attention, context changes the decision, and the same warning repeated becomes wallpaper. | See 2.4 (second-exposure drop), 2.5 (context-specific reasons) and 2.8 (frequency budget). | Reeder et al. 2018; Anderson et al. 2015; Google 2025 | 2018–2025 | Inference from 2.4, 2.5, 2.8 |

**Implications:** Use full-page, opinionated interstitials with one prominent safe action and the bypass behind a disclosure. Add a deliberate delay before the action activates and vary appearance by threat class. Keep a warning budget: rare, specific, and suppressed for sites the user visits often. Measure adherence and comprehension separately, and do not spend effort on wording alone when the evidence says understanding will not improve.

## 3. Identity signals: padlock, URL and lookalikes

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **Most users misread the padlock as a general trust signal.** A US survey of 1,880 Chrome users. | 74% said the padlock means "the website is secure"; 70% "the connection is secure"; 51% "safe to enter data"; 30% privacy; 23% phishing protection; 19% virus protection. Only 11% expected connection security exclusively; 59% were over-confident and 30% misinformed. | von Zezschwitz, Chen and Stark, "It builds trust with the customers", IEEE S&P Workshops (SecWeb), https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/ (PDF: https://cs.brown.edu/courses/csci2390/2024/readings/padlock.pdf) | 2022 | High · S |
| 3.2 | **The padlock changes behaviour, and most people who notice it click it.** Removing or replacing it reduced willingness to continue. | Baseline 74% would probably continue an order; with no icon 50%; with the tune icon 53% (both p<.001). 52% mentioned the padlock when justifying trust. 81% of those who mentioned it clicked it, versus 26% of those who did not. | von Zezschwitz et al., SecWeb 2022, https://cs.brown.edu/courses/csci2390/2024/readings/padlock.pdf | 2022 | High · S |
| 3.3 | **Chrome replaced the lock with a neutral site-controls icon in Chrome 117.** HTTPS is now the norm, nearly all phishing uses HTTPS, and users never learned the lock was clickable. HTTP pages still show "Not secure". | Over 95% of Chrome/Windows page loads were HTTPS. Only 11% understood the lock (row 3.1). Users opened site controls more often in testing and showed no added confusion. | Chromium Blog, "An Update on the Lock Icon", https://blog.chromium.org/2023/05/an-update-on-lock-icon.html | 2023-05-02 | High (prior note) · S |
| 3.4 | **No URL or EV format has been shown to improve identity judgements.** Removing the EV indicator did not change most behavioural metrics, and 14 survey iterations of EV and URL formatting found no significant improvement. | Field experiment "orders of magnitude larger than any prior study"; only one origin was significant after Bonferroni correction in the per-origin analysis. 14 iterations across Chrome and Safari. | Thompson et al., "The Web's Identity Crisis", USENIX Security 2019, https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/thompson | 2019-08 | High · S |
| 3.5 | **Users cannot reliably parse URLs, especially lookalikes.** | Hosts identified correctly in 58% of URLs on average: 93% for real URLs, 40% for obfuscated or lookalike URLs. Participants were confident despite errors. | Reynolds et al., "Measuring Identity Confusion with URLs", CHI 2020, https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/ | 2020-04 | Medium-High (prior note) · S |
| 3.6 | **Showing only the "who" part of a URL barely helps phishing detection.** | n=200. Detection 71% with the full URL versus 76% with domain only; not significant. | Mossano et al. (KIT), https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000163039/151499531 | 2023 | Medium · S |
| 3.7 | **Chrome abandoned its simplified-domain experiment because it did not move security metrics.** No metric values were published. | Scheme and "www" stay elided; the path is shown. | PortSwigger coverage of the abandonment, https://portswigger.net/daily-swig/google-abandons-plans-to-simplify-urls-in-chrome-following-real-world-testing | 2021-06 | Medium (secondary; vendor numbers unpublished) |
| 3.8 | **Chromium's IDN policy displays punycode only for specific confusable cases.** Cases: mixed-script labels confusable under UTS #39, whole-script confusables on a TLD not associated with that script, digit lookalikes, and skeleton matches against top or visited domains. The stated principle is to avoid over-punycoding because it harms non-Latin users. | Policy, no effectiveness data. Lookalike domains also get an on-device "Did you mean...?" interstitial or a smaller safety tip depending on confidence. | Chromium docs, IDN, https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md ; Lookalikes, https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/lookalikes/lookalike-domains.md | Living docs | High (policy) / Gap (effect) |
| 3.9 | **IDN homographs defeated the browser filters of the time.** A Cyrillic "аррӏе.com" passed as Apple in Chrome, Firefox, Opera and Internet Explorer when a Cyrillic system language was enabled; Safari was not vulnerable. | Reported to Chrome and Firefox on 20 Jan 2017; Chrome fixed in 58 (the post says 59). Mozilla declined a browser-side fix, arguing domain owners must check whole-script homographs; Firefox users could force punycode. | Xudong Zheng, "Phishing with Unicode Domains", https://www.xudongz.com/blog/2017/idn-phishing/ | 2017-04 | High · S |
| 3.10 | **The safe display default is the full URL with the registrable domain emphasized, plus active lookalike warnings.** URL styling alone is null; hiding the path is null; the measurable defence is the interstitial or tip. | See 3.4 (no format helped), 3.6 (domain-only n.s.), 3.7 (trial abandoned) and 3.8 (lookalike interstitial). | Inference from 3.4, 3.6, 3.7, 3.8 | 2026-10 | Inference |

**Implications:** Use no positive "secure" indicator. Replace the padlock with a neutral, clickable site-controls entry point and keep a strong negative signal for HTTP. Show the full URL with the registrable domain emphasized, but do not expect any URL format to stop phishing. Put the effort into IDN rules, lookalike interstitials and credential binding. Test every label for comprehension; icon recognition cannot be assumed.

## 4. UI redress and spoofing: the trusted region and the prompt rectangle

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | **Browser UI is a trust boundary and the "line of death" is its spatial expression.** Trustworthy UI should appear above the line, or at least be anchored to or overlap it. The doc warns the boundary is poorly understood and that recognizability and signals such as branding or account labels matter more than position alone. | Policy. Spoofing is untrusted page content passing as browser UI; the harm is data exposure or a pushed action. | Chromium docs, "Security considerations for browser UI", https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/security-considerations-for-browser-ui.md | Living doc | High (policy) |
| 4.2 | **The two attacks are spoofing and clickjacking, and browser UI clickjacking is not systematically solved.** "Clickjacking is mostly under control on the web, but it hasn't been systematically tackled for browser UI." The doc also bans site-authored explanations inside permission prompts and warns that occlusion can be worse than counterfeiting. | Policy. | Chromium docs, "Security considerations for browser UI", https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/security-considerations-for-browser-ui.md | Living doc | High |
| 4.3 | **The documented defences are structural, not textual.** Do not default-select accept; require multiple clicks or gestures; introduce a delay before the action activates; prefer two interactions in distinct locations. | Large interstitials use about 3 seconds; dialogs use about 500 ms. `InputEventActivationProtector` ignores UI events within 500 ms of the sensitive UI appearing. | Chromium docs, "Security considerations for browser UI", https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/docs/security/security-considerations-for-browser-ui.md | Living doc | High |

## Rules the shell must not break

1. **No page or shell content may draw a security prompt.** Every prompt is composited above page content, its rectangle is owned by the broker, and page strings never enter it (rows 4.1–4.3, 5.3–5.5, 7.1–7.2).
2. **No approval without broker-observed OS input inside the prompt rectangle.** Shell messages saying "approved" are ignored; synthetic, relayed and out-of-rectangle input never counts (rows 4.4–4.7, 7.3–7.6, 7.11).
3. **No input class is exempt.** Mouse, touch, keyboard, drag and assistive-technology input get the same delay, visibility and hit-test rules (rows 4.5, 4.6, 7.4, 7.5, 7.9–7.10).
4. **No default-selected accept and no Enter-to-approve.** The safe action is explicit, and an approval cannot be produced by a repeated or carried-over gesture (rows 4.3, 7.2, 7.3).
5. **No permission prompt without a user gesture, and no re-prompt after a deny.** Notification prompts start as a quiet chip and only escalate on a strong intent signal (rows 1.1–1.6, 1.13, 1.14).
6. **No positive trust indicator.** No padlock, no "secure" badge, no brand colour on a security state; HTTP is marked "Not secure" and identity detail sits in the site panel (rows 3.1–3.4, 3.10).
7. **The shell and prompt renderers never hold a secret.** Usernames only; the broker writes passwords and passkeys into the target form after consent (rows 5.3, 5.5, 5.7–5.9).
8. **No automatic open of a dangerous download and no silent external-protocol launch.** Both need a gesture and a per-origin, broker-approved prompt naming the file type or the scheme, handler and origin (rows 6.3, 6.8–6.10, 6.13).
9. **No restored session may re-grant a permission, replay a secret or reopen a crash loop.** Restore is a new page load; auto-revoked and one-time grants are re-asked (rows 8.2, 8.5, 8.6).
10. **Recovery never becomes a bypass.** The native recovery window performs a fixed, enumerated set of actions, states what each disables, and cannot be reached or imitated by page or shell content (rows 5.11, 8.2–8.4).
11. **Accessibility is a governed consent channel.** An AT client's input is broker-observed and policy-scoped; no prompt class is approvable by an AT-only path (rows 7.9–7.10).
12. **Warnings stay rare, specific and measurable.** A warning budget exists, appearance varies by threat class, and adherence and comprehension are measured separately (rows 2.4, 2.5, 2.7–2.10, 3.8).
