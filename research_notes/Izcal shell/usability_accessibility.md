# Izcal shell: usability and accessibility

Compiled 21 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred: peer-reviewed papers, W3C standards and notes, vendor research and product documentation. Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, a vendor claim without data, or a claim read in a prior note; **Low** = secondary source only; **Gap** = no public evidence found.

**Scope and relation to the other shell notes.** This note is the usability and accessibility evidence base for the Izcal shell report. It complements `Shell UI design/tabs_onboarding_a11y.md` and `Shell UI design/security_permissions.md`; where the same study carries a design-relevant number, it is cited here with the shell-specific implication rather than repeated in full. The tab, URL, settings, onboarding, history, download, find, keyboard, localization, visual and prompt findings below are the ones the shell's targets UI-1 to UI-11 depend on.

**Method and M-1 note.** No project's source code, headers, commits, diffs or code reviews were read or cited (M-1 part 3). Product behaviour comes from help pages, vendor blogs, published papers, standards and issue-tracker prose. The shared web-search budget ran out before this note was written; later sources were fetched directly. Claims that could not be checked this session are graded Medium or Low. Findings that rest on a single study, a vendor claim or an issue report say so in the Numbers, Source or Confidence cell; **High** means the source was read and the claim checked, not that the result has been replicated.

## 1. Tabs, tab overload and session continuity

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Tab switching is a primary navigation mechanism, not a side feature.** In a logged study, most participants switched tabs more often than they used Back, making tab switching the second most important navigation action after link clicking. | N=21 Firefox users, 13–21 days each. 17 of 21 switched tabs more often than they used Back. Back was a median 7.1% of navigation actions. 45% of tabs were selected exactly once and 25% were never selected. One participant reached 42 open tabs. | [Dubroy & Balakrishnan, "A Study of Tabbed Browsing Among Mozilla Firefox Users," CHI 2010](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/chi2010_tabbedbrowsing.pdf) | 2010-04 | High (small, old sample) |
| 1.2 | **Tabs do four different jobs at once.** The reasons participants gave were keeping reminders, multitasking, moving back and forth between pages, and comparing pages. The browser has no first-class object for any of them. | 17/21 used tabs as reminders; 11/21 for multitasking; 13/21 to go back and forth; 10/21 to compare; 14/21 valued opening links in the background; 8/21 cited task grouping in separate windows and 8/21 cited seeing two pages at once. | [Dubroy & Balakrishnan, CHI 2010](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/chi2010_tabbedbrowsing.pdf) | 2010-04 | High |
| 1.3 | **Parallel browsing is the norm.** A log study of millions of users found that most tab sessions contain at least one switch, and that tabs divide attention rather than increase the number of pages read. | 57.4% of tab sessions had at least one tab switch; 42.6% had none. 88.7% of pageviews stayed on the same tab for the next pageview. 5–10 pageviews per tab was common. The authors conclude that tabs do not increase total pages viewed; they split activity across tabs. | [Huang & White, "Parallel Browsing Behavior on the Web," Hypertext 2010](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/ParallelBrowsing_HT10.pdf) | 2010-06 | High |
| 1.4 | **Tab overload is driven by fear of loss.** Users keep tabs as reminders and as protection against having to find a page again, while crashes and attention costs push the other way. | N=103 survey plus 10 information-worker interviews with four tab walkthroughs over two weeks. 55% said they "feel like I can't let go of my tabs"; 59% agreed they would find tabs that should be closed; only 19% blamed laziness. About 25% said a browser or computer had crashed from too many tabs; about 30% self-described as tab hoarders (press release and coverage). | [Chang et al., "When the Tab Comes Due," CHI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445585); [CMU release](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2021/may/overcoming-tab-overload.html) | 2021-05 | Medium (abstract and release; full text blocked) |
| 1.5 | **Firefox tab groups reached a large cumulative user count, but the vendor published no share.** The number is consistent with tab organization being a minority behaviour, not a default need. | "More than 18 million people" had used tab groups, cumulative since Firefox 138. No denominator, no active-use share. | [Mozilla, "Firefox tab groups just got an upgrade"](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/tab-groups-updates/) | 2025-10/11 | Medium (vendor, method undisclosed) |
| 1.6 | **Vertical tabs are now offered by every major desktop browser, and vendors describe users switching between vertical and horizontal.** Edge added an always-visible toggle and a collapsible pane after research found this back-and-forth. No vendor publishes adoption. | Firefox 136 shipped vertical tabs from Mozilla Connect feedback (2 April 2025). Chrome shipped native vertical tabs on 7 April 2026. Edge's research and toggle date to March 2021. | [Mozilla vertical tabs](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/vertical-tabs-and-the-firefox-community/); [Google Chrome vertical tabs](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/chrome/new-chrome-productivity-features/); [Microsoft Research, Edge vertical tabs](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=728920) | 2021-03 to 2026-04 | High (qualitative); Gap (adoption) |
| 1.7 | **Power-user organization features have tiny reach.** Arc's Spaces, Live Folders and Calendar preview were used regularly by a small fraction of daily users, which is the best public proxy for opt-in tab organization. | 5.52% of DAUs regularly used more than one Space; 4.17% used Live Folders; 0.4% used Calendar Preview on hover. By contrast Dia's chat-with-tabs reached 40% of DAUs and personalization 37%. | [The Browser Company, "Letter to Arc members 2025"](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025) | 2025-05-26 | High |
| 1.8 | **Tab groups were designed for two user types and tested internally for months, but never measured in public.** The design distinction was "tab minimalists" versus "tab collectors". | No adoption or outcome numbers published. | [Google, "Manage tabs with Google Chrome"](https://blog.google/products/chrome/manage-tabs-with-google-chrome/) | 2020-05-13 | High (qualitative only) |
| 1.9 | **Tab search exists, but its use and success are unmeasured.** Chrome exposes tab search from the address bar and Firefox exposes a tab-search control; neither publishes usage, query success or time-to-tab. | No public data. | [Chrome Help, "Search your tabs"](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/10005023); Firefox tab-search documentation | 2026 (living) | Gap |
| 1.10 | **Recently closed is a recovery path with no published use.** Both Chrome and Firefox offer Ctrl+Shift+T and a Recently closed menu, but no vendor reports how often users recover a tab this way or whether the label is understood. | No public data. | [Chrome Help, "Reopen closed tabs"](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95426); Firefox Help, "Restore previous session" | 2026 (living) | Gap |
| 1.11 | **Split view shipped without public task-benefit evidence.** Edge and Chrome both offer side-by-side tabs; no vendor publishes adoption, comparison-task success or the effect on tab count. | No public data. | Microsoft Edge help, "Split screen"; Chrome help, split tabs | 2024–2026 | Gap |
| 1.12 | **Session restore is the safety net under tab overload, but its use is unmeasured.** Chrome offers "Continue where you left off"; Firefox can restore the previous session. The behaviour is documented; the reliance is not. | No public usage or success rate. The fear of losing tabs is documented in 1.4. | [Chrome Help, "Continue where you left off"](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95426); [Firefox Help, "Restore previous session"](https://support.mozilla.org/kb/restore-previous-session) | 2026 (living) | Medium (documented behaviour); Gap (usage) |

**Implications:** Ship horizontal tabs with tab search and recently closed in Stage 1, and make vertical tabs a toggle rather than the default. Do not assume users will organize tabs into groups or spaces: the public evidence says most will not. Session restore is a safety feature for a documented fear, so it should be on by default and its recovery should be visible, not buried.

## 2. Address bar and navigation

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **Typing a URL is a small share of navigation.** Links dominate, and direct access is roughly a tenth of actions, so the address bar's main job is search and recall, not URL entry. | N=25 long-term click-stream study: links 44% of navigation actions, form submissions including search 15%, new window or tab 11%, Back 14.3%, direct access 9%. | [Obendorf, Weinreich, Herder & Mayer, "Web Page Revisitation Revisited," CHI 2007](https://vsis-www.informatik.uni-hamburg.de/getDoc.php/publications/280/chi2007-newformat.pdf) | 2007-04 | High (dated) |
| 2.2 | **The omnibox is designed to accelerate typing, not to be a dashboard.** Chromium's principles merge URL and search entry, keep the dropdown minimal, keep the default match stable as async results arrive, and treat single-word input as a search because most single words are searches. | Qualitative; no published share figures. | [Chromium, "Omnibox design principles"](https://www.chromium.org/user-experience/omnibox/) | living (accessed 2026-10-21) | High |
| 2.3 | **The padlock is clicked by half of users, and replacing it with a neutral icon reduces clicks and discoverability.** This is usability evidence for a site-controls surface, not only security evidence. | N=1,880. 50% clicked the padlock in the baseline condition; with the tune icon, the click ratio fell to 40%. Discoverability of Page Info was 42% desktop and 52% mobile; 36% desktop and 42% mobile reached it through the overflow menu. Of users shown no icon, 30% still clicked the icon area; only 9% of users who did not mention the padlock clicked it. | [von Zezschwitz, Chen & Stark, "It builds trust with the customers," IEEE S&P Workshops 2022](https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/) | 2022 | High |
| 2.4 | **The padlock is widely misunderstood.** Most users read it as a general trust signal, not as connection security, and only a minority refer to it when judging a site. | 89% held misconceptions; 11% were correct; 59% over-confident (connection security plus more); 30% misinformed. 74% read it as "the website is secure", 70% as "the connection is secure", 51% as "safe to enter data". Only 23–44% mentioned the padlock when judging trust. With no icon, 50% would continue an order versus 74% at baseline; with the tune icon, 53% versus 74%. | [von Zezschwitz et al., 2022](https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/) | 2022 | High |
| 2.5 | **No URL or EV format tested has improved identity judgements.** Fourteen survey iterations of EV and URL formatting, including domain-emphasis variants, produced no significant improvement; removing EV did not change most behaviour. | 14 format iterations across Chrome and Safari. A field experiment "orders of magnitude larger" than prior work; after per-origin correction only one origin was significant. EV was described as adding "little value in its current form". | [Thompson et al., "The Web's Identity Crisis," USENIX Security 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/thompson) | 2019-08 | High |
| 2.6 | **Users cannot reliably identify the host in a URL, and they are confident while wrong.** Lookalike and obfuscated URLs are where the failure concentrates. | Hosts identified correctly in 58% of URLs on average: 93% for real URLs, 40% for obfuscated or lookalike URLs. Participants were confident despite errors. | [Reynolds et al., "Measuring Identity Confusion with Uniform Resource Locators," CHI 2020](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/) | 2020-04 | Medium-High (abstract; split from prior note) |
| 2.7 | **Showing only the registrable domain was tried and abandoned.** Chrome's simplified-domain field trial hid the path until focus; it ended because it "didn't move relevant security metrics". Full URLs returned, though the scheme and "www" stay elided. | No metric values published. | [PortSwigger, "Google abandons plans to simplify URLs"](https://portswigger.net/daily-swig/google-abandons-plans-to-simplify-urls-in-chrome-following-real-world-testing) | trial 2020-08 to 2021-06 | Medium (outcome; no numbers) |
| 2.8 | **IDN display is a per-label safety decision, not a global punycode switch.** In 2017 a Cyrillic "аррӏе.com" passed as Apple in Chrome, Firefox, Opera and Internet Explorer when a Cyrillic system language was enabled; Safari was not vulnerable. Modern policy shows punycode for confusable mixed-script labels, digit lookalikes and skeleton matches, but not for all non-Latin text. | `xn--80ak6aa92e.com`; UTS #39 defines confusables, mixed-script detection and restriction levels. Broad punycoding harms non-Latin users, so the policy is targeted. | [Zheng, "Phishing with Unicode Domains"](https://www.xudongz.com/blog/2017/idn-phishing/); [Unicode UTS #39](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr39/) | 2017-04; living | High (incident and policy); Gap (effectiveness) |
| 2.9 | **The omnibox usage split is unknown.** No vendor publishes the share of omnibox navigations that are typed URLs, searches, history suggestions or bookmark suggestions. Design debates about the default match are therefore not backed by public usage data. | No public data. | — | — | Gap |

**Implications:** Keep the full URL visible with the registrable domain emphasised, but treat that as a cheap convention, not a phishing control: every tested format failed to improve identity judgements. Invest the effort in an active lookalike warning, a neutral site-controls surface that is still discoverable, and per-label IDN rules. Do not hide the path; Chrome's domain-only trial did not move security metrics and was reverted.

## 3. Settings and defaults

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **Few users change settings at all.** In a sample of Word settings files, less than 5% had changed anything; because autosave shipped off by accident, about 95% ran without it and assumed the default was deliberate. | <5% of "several hundred" settings files. | [Spool, "Do users change their settings?", UIE](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/) | 2011-09-14 | Medium (informal study) |
| 3.2 | **Customization concentrates in experienced users.** The counterpoint to 3.1 is that a minority will customize deeply if the product lets them. | 92% of WordPerfect users had customized something, with a mean of 9.1 changes. | [Page, Johnsgard, Albert & Allen, "User Customization of a Word Processor," CHI 1996](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html) | 1996-04 | High |
| 3.3 | **Defaults dominate privacy behaviour.** Before Firefox turned Enhanced Tracking Protection on by default, only about 20% had enabled it; the default flip took coverage to effectively everyone. | About 20% opt-in before; about 100% after (new users from June 2019, all users with Firefox 69). | [Mozilla, "Firefox's enhanced tracking protection"](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/?p=62557) | 2019-09-03 | High |
| 3.4 | **Defaults dominate browser choice too.** Most users stay with the preset browser, and only about a tenth actively install a different one. | About 90% of iPhone users use Safari; about 25% of Samsung users use Samsung Internet; about 10% of users actively install another browser. | [Mozilla, "Can browser choice screens be effective?"](https://research.mozilla.org/files/2023/09/Can-browser-choice-screens-be-effective_-Mozilla-experiment-report.pdf) | 2023-09 | High |
| 3.5 | **Firefox's 2026 settings redesign was driven by a documented findability problem.** "General" was retired and split into Appearance, Accessibility, Languages, and Tabs & browsing; Passwords and Autofill were reworked; search was improved. The stated problems were crowded pages, scattered related options, and users unable to predict where an option lives. Methods included interviews, usability testing, card sorting, usage-data analysis and community feedback. | No metrics published. | [Mozilla, "Firefox settings"](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/firefox-settings/) | 2026-06-16 | High (design); Gap (outcomes) |
| 3.6 | **The 2017 Preferences reorg already used card sorting and added settings search.** It cut the number of panes and moved sections, which shows this is a recurring problem rather than a one-off redesign. | Panes reduced, planned to five; section moves tracked publicly; card sort run. | [gHacks, "Mozilla updating Firefox preferences"](https://www.ghacks.net/2017/03/07/mozilla-updating-firefox-preferences/) | 2017-03 | Medium |
| 3.7 | **Chrome's settings have converged on the same shape: a short "Basics" list, a separate Passwords and Autofill group, and privacy promoted out of Advanced.** Settings search is present. | "Basics" trimmed to four items (Search engine, Address bar, Privacy & security, Safety check); privacy promoted from Advanced in 2019. | [Android Central, "Chrome privacy settings"](https://androidcentral.com/google-wants-make-privacy-and-security-settings-more-prominent-chrome) | 2019–2025 | Medium |
| 3.8 | **Settings-open rate and settings-search use are unmeasured.** No vendor publishes how often users open settings, how often they use search there, or whether search succeeds. | No public data. | — | — | Gap |
| 3.9 | **AI settings are new and unmeasured.** Chrome places AI Mode in the address bar and lets users hide it from a right-click menu. No vendor publishes comprehension, opt-in or opt-out rates for AI controls. | No public data; feature behaviour from product help. | [Chrome Help, "Search the web in Chrome"](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95440?hl=en) | 2026 (living) | Medium (feature); Gap (usage and comprehension) |

**Implications:** Ship a small number of task-named groups with search, and put safety-relevant behaviour on the safe default because most users will never change it. Do not use settings as the place where a security or privacy promise is delivered. For AI, the control must be visible, named in user terms, and off by default; plan to measure comprehension because no baseline exists.

## 4. Onboarding, import and first run

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | **Most users skip import when asked, and automatic import is easy to miss but can work.** Firefox's research found users skipped because they wanted to try the browser first, wanted a fresh start, or did not know how long import would take. An auto-import prototype with undo was tested and then shelved in favour of a better prompt. | 22/67 participants discovered the auto-import message unprompted; 57/67 kept the imported data, including 13 of 17 who had voiced privacy concerns; 14–67% voiced privacy concerns depending on design. In a 1% in-product experiment, longer import time was associated with fewer subsequent sessions (p=0.00127). | [Davidson et al., "Will Automatically Importing User Data Help Overcome the Blank Slate Problem?", CHI 2018 LBW](https://research.mozilla.org/files/2018/05/Will-Automatically-Importing-User-Data-Help-Overcome-the-Blank-Slate-Problem.pdf) | 2018-04 | High |
| 4.2 | **A default-browser prompt at first run did not change retention.** Mozilla moved the modal prompt from first run to the second startup in Firefox 55; preliminary cohort data showed no retention change, and the small install difference was not a retention measure. | About 5% higher average daily installs for the prompt cohort; retention "no change (positive or negative)". | [Bugzilla 1143116 (duplicate of 1367073)](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1143116) | about 2015–2017 | Medium |
| 4.3 | **When the choice screen appears changes what users pick.** Showing the browser choice screen at first browser launch instead of device setup raised preset-browser choice from 11% to 19%; most users still preferred device setup. | N=12,000 simulated device setups. 98% of choosers expected to stay with their pick versus 51% of the control group. Chrome ranked fourth instead of first lost 7.1 pp; the preset browser lost 6.3 pp. 65% preferred setup timing versus 33% first launch. "Felt in control" rose 12% to 75%; satisfaction with the information rose from 56% to 64%. | [Mozilla, "Can browser choice screens be effective?"](https://research.mozilla.org/files/2023/09/Can-browser-choice-screens-be-effective_-Mozilla-experiment-report.pdf) | 2023-09 | High |
| 4.4 | **Onboarding completion versus length has no public benchmark.** Mozilla's about:welcome is a multi-screen, experiment-driven flow, but no vendor publishes completion, skip or drop-off by screen. | No public data. | [Mozilla, about:welcome documentation](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/browser/components/asrouter/docs/about-welcome.html) | living | Gap |
| 4.5 | **Import is a set of lossy, loosely specified formats.** Bookmarks use the ad-hoc Netscape HTML dialect (Firefox can also export bookmarks as JSON); passwords use CSV. Merge and duplicate semantics are not documented, and dates are decimal seconds since 1970 in the bookmark format. | Netscape bookmark file: `<!DOCTYPE NETSCAPE-Bookmark-file-1>`, nested `DL`/`DT`, folders as `H3`, `ADD_DATE`, `LAST_VISIT`, `LAST_MODIFIED` in seconds. Chrome imports into "Other bookmarks" when bookmarks already exist; no documented merge rule. | [Microsoft, Netscape Bookmark File Format](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/windows/internet-explorer/ie-developer/platform-apis/aa753582%28v=vs.85%29); [Chrome Help 96816](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/96816) | 2017-08-15; accessed 2026-10-21 | High (formats); Medium (merge semantics) |
| 4.6 | **Privacy-policy comprehension is poor, and almost nobody reads policies fully.** People are asked to approve policies constantly, but most do not read them and most do not understand the law that is supposed to protect them. | N=4,272 U.S. adults. 97% are ever asked to approve a privacy policy; 9% always and 13% often read one; 36% never do; only 22% of readers read all the way through. 63% understand very little or nothing about privacy laws; 59% say the same about what companies do with their data; 81% say the risks of company data collection outweigh the benefits. | [Pew Research Center, "Americans and Privacy"](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2019/11/15/americans-and-privacy-concerned-confused-and-feeling-lack-of-control-over-their-personal-information/) | 2019-11-15 | High |
| 4.7 | **First-run choices become long-term choices.** Because few users change settings (3.1) and defaults dominate privacy behaviour (3.3), anything selected or left unselected during onboarding is effectively the product's long-term configuration. | See 3.1 and 3.3. | Inference from 3.1, 3.3, 4.1 | 2026-10 | Inference |
| 4.8 | **No evidence shows that onboarding personalization improves retention.** The one retention test found here, the default-browser prompt, was flat. Theme, import and default-browser cards are usually justified by preference, not by retention. | No public retention effect found. | See 4.2; no other public test located | 2026-10 | Gap |

**Implications:** Keep onboarding to four skippable screens and never block first paint on import. Import after first paint, show progress, offer undo, and state what is and is not copied; the privacy copy matters because 14–67% of tested users raised concerns. Do not promise retention effects that have not been measured.

## 5. History, bookmarks and sync

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | **Revisiting is the normal case, not the exception.** Adar et al. summarise prior work finding that 50–80% of surfing involves previously visited pages, and their own log study characterises the patterns of return. | 612,000 opt-in users over five weeks; 54,788 URLs sampled. Prior-work range: 50% to 80% of surfing involves previously visited pages. Four revisitation patterns: fast, medium, slow and hybrid. | [Adar, Teevan & Dumais, "Large Scale Analysis of Web Revisitation Patterns," CHI 2008](http://cond.org/chi1159-adar.pdf) | 2008-04 | High |
| 5.2 | **Bookmarks are created but rarely used for retrieval.** In an elicited personal-information-retrieval study, most bookmarked targets were not reached through the bookmark facility, and bookmarked sites were not re-found better than non-bookmarked ones. | 50 participants, 250 targets. 41/250 (16%) retrieved via the bookmark facility; 9/250 (4%) through the bookmark menu hierarchy; the rest were visible in the bookmarks bar. | [Bergman, Whittaker & Schooler, bookmark re-finding study, Journal of Librarianship and Information Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0961000620949652) | 2021 | Medium (read in prior note) |
| 5.3 | **History lists fail at recognition and discovery.** Users struggle to recognise pages from titles and URLs and to find items in long lists; history search exists but has a high discovery cost, so users do not invest in it. | No measured success rate. | [Kaasten & Greenberg, MSc thesis on history and bookmark integration](https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2001-Kaasten.MScThesis.pdf) | 2001 | Medium |
| 5.4 | **The address bar became a search surface over the user's own data.** Firefox 3's Smart Location Bar ranked history and bookmark matches by frequency and recency and learned from selection; the Star button and tags made bookmarking a two-click action. | Firefox 3, 17 June 2008. | [Firefox 3 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/3.0/releasenotes/) | 2008-06-17 | High |
| 5.5 | **The history data model is visits, not pages, and it records how each visit happened.** Chrome's history API exposes 11 transition types including link, typed, auto_bookmark, form_submit, reload and keyword; each visit has an ID, time, referring visit and local-versus-synced flag. | 11 transition types: link, typed, auto_bookmark, auto_subframe, manual_subframe, generated, auto_toplevel, form_submit, reload, keyword, keyword_generated. | [Chrome history API](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/reference/api/history) | accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 5.6 | **History-search use and success are unmeasured.** Every major browser ships history search; none publishes how often it is opened, what users search for, or whether they find the page. | No public data. | — | — | Gap |
| 5.7 | **Sync expectations are unmeasured.** No representative study was found of what users expect sync to copy, how they expect it to be protected, or what they think happens when sync is off. Vendors document encrypted sync and account-scoped data, not user expectations. | No public data. | [Chrome Sync help](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/185277); [Firefox Sync help](https://support.mozilla.org/kb/how-do-i-set-sync-my-computer) | 2026 (living) | Gap |
| 5.8 | **Bookmark rot is real but unquantified.** No browser publishes link-rot or bookmark-deletion rates; the claim that users never delete bookmarks rests on old, small studies. | No public data. | — (absence checked in vendor docs and the studies above) | 2026-10-21 | Gap |

**Implications:** Treat history and bookmarks as one searchable store with provenance, not two lists. Surface bookmarks where the page is, not only in a folder tree: the menu hierarchy retrieved 4% of targets. A prettier history list will not fix a recognition problem; the shell must answer "where did this row come from, and is it still what I saw?"

## 6. Downloads

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | **Chrome replaced the bottom download shelf with a toolbar-anchored bubble, for trust separation and richer warnings.** Early experiment feedback changed how often the bubble auto-opens, and users can disable auto-open. | No usage or outcome numbers. | [Chromium Blog, "Redesigning Chrome downloads"](https://blog.chromium.org/2023/08/redesigning-chrome-downloads-to-keep.html) | 2023-08-03 | High (qualitative) |
| 6.2 | **Download warnings are graded by confidence, and deep scanning finds far more malware.** Chrome uses "Suspicious" (grey, lower confidence) and "Dangerous" (red, high confidence); enhanced protection deep-scans files and flags malware at more than 50 times the base rate. Encrypted archives prompt for a password. | More than 50× flag rate for deep-scanned files. | 6.3 | **Download resumption is a protocol feature, not a UX guarantee.** HTTP Range requests and 206 responses let a client continue a partial transfer; browsers retry interrupted downloads, but no vendor publishes resumption success, time lost or user-visible failure rates. | HTTP Range and 206 Partial Content are specified in RFC 9110. No public UX data. | [RFC 9110, section 14](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-14) | 2022-06 | High (protocol); Gap (UX) |
| 6.4 | **Download-manager placement has not been compared.** Shelf, bubble and full page each exist; no study compares discoverability, interruption or task success across them. | No public data. | — | — | Gap |
| 6.5 | **Download labels can confuse.** A filed report says Firefox's Ctrl+J window titled "Library" is confusing "from a User Interface Design aspect", and support could not tell whether the Downloads dialog was open. | One bug report, not a rate. | [Bugzilla 1936387](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1936387) | about 2025 | Low |

**Implications:** Anchor download status to trusted chrome, use graded warnings with distinct visuals per confidence, and make the dangerous-file action the safe one. Resume with Range and verify the result; tell the user what was checked and what was not. Name the surface "Downloads", not "Library".

## 7. Find in page

## Usability requirements with evidence

1. **Show the full URL with the registrable domain emphasised; do not hide the path.** Every tested URL format failed to improve identity judgements (2.5), Chrome's domain-only trial was abandoned for not moving security metrics (2.7), and users identify only 40% of lookalike hosts (2.6).
2. **Make site controls a neutral, discoverable surface, not a padlock that doubles as a trust badge.** The padlock is misunderstood by 89% of users (2.4), yet half click it and replacing it cuts clicks from 50% to 40% (2.3); display punycode only for confusable labels (2.8).
3. **Ship horizontal tabs by default with tab search and recently closed, and make vertical tabs an optional toggle.** Tab switching is a primary navigation action (1.1), parallel browsing is the norm (1.3), and the best public proxy for opt-in organization, Arc Spaces, reached 5.52% of daily users (1.7).
4. **Restore the previous session by default and make recovery visible.** Fear of losing tabs is a documented driver of overload (1.4), and session restore is the existing safety net (1.12).
5. **Use a short, task-named settings list with search, and put safety on the default.** Fewer than 5% of sampled Word users changed any setting (3.1), defaults took Enhanced Tracking Protection from about 20% to about 100% (3.3), and Firefox's 2026 redesign was driven by users unable to predict where an option lives (3.5).
6. **Keep onboarding to four skippable screens; import after first paint with progress, undo and explicit privacy copy.** Most users skip import when asked (4.1), slow import was associated with fewer sessions (4.1), and only 22% of people read privacy policies often or always (4.6).
7. **Make history and bookmarks one searchable store with provenance, and surface bookmarks in context.** Half to four-fifths of browsing is revisitation (5.1), but only 16% of bookmarked targets were reached through the bookmark facility and 4% through the menu hierarchy (5.2).
8. **Anchor downloads to trusted chrome, grade warnings by confidence, resume with Range and state what was checked.** The bubble was chosen for trust separation and richer warnings (6.1), and deep scans flag malware at more than 50× the base rate (6.2).
9. **Keep find in Stage 1 with visible case, diacritics and whole-word options, reveal hidden matches and announce the count.** Firefox took about 17 years to match diacritics (7.4), Safari was reported to skip hidden content (7.5), and a changing count is a WCAG status message (7.6).
10. **Build the toolbar and tab strip to the APG contracts: one toolbar tab stop, roving focus, arrow navigation, Escape to content and focus restored after dialogs and tab close.** Toolbar and tab patterns define these contracts (8.1, 8.2, 8.9), and keyboard access is a top-five screen-reader problem (8.5).
11. **Honor forced colors, reduced motion, 200% text, 320 px reflow and 24×24 px targets; do not encode meaning in colour alone.** These are WCAG 2.2 requirements that apply to chrome through WCAG2ICT (8.3, 8.10–8.14), and colour blindness affects 8% of men (10.4).
12. **Default notifications and other noisy prompts to a quiet chip, require a gesture, and offer one-time grants.** Notifications are 74% of prompts with a 12% desktop grant rate (11.1), about 99% of notification prompts were unaccepted before the gesture requirement (11.2), and one-time grants are now standard (11.3).
