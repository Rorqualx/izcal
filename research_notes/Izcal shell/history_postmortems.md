## 1. The window, the toolbar and tabbed browsing (1993–2006)

| # | Finding | Numbers/dates | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Mosaic made the toolbar the browser.** NCSA describes Mosaic as the first browser to show inline images and as shipping icon buttons for Back, Forward and Home plus bookmarks. It was the first browser used widely by non-programmers. | 5,000 downloads per month in December 1993 | [NCSA Mosaic project page](https://www.ncsa.illinois.edu/research/project-highlights/ncsa-mosaic) | 1993 (page undated) | High |
| 1.2 | **Mosaic 2.0's multiple-window model.** Each document window carried its own navigation controls and its own Navigate, Options and File menus; the stop icon interrupted I/O; the URL of the link under the pointer appeared in a status area; forms were drawn as native Motif widgets. A page was an OS window with its own chrome, not a tab in a container. | Per-window controls, no tab strip | [Mosaic 2.0 help](https://www.w3.org/MarkUp/html-test/ncsa/help-on-version-2.0.html) | v2.0, 1994–95 | High |
| 1.3 | **The cost of the window-per-page model.** Because the window was the unit of navigation, every parallel task required another OS window and another taskbar entry; there was no tab strip or session container that grouped pages into one window. The model survived into the 2000s in browsers that had not adopted tabs. | 1 taskbar entry per page | Mosaic 2.0 help; [IE7 press release](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2006/10/18/microsoft-releases-windows-internet-explorer-7-for-windows-xp) | 1994–2006 | High for the model; Medium for the consequence |
| 1.4 | **Netscape's suite became Mozilla, and the slim browser was a reaction to it.** Netscape released its browser-suite source in 1998 and the Mozilla project started from it. Mozilla 1.0 (2002) still shipped a browser, an email client and other applications in one suite, but by then more than 90% of internet users browsed with Internet Explorer. Phoenix, later Firefox, was released by Mozilla community members the same year with the goal of "the best possible browsing experience to the widest possible set of people"; Firefox 1.0 arrived in 2004 and passed 100 million downloads in under a year. | >90% IE share in 2002; 100M downloads in under a year | [History of the Mozilla Project](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/about/history/) | 1998–2004 | High |
| 1.5 | **IE7 brought tabs to the IE majority.** Microsoft's launch release describes IE7 as "introducing users of Internet Explorer to tabbed browsing": multiple sites in one window, Quick Tabs for switching, and a tab strip in the browser frame. Tabs had been in competing browsers for years; IE's adoption is the point at which they became the default window model for most users. | IE7, 18 October 2006 | [Microsoft press release](https://news.microsoft.com/source/2006/10/18/microsoft-releases-windows-internet-explorer-7-for-windows-xp) | 2006-10-18 | High |
| 1.6 | **Opera's original bet was MDI, not tabs.** Opera began in 1994 at Telenor; its first public release was 2.12. The 14 July 1996 Usenet announcement offered an "MDI user interface and being able to browse in multiple windows", multiple startup windows and a full keyboard interface. MDI put several documents inside one parent window as child panes, a desktop-application pattern rather than a document-window pattern. | 14 July 1996 | [Mark Schenk, Opera history](http://www.markschenk.com/various/ohistory/index.html) | 1996-07-14 | High |
| 1.7 | **Opera moved from MDI to a choice and then to a skinned tabbed UI.** Opera 6 offered either SDI or MDI. Opera 7 rebuilt the whole interface with custom cross-platform skinning and configurable menus and toolbars. Opera 8 emphasised the tabbed interface and added a trash can for closed pages. The MDI model was retired; the configurable, skinned chrome survived as Opera's differentiator. | Opera 6, Opera 7, Opera 8 | [Mark Schenk, Opera history](http://www.markschenk.com/various/ohistory/index.html); [Adam Stiles, tab history](https://ajstiles.wordpress.com/2005/02/11/tabbed_browser_) | 2001–2005 | High; date conflict flagged: Stiles dates Opera tabs to v7 late 2002, Schenk dates the v7 release to early 2003 |
| 1.8 | **Tabs were invented for the browser in 1997–98.** Adam Stiles started SimulBrowse in summer 1997 and published it on 4 January 1998. He has said the idea came from the tabs in the HomeSite HTML editor; each SimulBrowse tab embedded MSHTML, so each tab was an independent live page. Earlier "tabs" in Booklink and GNN were session-history tabs, not independent pages. | Published 4 January 1998 | [Adam Stiles, "The history of tabs"](https://ajstiles.wordpress.com/2005/02/11/tabbed_browser_) | 2005-02-11 (event 1997–98) | High |
| 1.9 | **Mozilla's tabs were a weekend experiment.** Dave Hyatt saw MultiZilla and NetCaptor, wrote a simplified tab implementation for Mozilla in a weekend and checked it in as an experiment; in his account, Mozilla had tabs before Opera did. The implementation was deliberately smaller than the tab extensions it replaced. | 1 weekend | [BuzzFeed News, "Meet the man who invented tabs"](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/josephbernstein/meet-the-man-who-invented-tabs) | undated interview | Medium (journalism quoting Hyatt) |
| 1.10 | **Why tabs won: parallel work without parallel windows.** A log study of millions of users found that 57.4% of tab sessions involve at least one tab switch, a power-law distribution: most sessions use few tabs, a minority use many. Tabs let that parallel browsing happen inside one window and one taskbar entry, which is exactly what the window-per-page model could not do. | 57.4% of tab sessions | [Huang and White, "Parallel Browsing Behavior on the Web"](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/ParallelBrowsing_HT10.pdf) | 2010-06 | High |
| 1.11 | **The cost tabs created: hoarding.** Users keep tabs open as reminders, to avoid re-finding, and from a fear that anything out of sight is gone; closing them takes attention and memory. Most users know some tabs should be closed but cannot let go, and a quarter say too many tabs have crashed their browser or computer. | N=103 survey plus 10 information workers; 55% "can't let go"; ~25% crash; 19% blamed laziness | [Chang et al., "When the Tab Comes Due", CHI '21](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445585); [CMU release](https://www.cmu.edu/news/stories/archives/2021/may/overcoming-tab-overload.html) | 2021-05 | Medium (abstract and excerpts; full PDF blocked) |
| 1.12 | **Firefox 2 made tabs the default behaviour.** Links opened from a page opened in new tabs by default, each tab got its own close button, the tab strip gained scroll arrows, and a recently closed tabs list appeared. Tabs stopped being an advanced option and became the normal way a page opens another page. | Firefox 2, 24 October 2006 | [Firefox 2 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/2.0/releasenotes/) | 2006-10-24 | High |

**Implications:** Tabs are the settled container because they preserve one window and one taskbar entry while allowing parallel work (1.10); window-per-page and MDI were reasonable for their eras but lost (1.2, 1.6). Izcal should keep tabs as the default and treat hoarding as a tab-management problem, not a reason to replace tabs (1.11).

## 2. The address bar, the omnibox and search

| # | Finding | Numbers/dates | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **The status area was the first "where will this click go" chrome.** Mosaic 2.0 displayed the URL of the link under the pointer in a status area outside the document. It was a trusted surface only by convention: the text was drawn by the browser, but nothing marked it as more trustworthy than the page. | — | [Mosaic 2.0 help](https://www.w3.org/MarkUp/html-test/ncsa/help-on-version-2.0.html) | v2.0, 1994–95 | High |
| 2.2 | **Firefox 2 put search and feeds in the chrome.** The integrated search box offered search suggestions. Web feed preview detected feeds and offered Live Bookmarks, with My Yahoo!, Bloglines and Google Reader preloaded as readers. Search and subscription were both browser-chrome features, not page features. | Firefox 2, 2006 | [Firefox 2 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/2.0/releasenotes/) | 2006-10-24 | High |
| 2.3 | **Firefox 3's Smart Location Bar ranked by frequency and recency and learned.** It matched history and bookmarks, adapted to what the user selected, and offered a Star button for bookmarking plus tags. The Library window unified bookmarks, tags and history with backups and Smart Bookmark Folders. The address bar became a search surface over the user's own data, not only a URL field. | Firefox 3, 17 June 2008 | [Firefox 3 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/3.0/releasenotes/) | 2008-06-17 | High |
| 2.4 | **Firefox 4 let the location bar switch to open tabs.** Typing in the Smart Location Bar could search and switch to an already-open tab; stop and reload merged into one button. The bar now covered navigation, search, history and window management in one field. | Firefox 4, 22 March 2011 | [Firefox 4 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/4.0/releasenotes/) | 2011-03-22 | High |
| 2.5 | **Safari 6 removed the separate search field.** The "address and search field" took a Top Hit from history and bookmarks, listed matches by category and started a Google search. It hid the `http://` prefix, greyed everything after the domain, and integrated the Reader button into the right edge of the field. The combined field is now the norm. | Safari 6, July 2012 | [TidBITS, "What's New in Mountain Lion's Safari 6"](https://tidbits.com/2012/07/27/whats-new-in-mountain-lions-safari-6) | 2012-07-27 | High |
| 2.6 | **Chromium's omnibox principles.** Merge the URL and search boxes to reduce cognitive overhead. Keep the dropdown visually minimal because users ignore it most of the time. Keep the default match stable and predictable: synchronous inline autocomplete, and the top item does not reshuffle as asynchronous results arrive. The model is "accelerate my typing", not proactive results; single-word inputs default to search because they are mostly searches. | Qualitative; no published shares | [Chromium omnibox design](https://www.chromium.org/user-experience/omnibox/) | living doc | High |
| 2.7 | **The address bar is a security surface, not just an input.** Chromium's guidance says the registrable domain is the security context; subdomain, userinfo, path, query and fragment are controlled by the site and may be crafted to spoof. It recommends showing the origin at all times, avoiding URL display where the user is making a trust decision, canonicalising, and applying IDN and homograph rules. | — | [Chromium, "Guidelines for URL Display"](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/refs/heads/main/docs/security/url_display_guidelines/url_display_guidelines.md) | living doc | High |
| 2.8 | **Gap: no public vendor data on what the omnibox is used for.** No vendor publishes the share of omnibox navigations that are typed URLs, searches, or history/bookmark suggestions. The design principles in 2.6 are not backed by a published usage split. | — | — | — | Gap |

**Implications:** The omnibox is a trust surface as much as an input (2.7); Izcal should merge URL and search but keep a stable origin visible, and it should not let asynchronous suggestions reshuffle the default match (2.6). Where a trust decision is being made, show the origin and avoid decorative URL styling.

## 3. Toolbars, bookmarks and history managers

| # | Finding | Numbers/dates | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **The icon toolbar and bookmarks were Mosaic's chrome.** Back, Forward, Home and a bookmarks list were the first widely used browser controls outside the page. The toolbar was the model later browsers extended with reload, stop, home, search and bookmark buttons. | 1993 | [NCSA Mosaic project page](https://www.ncsa.illinois.edu/research/project-highlights/ncsa-mosaic) | 1993 | High |

The toolbar became a small action shelf rather than a navigation-only strip. | Safari 6, July 2012 | [TidBITS](https://tidbits.com/2012/07/27/whats-new-in-mountain-lions-safari-6) | 2012-07-27 | High |

| 3.7 | **Few people change settings, so defaults are the product.** A sample of several hundred Word settings files found that "less than 5%" had changed anything. Autosave shipped off by accident, so about 95% ran without it and assumed the default was deliberate. | <5% | [Jared Spool, "Do users change their settings?"](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/) | 2011-09-14 | Medium (informal study, often over-cited) |
| 3.8 | **Power users are the exception.** 92% of WordPerfect users customized something, with a mean of 9.1 changes; customization concentrated in experienced users. The default must serve the majority, but the customization surface must be real for the minority who use it. | 92%; mean 9.1 changes | [Page et al., "User Customization of a Word Processor", CHI '96](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html) | 1996 | High |

**Implications:** Default chrome is the product because few users change it (3.7), while a minority will customize deeply (3.8). Izcal should ship a minimal toolbar and bookmark surface by default and put customization behind a deliberate mode, rather than assuming users will remove what it adds.

## 4. Firefox's chrome: XUL to HTML, Proton and Nova

| # | Finding | Numbers/dates | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | **What XUL was.** XUL is an XML-based language for building cross-platform application interfaces: windows, toolbars, menus, tabbed dialogs and trees. It is Mozilla-specific, has a different box model from HTML, and can be backed by C++. It was the right tool when HTML could not express application chrome. | — | [Firefox Source Docs, "XUL and HTML"](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/browser/components/storybook/docs/README.xul-and-html.stories.html) | living doc | High |
| 4.2 | **Why Mozilla moved away from XUL.** In Mozilla's words, "HTML is powerful enough for us to create almost an entire application with it", so "the need for the features of XUL has diminished". Mozilla now prefers HTML because the model "is better understood by the web and front-end community" and because HTML gains new web features "without backporting them to XUL". Windows, popups and panels still require XUL, and new code should use HTML whenever possible. | — | [Firefox Source Docs, "XUL and HTML"](https://firefox-source-docs.mozilla.org/browser/components/storybook/docs/README.xul-and-html.stories.html) | living doc | High |
| 4.3 | **The XBL replacement project started at about 300 bindings.** Newsletter 1 (2018) said 7 bindings had been removed and 289 remained. Removing the `image` binding alone mattered because it accounted for around 25% of XBL instances created during a mochitest-browser run. | ~300 bindings; 25% of XBL instances from one binding | [XBL Replacement Newsletter 1](https://briangrinstead.com/blog/xbl-replacement-newsletter-1/) | 2018 | High |
| 4.4 | **Newsletter 17 reached zero bindings.** The count went from 300 at project start to 7 in the previous newsletter to 0. Popups became menupopups; panels and arrowpanels became custom elements with Shadow DOM and CSS Shadow Parts; arrowscrollbox, autocomplete and textbox were removed. | 300 → 7 → 0 bindings | [XBL Replacement Newsletter 17](https://briangrinstead.com/blog/xbl-replacement-newsletter-17/) | 2019 | High |
| 4.5 | **Newsletter 20 reached zero XUL files.** About 1,500 files were renamed to `.xhtml`; the burndown went from about 1,400 documents on 2 October 2019 to 0 on 13 December 2019. Remaining work was DTD-to-Fluent localisation, XUL flex and layout frames, and XULElements-to-HTMLElements. The migration removed about 24,000 lines of code net; panel, browser and menu stayed XUL because HTML has no equivalent. | ~1,500 files; 1,400 → 0 documents in 72 days; ~24K LOC net removed | [XBL Replacement Newsletter 20](https://briangrinstead.com/blog/xbl-replacement-newsletter-20/) | 2019-12-13 | High |
| 4.6 | **Firefox 57 Quantum was the break with the old extension model.** It shipped WebExtensions-only, so legacy extensions stopped working. It was over twice as fast as six months earlier, had a redesigned interface and, for new installs, a unified address and search bar. The extension model changed in 2017, but the UI migration continued afterwards: the XBL replacement project ran from 2018 and reached zero XUL files in December 2019. | Firefox 57, 14 November 2017; >2x faster than six months earlier | [Firefox 57 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/57.0/releasenotes/) | 2017-11-14 | High |
| 4.7 | **Firefox 89 Proton simplified the chrome.** It simplified the toolbar and chrome, streamlined menus, updated prompts, introduced floating tabs, reduced interruptions, used native macOS context menus and non-native form controls, and hid the toolbar in full screen. The redesign traded density for calm. | Firefox 89, 1 June 2021 | [Firefox 89 release notes](https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/firefox/89.0/releasenotes/) | 2021-06-01 | High |
| 4.8 | **Proton removed Compact density on an assumption, not on data.** Bug 1693028 says compact is "hard to discover, and we assume gets low engagement". The rationale used a screen-height report: 31.7% of screens at 768 px height and 61.6% above. Proton's tabs and toolbar were 92 px; it measured 26% taller than Quantum and 15% taller than Chromium on Ubuntu. Compact was kept only for existing users behind an `about:config` pref, and telemetry for its usage was added afterwards. | 31.7% at 768 px; 61.6% above; 92 px; 26% taller than Quantum; 15% taller than Chromium | [Bugzilla 1693028](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1693028) | 2021-02 to 2021-04 | High |
| 4.9 | **Nova restored Compact as a first-class option.** It returned as Window Density under Appearance, with an Automatic default. Mozilla's post says "People told us that they missed it, and we listened". The design reached all users in Firefox 157 in late September 2026. | Firefox 157, late September 2026 | [Mozilla, "A new Firefox design"](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/new-firefox-design/); [PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/3247664/firefoxs-biggest-redesign-in-years-just-landed-with-nova.html) | 2026-05-21, updated 2026-09-29 | High for the return; Medium for the version number |
| 4.10 | **Firefox redesigned settings in 2026 by task.** The long-standing General page was retired and split into Appearance, Accessibility, Languages, and Tabs & browsing. Passwords and autofill were reworked, and search was improved. The rationale: crowded pages, related options scattered across sections, and users unable to predict where an option lives. The methods were interviews, usability testing, card sorting, usage-data analysis and Mozilla Connect/Reddit feedback. | Default in Nightly 153 | [Mozilla, "Firefox settings"](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/firefox-settings/); [Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Firefox-Nightly-New-Settings) | 2026-06-16 | High |
| 4.11 | **The 2017 preferences reorg was the earlier attempt.** It cut the number of panes, planned for about five, and added search inside preferences; the section moves were tracked in Bug 1365133 and fixed in Firefox 56. A card sort was run. The 2026 redesign repeats the same problem statement nine years later. | ~5 panes; Firefox 56 | [gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2017/03/07/mozilla-updating-firefox-preferences/); [Bugzilla 1365133](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1365133) | 2017 | Medium |

**Implications:** XUL was the right tool before HTML could express application chrome (4.1), but its maintenance cost and the web platform's growth made HTML and custom elements the better base (4.2–4.5). Izcal's shell HTML/CSS choice is the same direction, with one warning: the remaining XUL-only cases, windows, popups and panels, are exactly the privileged chrome that must stay outside a sandboxed shell renderer. Removing density without measuring it cost Mozilla credibility and was reversed (4.8, 4.9); Izcal should instrument before it removes.

## 5. Chrome's shell: Views, WebUI and the 2023 refresh

| # | Finding | Numbers/dates | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## Biggest lessons

1. **Tabs won because they preserve one window while allowing parallel work.** Keep them the default and solve hoarding as a separate problem (1.10, 1.11).
2. **The omnibox is a trust surface, not only an input.** Keep a stable origin visible and never let suggestions reshuffle the default match (2.6, 2.7).
3. **Defaults are the product.** Fewer than 5% of users change settings, so ship the right default chrome and make customization deliberate (3.7, 3.8).
4. **Do not remove a feature on an assumption.** Proton's Compact density was removed without usage data and restored in Nova after users complained (4.8, 4.9).
5. **A UI toolkit is a long-term tax.** XUL was right for its era, but the migration to HTML and custom elements took years and removed about 24,000 lines net (4.1–4.5).
6. **Keep the trusted frame native and the shell content sandboxed.** Chrome's Views frame plus WebUI content is the working precedent (5.2, 5.4).
7. **Novel organisation is optional chrome until proven.** Before the retreat, more than one Space was used by 5.52% of Arc users, Live Folders by 4.17% and Calendar Preview by 0.4% (7.3, 7.4).
8. **Recovery must not depend on the shell.** Safe/Troubleshoot Mode and session restore are old, durable patterns (8.1–8.4).
9. **Do not rely on a passive security indicator.** 89% of users misread the padlock, and Chrome replaced it with a neutral tune icon (9.1, 9.2).
10. **Keep warnings rare and opinionated.** Design and frequency, not better text, move adherence (9.3–9.7).
11. **Gate prompts on a user gesture and offer one-time grants.** Without a gesture, about 99% of notification prompts were unaccepted (9.8–9.10).
12. **Never put trusted UI below the line of death.** Fullscreen and PWA overlays remove the line entirely, so the trusted region must be native and above the page (9.15–9.17).
