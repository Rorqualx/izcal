# Izcal shell: today's landscape, user pain and unexploited opportunities

Compiled 21 October 2026. This is the phase-2/3 research note for the shell strand: what users complain about in current browser shells, what those shells still do not show, and which gaps Izcal's architecture can close. It is evidence and questions, not a design; the spec decides.

**Method.** Primary sources were preferred: peer-reviewed papers, W3C notes and specifications, regulator reports, vendor posts with data, and issue-tracker prose. No project's source code, headers, commits, diffs or code reviews were read or cited (M-1 part 3). Where only a bug thread or a forum states a fact, it is graded Low and used only to show that the failure mode is reported, never to size it.

**Confidence.** **High** = the primary source was read in this pass, or a project research note quotes its figure verbatim from a primary it read. **Medium** = a vendor claim without effect sizes, a sponsored survey, or a project note's paraphrase. **Low** = secondary coverage, forum or issue prose, or a single unreplicated report.

**Row IDs** are stable so the spec can cite them. Section 5 turns the gaps into opportunities O1–O13 with a falsifier for each; Section 6 ranks the ones most worth closing.

## 1. What users complain about in 2026

### 1.1 Settings, defaults and onboarding

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1.1 | **Almost nobody changes default settings, so the default is the product.** A sample of several hundred Word settings files found "less than 5%" had changed anything; autosave shipped off by accident and about 95% ran without it, assuming the default was deliberate. | <5% changed anything | [Spool, "Do users change their settings?"](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/) | 2011-09-14 | Medium (informal, small sample) |
| 1.1.2 | **The counterpoint: experienced users do customize, heavily.** 92% of WordPerfect users had changed something, mean 9.1 changes. Any "nobody uses settings" claim is a statement about the median, not the tail. | 92%, mean 9.1 changes | [Page et al., CHI '96](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html) | 1996 | High |
| 1.1.3 | **A privacy default, not a setting, moved adoption from a fifth to nearly everyone.** Before Firefox made Enhanced Tracking Protection default-on, about 20% had it on; the default flip took coverage to effectively all users. | ~20% → ~100% | [Mozilla, ETP by default](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/?p=62557) | 2019-09-03 | High |
| 1.1.4 | **Settings sprawl is a named vendor problem.** Firefox retired its catch-all "General" page in June 2026 and split it into Appearance, Accessibility, Languages and Tabs & browsing, after interviews, usability tests, card sorting and usage-data analysis. Mozilla's stated reasons: crowded pages, related options scattered, and users unable to predict where an option lives. | No metrics published | [Mozilla, Firefox settings redesign](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/firefox-settings/) | 2026-06-16 | Medium (primary returned 403; project note graded High) |
| 1.1.5 | **Removing a setting without measuring it backfired.** Mozilla removed Compact density in 2021 because it was "hard to discover" and assumed to get low engagement; usage telemetry was added only afterwards, and Compact returned as a first-class Window Density option in Nova, reaching all users in Firefox 157. | Proton UI 26% taller than Quantum, 15% taller than Chromium; 31.7% of screens at 768 px height | [Bugzilla 1693028](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1693028); [Mozilla, Nova](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/new-firefox-design/) | 2021; restored 2026-09 | High (removal), Medium (version) |
| 1.1.6 | **Import is skipped when it is slow or unexplained.** In Mozilla's first-run import study most users skipped import (they wanted a fresh start or did not know how long it would take); a 1% in-product experiment associated longer import time with fewer subsequent sessions. | 22/67 noticed auto-import unprompted; 57/67 kept data; 14–67% voiced privacy concerns; longer import → fewer sessions, p=0.00127 | [Davidson et al., CHI '18 LBW](https://doi.org/10.1145/3170427.3188542) | 2018-04 | High |
| 1.1.7 | **The default-browser ask is a one-shot decision with no retention payoff.** Moving Firefox's first-run default-browser modal to the second startup showed "no change (positive or negative) to retention"; average daily installs were about 5% higher, which is not retention. Choice-screen timing is order-sensitive: asking at first browser launch instead of device setup raised preset-browser choice from 11% to 19%. | n=12,000 choice-screen experiment; 11% → 19% | [Bugzilla 1143116](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1143116); [Mozilla choice-screen report](https://research.mozilla.org/files/2023/09/Can-browser-choice-screens-be-effective_-Mozilla-experiment-report.pdf) | ~2015–17; 2023-09 | Medium; High |
| 1.1.8 | **No vendor publishes onboarding completion by length, settings-search use, or a browser SUS benchmark.** These are open measurement gaps, not evidence that the current flows work. | none published | [Project note: tabs_onboarding_a11y, gaps](../Shell%20UI%20design/tabs_onboarding_a11y.md) | 2026-10-14 | High (absence checked) |

**Implications:** The shell's settings must be task-organized and every row must state what the default does, because 80–95% will never change it (1.1.1, 1.1.3). Removing or hiding any setting needs its own use measurement first (1.1.5); UI-4, UI-6 and UI-11 are the shell's instruments. Import belongs in onboarding but must be fast, local and undoable (1.1.6), and the default-browser ask must never repeat (1.1.7, R19).

### 1.2 Tabs, sessions and lost work

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.2.1 | **Fear of loss, not laziness, drives tab clutter.** Users keep tabs as reminders and because out-of-sight feels gone; about a quarter say their browser crashed from too many tabs. | N=103 survey; 55% "can't let go"; ~25% crashed; 59% would close some tabs | [Chang et al., CHI '21](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445585) | 2021-05 | Medium |
| 1.2.2 | **Most sessions use one tab; a long tail uses many, and most tabs are barely used.** The most common state is one tab, with a second bump around 16; 45% of tabs were selected exactly once and 25% never. | N=21 over 13–21 days; median 73.3% of tab switches were revisits; Back 7.1% of navigation | [Dubroy & Balakrishnan, CHI 2010](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/chi2010_tabbedbrowsing.pdf) | 2010-04 | High (old, small) |
| 1.2.3 | **Tab-overload features are used by a small minority.** Arc's Spaces reached 5.52% of DAUs, Live Folders 4.17%, Calendar Preview 0.4%; Arc's own letter calls the cause a "novelty tax" — "too different, with too many new things to learn, for too little reward." | 5.52% / 4.17% / 0.4% of DAUs | [The Browser Company, letter to Arc members](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025) | 2025-05-26 | High |
| 1.2.4 | **Session restore fails often enough to be a trust problem, and the failure is silent.** A Chromium issue discussion reports about 1.2–1.3% of browser restorations fail to restore tabs; users see missing tabs with no explanation and no record of what was lost. | ~1.2–1.3% of restorations | [Chromium issue 40196304](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/40196304) | 2021 (M96 discussion) | Medium (issue prose) |
| 1.2.5 | **Every vendor now ships the table-stakes remedies, with no adoption or outcome data.** Chrome added native vertical tabs in April 2026; Edge found users switch between vertical and horizontal layouts and added a persistent toggle; Firefox's tab groups passed 18 million cumulative users. | 18M cumulative (vendor) | [Google, vertical tabs](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/chrome/new-chrome-productivity-features/); [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=728920); [Mozilla, tab groups](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/tab-groups-updates/) | 2026-04-07; 2021-03; 2025-11 | High; High (qualitative); Medium |

**Implications:** The shell must optimize for recoverability, not organization: a searchable recently-closed list, automatic session snapshots that survive a crash, and cold-tab discarding instead of asking the user to close (R18, Stage 2). Vertical tabs and groups are table stakes and must never be forced (UP5). No closed-tab or session-restore feature has published an outcome measure, so the shell should instrument recall and restore success itself.

### 1.3 History and bookmarks

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.3.1 | **Bookmarks are created but not used.** In an elicited personal-information-retrieval study, only 16% of bookmarked targets were retrieved via the bookmark facility, and only 4% through the bookmark menu hierarchy; the rest were visible in the bar. Bookmarked sites were not re-found better than non-bookmarked ones. | 41/250 (16%); 9/250 (4%); 50 participants | [Bergman, Whittaker & Schooler, J. Librarianship & Info. Science](https://doi.org/10.1177/0961000620949652) | 2021 | High |
| 1.3.2 | **Bookmark rot is a real experience but has no public measurement.** No browser publishes bookmark link-rot or deletion rates, and the "users never delete" claim rests on old, small studies. This note treats rot as a qualitative failure whose size must be measured, not assumed. | none published | — (gap; vendor documentation and the bookmark studies above checked) | 2026-10-21 | High (absence checked) |
| 1.3.3 | **History lists fail at recognition and at discovery.** Users struggle to recognise pages from titles and URLs and to find items in long lists; history search exists but has a high discovery cost, so users do not invest in it. | No measured success rate | [Kaasten & Greenberg, MSc thesis](https://grouplab.cpsc.ucalgary.ca/grouplab/uploads/Publications/Publications/2001-Kaasten.MScThesis.pdf) | 2001 | Medium |
| 1.3.4 | **People re-Google instead of opening history.** Formative work on a history tool found users ignored History entirely, preferred re-searching or re-following known links, and consulted history only for pages visited within hours. | Small formative sample | [UIUC Trailblazer, formative evaluation](http://www.acm.uiuc.edu/macwarriors/projects/trailblazer/papers/formative_eval.pdf) | ~2001 | Low |
| 1.3.5 | **No current vendor publishes history-search or bookmark-retrieval outcomes.** Chrome, Firefox and Safari all ship history search and bookmark managers; none publishes a success or usage figure. | none | [Project note: tabs_onboarding_a11y, open gaps](../Shell%20UI%20design/tabs_onboarding_a11y.md) | 2026-10-14 | High (absence checked) |

**Implications:** A bookmark is a promise the browser does not keep: it is saved and rarely surfaced (1.3.1), and how often it rots is unmeasured (1.3.2). The shell should treat history and bookmarks as one store with provenance and health (O7), not two lists. Because history is available yet the least-used path in practice (1.3.3–1.3.4), a better list is not enough; the store must answer "where did this row come from and is it still what I saw?"

### 1.4 Downloads, updates, default-browser asks and other nags

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.4.1 | **Download UI moved into trusted chrome for a reason.** Chrome replaced the bottom shelf with a toolbar-anchored bubble, explicitly to separate trusted browser UI from page content and to leave room for warnings; users can disable auto-open. | No metrics | [Chromium blog, redesigning downloads](https://blog.chromium.org/2023/08/redesigning-chrome-downloads-to-keep.html) | 2023-08-03 | High (qualitative) |
| 1.4.2 | **The download manager can be mislabelled and users cannot tell it opened.** Firefox's Ctrl+J window was titled "Library," which a filed report calls confusing "from a User Interface Design aspect"; support could not tell whether the Downloads dialog was open. | One bug report, not a rate | [Bugzilla 1936387](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1936387) | ~2025 | Low |
| 1.4.3 | **"Downloaded" does not mean "findable."** iOS Safari users reported downloads present only in Safari's list and not in Files, with no statement of where the file actually went. | One forum report, not a rate | [Apple Developer Forums 769229](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/769229) | 2024-12 | Low |
| 1.4.4 | **Download warnings are graded, but their effect is unpublished.** Chrome uses two levels, "Suspicious" and "Dangerous," differentiated by icon, colour and text, and says deep scans flag malware at more than 50× the base rate; it publishes no bypass or heeding rate. | >50× flag rate (vendor) | [Google Security Blog](https://security.googleblog.com/2024/07/building-security-into-redesigned.html) | 2024-07-24 | Medium |
| 1.4.5 | **Update relaunches interrupt work, and the industry is trying to remove them.** Chrome exposes a relaunch-notification policy with a default 7-day period and a forced 2–4 a.m. window; users describe relaunch loops and lost tabs. In July 2026 Google was reported to be testing dynamic patching of child processes and auto-relaunch when no windows are open. | 7-day default; forced 02:00–04:00 window | [Chrome Enterprise policy](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/7679871); [SpidersWeb](https://spidersweb.pl/2026/07/google-chrome-aktualizacje-automatyczne-bez-restartowania-przegladarki.html) | undated; 2026-07 | Medium; Low |
| 1.4.6 | **Default-browser pressure is still a measured complaint, but the evidence is advocacy data.** A survey commissioned by the Browser Choice Alliance (whose members include Chrome, Opera and Vivaldi) found 72% of U.S. Windows users who tried to change their default reported difficulty and 29% said an update reset it; no fieldwork dates or question wording were published. | 2,000 U.S. users; 72% / 29% | [BCA release](https://browserchoicealliance.org/survey-finds-browser-choice-on-windows-undermines-user-expectations/) | 2026-08-12 | Medium (sponsored) |

**Implications:** The shell should make downloads explain themselves end to end — source, scan verdict, what opening will do, the real destination, and a reliable "show in folder" (O8) — because the reported failures are destination and comprehension failures, not missing warnings (1.4.3–1.4.4). Updates should apply without asking when the engine allows, and when a relaunch is unavoidable it must state what will be restored (1.4.5). The default-browser ask is once, through the OS API, with "not now" final (R19, UP3).

### 1.5 Prompts, permissions, notifications and identity

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5.1 | **Notifications dominate prompt volume and are mostly refused.** Notifications were 74% of Chrome's permission prompts, granted 12% of the time on desktop and 23% on Android. A quiet-UI A/B test on 40M+ users cut unnecessary prompt actions by up to 30% with under 5% grant loss. | 74%; 12% / 23%; 40M+ users | [Bilogrevic et al., USENIX Security 2021](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/bilogrevic) | 2021-08 | High |
| 1.5.2 | **Prompts without a gesture are almost never accepted; requiring one changes the ratio.** Firefox showed 1.45 billion prompts in a month and had 23.66 million accepted (about 1:60); after requiring a user gesture, first-time allow rates were 52–56% for new users. | 1.45B prompts; 23.66M accepted; 52–56% after gesture | [Mozilla, restricting notification prompts](https://blog.mozilla.org/futurereleases/2019/11/04/restricting-notification-permission-prompts-in-firefox/) | 2019-11-04 | High |
| 1.5.3 | **Automatic revocation and rate limits are now standard, and the outcome is reported without a baseline.** Chrome revokes notification permission after no recent engagement or repeated suspicious-warning signals and offers restoration in Safety Hub; it reports over 7 billion fewer Android notifications a day in Q1 2026 and caps disruptive domains at 1,000 messages a minute. | >7B/day reduction; 1,000/min cap | [Google Security Blog](https://blog.google/security/the-multi-layered-defenses-that-harden-chrome-against-abusive-notifications/) | 2026-08-11 | High (claims), Low (no baseline) |
| 1.5.4 | **The padlock taught the wrong lesson, and removing it helped.** In a 1,880-person study, 89% held misconceptions about the lock (11% correct); Chrome replaced it with a neutral tune icon in 2023 and reported users opened site controls more, with no confusion. | 89%; 74% read it as "the website is secure"; 51% as "safe to enter data" | [von Zezschwitz et al., IEEE S&P Workshops 2022](https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/); [Chromium blog](https://blog.chromium.org/2023/05/an-update-on-lock-icon.html) | 2022; 2023-05-02 | High |
| 1.5.5 | **Private-browsing wording leaves misconceptions intact or worsens them.** A 460-person, 13-disclosure study found basic local facts understood but sweeping promises harmful; concrete local-only statements did better. | n=460; 20 scenarios; 89.8–99.3% correct on local facts | [Wu et al., WWW 2018](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2018.wwwconf_conference-2018.21/) | 2018-04 | High (qualitative), Medium (numbers) |
| 1.5.6 | **Lookalike domains still defeat users.** Participants identified the real site for 93% of real URLs but only 40% of obfuscated or lookalike URLs, while feeling confident. | 93% real vs 40% lookalike | [Reynolds et al., CHI 2020](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/) | 2020-04 | Medium-High |
| 1.5.7 | **Prompts can be spoofed or abused, and the attacks are current.** Chrome patched UI spoofing in Permissions (CVE-2025-12911) and misrepresentation in `PermissionElement` (CVE-2026-106302); an extension could spoof prompts (CVE-2025-1923). Camera-first phishing asks for camera, microphone, location and device permissions under "fund verification." | Three CVEs; campaign reports | [CVE-2025-12911](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2025-12911); [CVE-2026-106302](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2026-106302); [Netcraft](https://www.netcraft.com/blog/how-camera-first-phishing-turns-payment-verification-into-surveillance) | 2025; 2026 | High (CVE existence), Low (campaign rates) |
| 1.5.8 | **Passkeys beat passwords where they are offered, but enrollment is uneven.** Cross-industry vendor data reports 93% passkey sign-in success vs 63%, 8.5 s vs 31.2 s, and 36% of accounts enrolled; recovery, not the sign-in moment, remains the hard part. | 93% vs 63%; 8.5 s vs 31.2 s; −73% time; 36% enrolled | [FIDO Passkey Index](https://fidoalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/FIDO-Passkey-Index-October-2025.pdf) | 2025-10 | Medium (self-reported) |
| 1.5.9 | **Autofill is origin-bound, which is a phishing defence, but its absence is not explained.** An evaluation of 13 password managers and 30 interviews found origin binding prevents lookalike fills but desktop managers have unsafe defaults (fill without interaction, clickjacking); no field study quantifies phishing prevented. | 13 managers; 30 interviews | [Oesch & Ruoti, USENIX Security 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity20/presentation/oesch) | 2020-08 | High (mechanism), gap (field effect) |

**Implications:** Prompt volume is a solved problem only at the margin; the unresolved problem is comprehension and trust (1.5.4–1.5.7). Izcal's trusted-region prompt (UI-10) makes a prompt unspoofable but does not tell the user *why* it appeared, *what* it protects or *what happened after* — the prompt ledger (O1) and honest labels (UI-3) close that gap. Container and site identity must appear in the prompt itself (1.5.7, R7), and autofill's absence on a lookalike should be a visible cue, not silence (1.5.9).

### 1.6 AI bloat and the choice to refuse

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.6.1 | **Chrome downloaded a ~4 GB model without asking.** On a fresh profile with no user input, Chrome downloaded Gemini Nano weights in 14 m 28 s; eligibility required ~22 GB free and a capable GPU/CPU. | 1.6.3 | **The counter-market is explicit.** Firefox 148 added a master "Block AI enhancements" switch that persists across updates; Brave Leo is inactive until opt-in; Vivaldi ships no LLM features. | Three vendors | [Mozilla, AI controls](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/ai-controls/); [Brave, Leo](https://brave.com/leo/); [Vivaldi](https://vivaldi.com/blog/technology/vivaldi-wont-allow-a-machine-to-lie-to-you/) | 2026-02-24 | High |
| 1.6.4 | **Edge folded Copilot back into default browsing.** Copilot Mode launched in July 2025 as opt-in; Edge 144 (January 2026) let users hide the toolbar icon after backlash; in May 2026 Microsoft retired the separate mode and integrated its features into default Edge. | No adoption data | [gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2026/05/15/microsoft-edge-retires-copilot-mode-and-integrates-ai-features-across-desktop-and-mobile/); [WinBuzzer](https://winbuzzer.com/2026/01/19/new-microsoft-edge-144-allows-copilot-removal-after-sustained-user-backlash-xcxwbn/) | 2026-05-15; 2026-01-19 | Medium |

**Implications:** AI-off by default (B10, R4) is the best-supported shell policy in the 2026 evidence, and it must cover **UI surfaces**, not only processes and files (1.6.2): zero AI entry points, chips, promotions or model downloads with AI off (UI-6). The shell should also measure the upgrade path, because Firefox's lesson is that a kill switch earns trust only when the choice survives updates (1.6.3).

### 1.7 Accessibility, zoom, find and print

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.7.1 | **The web is detectably inaccessible at scale.** 95.9% of the top million homepages had detectable WCAG 2 failures in the February 2026 crawl; low contrast led at 83.9%. **Implications:** Shell accessibility is a ship blocker, not a polish item (UI-1, UI-2), and the engine's semantic plane gives the shell the same tree it gives pages (B8, UP4). Zoom must be a first-class setting with per-site memory (1.7.4–1.7.5); find and print need honest states — what Find skipped, what the printed page will contain — rather than silence (1.7.6).

### 1.8 Sync, switching and portability

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.8.1 | **Habit, not data transfer, is the main switching barrier.** In an Austrian regulator study, 61% named habit, 83% had not switched or considered it in two years, and only 12–13% of those who switched cited transferring logins or bookmarks. | 61%; 83%; 12–13% | [RTR switching-barriers study](https://www.rtr.at/TKP/aktuelles/publikationen/publikationen/Studie_Wechselbarrieren_05_2022.pdf) | 2022-05 | Medium |
| 1.8.2 | **Portability is a patchwork, especially on mobile.** A 2026 review cites a CMA survey in which 16% of UK mobile browser users had switched, and describes desktop import as largely seamless while mobile needs a Mac or PC for Safari exports and passwords. | 16% switched | [DTI, browser data portability](https://dtinit.org/blog/2026/04/14/web-browsers-data-portability-patchwork) | 2026-04-14 | Medium |
| 1.8.3 | **Vendors now compete on migration friction.** Google shipped an on-device Safari-to-Chrome migration wizard (Chrome 145 beta) moving passwords, bookmarks, history and cards. | No effect data | [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-google-tool-safari-chrome/) | 2026-01 | Medium |
| 1.8.4 | **A browser shutdown strands user state, including anything held only in its sync service.** When OpenAI shut Atlas down on 9 August 2026, browser data did not migrate; the replacement was a browser inside ChatGPT plus a cloud browser and a Chrome extension. | Shutdown announced 2026-07-09 | [OpenAI release notes](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/6825453-chatgpt-release-notes); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/09/openai-is-shutting-down-atlas-but-its-ai-browser-ambitions-are-still-growing/) | 2026-08-09 | High |

**Implications:** Import transparency (O9) matters less for winning a switcher than for not losing one later: if habit dominates (1.8.1), the shell must make the first week's data legible and exportable (1.8.2–1.8.4). Sync is absent from the shell design (R16 covers import only); the spec should either promise an open export format and no lock-in, or name sync as out of scope and say so in the product.

**Notes on the five strongest.**

**O1 and O2 are the same idea at two moments.** O1 is the durable record of a decision; O2 is the on-demand explanation of any state. Both depend on Izcal's unusual position that the broker, not the page and not a native widget, decides and draws prompts (4.3). The incumbent trend is toward consolidating site controls and reducing interruption (Chrome 150, 2.3), which does not address comprehension. A ledger is the missing half: it turns a decision the user made in two seconds into something they can inspect later, and it makes "which prompt is real" a checkable fact rather than a visual convention (3.11).

**O3 is the only opportunity that needs a spec amendment.** The shell is a page (4.1), but DT-D2's recorded unit is the tab's browsing context group, not the shell renderer. A shell bug in the settings page, the tab strip or a prompt is therefore outside today's recording. The cost is a larger recorded unit and a redaction surface over history titles, URLs and form values; the falsifier is measured at M4 alongside the existing recording-cost target. If it fails, the fallback is a broker-fact replay ("what the shell was told and when") rather than a pixel replay.

**O5 and O11 are comprehension work, not feature work.** Both fail the same way: users cannot answer a short question about what they are looking at. UI-3 and UI-8 already exist as targets; these opportunities give them product surfaces to test.

**O7 is the opportunity most likely to be underestimated.** The evidence says bookmarks are saved and not used (16%/4%, 1.3.1) and history is ignored in favour of re-searching (1.3.4). A prettier list does not fix that. Provenance and health do: "you saved this in Work; it now redirects to a different article; here is what changed" is a different product from a folder tree.

**What the ranking leaves out.** A command palette (O6) is cheap and plausible, but Arc's 0.4–5.52% usage of its signature surfaces is the best available warning that a new interaction model is not where the pain is (1.2.3); it should be tested, not assumed. Sync is not ranked because the shell design does not yet specify it and the evidence says habit, not lock-in, is the main barrier (1.8.1); the honest move is an open export and a stated scope, not a sync feature built on a guess.

**The cross-cutting finding.** Every top gap is a **provenance** gap: the browser knows who asked, what it decided, what changed and what it will do, and shows the user almost none of it (Section 3). Izcal's architecture already records those facts for its own correctness (Section 4). The shell's distinctive product is therefore not more chrome, and not AI: it is an **accountable browser** — one that can say, on demand and afterwards, why the state on screen is what it is and what the browser did to it.
