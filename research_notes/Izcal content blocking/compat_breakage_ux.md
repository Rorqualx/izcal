# Izcal content blocking: compatibility, breakage and user experience

Compiled 19 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred (papers, specifications, maintainers' posts, release notes, help pages, court press releases). No engine or blocker source code was read, linked or used (M-1 part 3); GitHub pages cited here are wiki prose only.
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only, or recalled and not re-checked.

The shared web-search budget ran out part-way through. Topics researched after that point (sections 3.6–3.8, 4.5, 6.4–6.6, 7, 8.3) rely on primary pages fetched directly or are marked as unchecked gaps.

## 1. Breakage: how often, what kind, and how it is detected

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Breakage taxonomy from user reports.** Qualitative coding of extension-store reviews and GitHub issue threads for ten blocking tools gives **38 breakage types in 7 classes** and **15 mitigation strategies in 4 classes**. The classes, by share of breakage threads: loading and responsiveness about 27%; third-party resources and content (images, video) about 24%; interactions between the tool and the site or other extensions about 16%; HTML elements missing or broken about 9%; authentication. Video is over 10% of threads, login about 4%, extension detection ("disable your ad blocker") about 4%, CAPTCHA under 1%. Comment sections that fail to appear, or third-party comment logins that fail, are named as types | 18,932 reviews and issues; 95-participant survey | [Nisenoff et al., "Defining 'Broken'", USENIX Security 2023](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity23-nisenoff-broken.pdf) | 2023-08 | High |
| 1.2 | **Users rarely report breakage to anyone who can fix it**, and few use the tools' built-in reporting. | 1.6 | **Automatic per-rule breakage prediction is possible but imperfect.** A classifier trained on labels mined from EasyList's history (a rule added, then an exception added for a page) reaches **AUC 0.88**. The number of blocked event registrations was *not* predictive. | 1.8 | **Dynamic and cosmetic breakage are large shares.** 25–44% of user-reported breakage is dynamic (appears only after interaction, e.g. a video that loads but does not play). | 1.9 | SINBAD uses **visual saliency** to choose where to interact with a page, and compares subtrees to find the faulty rule. It reports about 20% better accuracy than the 2022 classifier | — | SINBAD, abstract | 2024-05 | High |
| 1.10 | **Mixed scripts.** Scripts that do both tracking and needed work are on **62.3% of the top 10K sites**; 70.6% of mixed scripts are third-party. | 1.11 | Duumviri adds an ML breakage detector to tracker detection and finds EasyPrivacy rules that are too strict | 97.44% agreement with EasyPrivacy on 15K pages; 74.19% lower bound for mixed trackers | [Duumviri, arXiv 2402.08031](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08031) | 2024-02 | Medium |
| 1.12 | **Tracking protection can reduce perceived breakage.** In a Firefox opt-in study with nine privacy branches, the Tracking Protection branch reported **fewer problems per user than control**; its disable rate was within the margin of error of control. Mozilla's explanation: third-party scripts make pages slow, and blocking them removes that. resistFingerprinting and firstPartyIsolation had significantly more disables. Disable rates across branches ranged from 5.7% to 9.7% | over 19,000 users; over 2,100 per branch | [Mozilla Data, "Improving privacy without breaking the web"](https://blog.mozilla.org/data/2018/01/26/improving-privacy-without-breaking-the-web/) | 2018-01-26 | High |
| 1.13 | In a 1,500-site Tranco crawl of extensions, breakage appears in 49.93% of "critical" store reviews. Adblock Plus was detected on 25 sites and AdGuard on 20 (about 1.3–1.7%), even though Adblock Plus allows "acceptable ads" | 1,500 sites | [Roongta and Greenstadt, ASIA CCS 2024](https://racro.github.io/papers/UserFocusedEvaluation_AsiaCCS.pdf) | 2024-07 | High |

**Implications:** Izcal should count breakage as a measured cost on the same footing as trackers blocked (NW-54). Its corpus check must include **interaction-triggered** and **cosmetic** breakage, not only load-time diffs, because those are half or more of real reports. The categories users care about (page does not load, login, streaming, payment, anti-adblock walls) should be weighted above missing images on news sites. Old breakage reports rot, so the ≤ 7-day† triage target is about keeping reports reproducible as well as about user experience.

**Implications:** Most big blockers converged on three tools: **neutral stand-ins** for blocked scripts, **narrow, time-limited exceptions** for login flows, and **platform APIs** (Storage Access) instead of list exceptions. Izcal's `$redirect` resources should be its own, written from the behaviour a page expects (documented APIs, black-box observation), and published with the evidence for each. Edge's engagement-based relaxation is a hidden, history-dependent input; if Izcal uses anything like it, it must be a recorded B1 input and visible in the site panel. Brave's first-party retreat is evidence that **default scope** (third-party only) matters more for breakage than list quality.

**Implications:** Whatever Izcal ships as the default is what nearly everyone will run, so the default must be the protection level Izcal is willing to defend on breakage. A **per-site off switch is the only control most users will touch**, and they use it on trusted, familiar sites. That makes "off for this site" a high-value moment to collect a consented breakage report and to offer a narrower fix ("allow only this login" rather than "off").

**Implications:** The NW-6.15 decision (send only URL and rules fired) is stricter than Firefox and Brave, which also send the protection configuration. The configuration matters for triage, so Izcal should send a **coarse, enumerated settings vector** (level, which built-in lists, per-site overrides), never custom list URLs, and should strip query and fragment as both vendors do. Reports should go through the OHTTP relay (Q-NW-6). Closing the loop ("fixed in list version N") would answer the most common complaint, and publishing median triage time would make Izcal the first to do so.

**Implications:** Stage 1 already decides "never auto-accept" and Izcal's own rule format. The evidence supports **hide-first** (no interaction with a CMP that may itself track) as the default, with **reject-through-CMP** only where hiding leaves the site unusable or where a legal regime gives a refusal signal weight. Either way, the hide action must undo scroll-lock, `inert` and focus traps (see section 8). Generic banner rules are a known source of breakage; site-specific rules with crawl evidence are safer. Anti-adblock walls are a legitimate publisher response under at least German law, so Izcal should not promise to defeat them by default.

**Implications:** There is **no public measurement of challenge rates caused by content blocking itself**, as distinct from IP reputation. PRIV-1 therefore needs Izcal's own black-box harness: the same pages, IP and session, with defaults on and off, counting challenges per 1,000 navigations by provider. Supporting Privacy Pass tokens (RFC 9576–9578) is the one known way to cut challenges without weakening blocking, and would need its own privacy review.

Izcal's `FailureReason::ContentBlocked` should carry, in DevTools only (not page-visible, NW-22), the list version, the rule and a link to its published evidence and contest process. Edge's note shows that raw request counts are a **misleading** headline metric; the shell's "Blocked here: 23 trackers" line (Shell UI 4.3) should count **distinct tracker organisations or scripts prevented**, not requests, and should say what a number means.

## 8. Accessibility and cosmetic filtering

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | CSS-hiding rules cause about 53% of rule-attributed breakage in SINBAD's data, so cosmetic filtering is a major breakage source in its own right | ≈ 53% | SINBAD (1.8) | 2024-05 | High |
| 8.2 | Hiding a consent notice can leave the page scroll-locked or covered by an overlay; Brave's banner blocking explicitly modifies pages to remove overlays and scroll-blocking | — | Brave 2022 (5.5) | 2022-09-28 | High |
| 8.3 | **No published report or study was found on accessibility harm from cosmetic filtering** (e.g. modals hidden while `aria-modal`, `inert` or a focus trap stays active, or skip links hidden). This is unverified, not evidence of absence | — | — | — | Gap |

**Implications:** By first principles, `display: none` from a user-origin sheet removes an element from the accessibility tree too, so a hidden ad is consistent for screen-reader and sighted users. The risk is **side effects left behind**: `inert` or `aria-hidden` on the rest of the page, focus traps, and `overflow: hidden` on the body. Izcal can check these mechanically after cosmetic rules apply (focusable content reachable; no `inert` ancestor of the main content). That is a cheap breakage test no other blocker publishes.

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10.1 | **Chrome heavy ad intervention** (Chrome 85) unloads an ad frame the user has not interacted with if it uses > 4 MB of network data, > 15 s of main-thread CPU in any 30 s window, or > 60 s of CPU in total, and shows "Ad removed" with a "Details" link | thresholds | [Chrome Developers, "Heavy ad interventions"](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/heavy-ad-interventions) | 2020-05-14 | High |

**Implications:** Behaviour-based limits remove a large share of ad cost with almost no false positives, because they act on measured behaviour and not on a list. They fit FP-D7 (interventions on sites where blocking is off). Izcal's equivalent should be a recorded B1 input, show a typed reason in DevTools, and publish its thresholds and hit rate per release.

## Design ideas for breakage handling and UX (ideas only)

1. **Breakage as a first-class metric per rule.** Every list entry ships with its corpus breakage result (load-time and interaction-triggered, including cosmetic) next to its tracking evidence. A rule without a breakage run does not ship.
2. **Saliency-driven interaction in the crawler.** Izcal's crawler is Izcal itself, so it can drive the same interactions (play video, open login, submit form, open comments) with and without each candidate rule and compare engine-level state (frames, network, layout, accessibility tree), not just screenshots.
3. **Graduated per-site controls**, not just on/off: "allow this login only, for this tab" (narrow, time-limited, like Brave's Google Sign-In permission), "allow embeds of type X" (like SmartBlock Embeds), then "off for this site". Each override is a recorded B1 input and is shown in the site panel.
4. **Turning protection off is a reporting moment.** When a user turns protection off for a site, offer a one-click report showing exactly what would be sent (URL without query or fragment, list version, enumerated settings, rules fired), through the OHTTP relay. After a fix ships, tell the user in the site panel ("fixed in list version N; protection can be turned back on").
5. **Izcal-written neutral resources and surrogates**, each documented with the page-visible behaviour it preserves and the black-box observations behind it; published like list entries.
6. **Hide-first banner handling** with mandatory cleanup (scroll-lock, overlay, `inert`, focus trap), checked automatically after the rule applies.
7. **Typed reasons in DevTools**: "blocked by Izcal list L v123, entry E, evidence link, contest link", plus a "show what Izcal changed on this page" view that covers network blocks, redirects to neutral resources, cosmetic hides and interventions in one timeline. Never page-visible.
8. **Honest counters**: count distinct tracker organisations or scripts prevented, explain the number on hover, and never present a falling count as weaker protection.
9. **Published operating numbers per release**: corpus breakage rate, report volume, median and p90 triage time, share of reports that were not Izcal's fault, CAPTCHA challenge rate with defaults on and off.
10. **Site-operator self-test**: a documented way to load a site in Izcal with a given list version and get the typed-reason log, so operators can check before contesting.

## Questions for measurement

1. What is the corpus breakage rate of Izcal's defaults when breakage includes interaction-triggered and cosmetic cases, and how much does it differ from a load-time-only check?
2. Which breakage categories (load failure, login/SSO, payment, video, comments, maps, CAPTCHA, consent manager, analytics-dependent navigation) dominate on Izcal's corpus, weighted by how much users care (Nisenoff et al.'s ranking)?
3. How often do users turn protection off per site, on which kinds of sites, and how often do they turn it back on? (Opt-in, aggregated, privacy-preserving telemetry only, or a lab study.)
4. What share of breakage is fixed by a neutral resource, a narrow exception, or a list change, and what does each cost in privacy?
5. What is the CAPTCHA and challenge rate per 1,000 navigations, by provider, with Izcal defaults on and off, from the same IP and session state (PRIV-1)? Does Privacy Pass support change it?
6. What share of reports are reproducible after 7, 30 and 90 days, and does the ≤ 7-day† triage target keep most of them reproducible?
7. Do users understand the site panel's counter? A small comprehension study comparing request counts, organisation counts and a qualitative label.
8. Do cosmetic rules on Izcal's corpus leave focus traps, `inert` ancestors or scroll-lock behind, and how often?
9. How many sites detect Izcal's blocking and show a wall, and do they detect it through the blocked request alone, which NW-22 says must not be page-distinguishable from a network error?
10. Which Izcal resource interventions (heavy-ad style) fire, how often, and with what false-positive rate on sites where blocking is off?
