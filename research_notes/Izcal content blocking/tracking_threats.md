# Izcal content blocking: tracking threats

Compiled 19 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred: papers, specifications, maintainers' posts, release notes and standards discussion. Under M-1 part 3, no engine or blocker source code was read, linked or cited. Where a value is stated only in source, it appears under "Questions for black-box measurement".
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text (for papers, the full text was extracted and searched); **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only, or not checked this session.

The shared web-search budget ran out partway through this strand. The priority topics were checked first: bounce tracking and UID smuggling, CNAME cloaking and server-side tracking, behavioural fingerprinting detection, filter-list fingerprinting, and the robustness of ML classifiers. Their findings are mostly High. After the budget ran out, only known primary pages were read directly. Items in sections 6–8 that could not be checked are marked Medium or Low.

## 1. Third-party trackers, cookie syncing and identifier-like values

**Implications:** A few hundred parties account for nearly all cross-site reach, so a reviewed, evidence-backed list is feasible (NW-D26). Izcal's crawler has to visit internal pages and keep state, or it will undercount. With partitioning, cookie syncing stops being the main way to join identities, and identifiers move into URLs, first-party cookies and fingerprints (sections 3–5). Izcal's behavioural tracker definition should reuse the published ID-detection heuristics (1.4) as ideas, and its own measurement should record which heuristic flagged each value.

**Implications:** Network blocking cannot be Izcal's main defence against fingerprinting. About half of fingerprinting is first-party-served, and EasyList-based blockers removed about 5% of it. The defence has to sit where values leave the engine (D-S1 and its amendments): seeded noise and normalization at readback, plus targeted per-script restrictions where behaviour-based attribution identifies a fingerprinting script. FP-Inspector found these restrictions halved breakage. Izcal's engine can attribute every API call to a script and stack (the frame's crawler role), which is the input a behavioural detector needs. The API inventory in 2.5 belongs in PRIV-2's surface list. Bot-detection fingerprinting (2.10) means PRIV-1's CAPTCHA-cost measurement has to cover any fingerprinting countermeasure, not only blocking.

## 3. Bounce tracking, UID smuggling and link decoration

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | **UID smuggling** (Randall et al., CrumbCruncher): an originator decorates the link with a UID, zero or more redirectors store it as a first-party value, and the destination receives it. Four coordinated crawlers (Safari-1, Safari-1R, Safari-2, Chrome-3) separate UIDs from session IDs. Random walks of 10 steps from each of the Tranco top 10K seed domains | UID smuggling on **8.11%** of unique URL paths; UID smuggling plus bounce tracking on **10.8%** (8.1% plus 2.7%). 13% of smuggling originates on sites that fingerprint | [Randall et al., "Measuring UID Smuggling in the Wild", IMC 2022](https://brave.com/research/files/measuring-uid-smuggling-in-the-wild.pdf) | 2022-10 | High |
| 3.2 | Redirectors divide into dedicated smugglers (redirectors with no other purpose) and multi-purpose smugglers. **Lists lag behind** | Of the 30 most common redirectors, 16 are dedicated (14 owned by advertisers); DoubleClick appears in more than 20% of smuggling cases. **41% (11 of 27) dedicated smugglers were missing from the Disconnect list**. 16% of UIDs have a lifetime under 90 days | Same, §4 | 2022 | High |
| 3.3 | The arXiv version gives the method's organization counts | 214 link-decoration domains (≥ 104 organizations); 23 bounce-tracking domains (≥ 16 organizations); "a little over 10%" of navigations | [arXiv 2203.10188 v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10188) | 2022-07-12 | High |
| 3.4 | Defences compared in 2022: **Safari** uses heuristics (deletes redirector storage unless the user interacts with it as a first party; the classification propagates along paths). **Firefox** uses the Disconnect list (clears storage after 24 h unless the site was visited as first party in the last 45 days). **Brave** skips the bounce when the destination is in a query parameter, strips listed parameters, keeps a list of smuggling URLs, offers an optional interstitial, and clears storage when the tab closes. **Chrome**: no defence in 2022 | — | Same, §6.1 | 2022 | High |
| 3.5 | **Privacy CG Navigational-Tracking Mitigations**: a Draft CG Report with no consensus on the algorithm. It defines link decoration, navigational tracking and bounce tracking. Its algorithm assumes third-party cookies are blocked, records "extended navigations", and marks a host as a bounce tracker if it redirects, writes cookies or activates a service worker without recent user activation. Suggested values: client bounce detection about 10 s, grace period about 1 h, timer period about 1 h, activation lifetime about 45 days. WebAuthn assertions count as activation. Deletion timing should be fuzzed because it leaks interaction state | — | [Privacy CG, Navigational-Tracking Mitigations](https://privacycg.github.io/nav-tracking-mitigations/) | 2025-03-25 | High |
| 3.6 | Chrome's bounce tracking mitigations have been on by default since October 2023, **only for users who block third-party cookies** (deletion if there was no interaction within 45 days). MDN dates the implementation to Chromium 116. **Firefox**: bounce tracking protection in ETP Strict, stateless (all bounces treated as stateful) since version 145; its list-free redirect tracking protection remains. **Safari** does not implement the CG algorithm; it has its own protection, first shipped in ITP 2.0 | — | [Chrome bounce tracking mitigations](https://privacysandbox.google.com/protections/bounce-tracking-mitigations) (updated 2025-10-27); [MDN, Bounce tracking mitigations](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Privacy/Guides/Bounce_tracking_mitigations) (modified 2026-01-05) | 2025–2026 | High |
| 3.7 | **Link decoration and query-parameter stripping**: Safari caps cookies to 24 h on a destination reached from a classified tracker with query or fragment decoration. Safari 17 blocks known tracking query parameters in Private Browsing (the list is not published). Firefox strips listed parameters (Nightly default from 2022, then Strict and Private Browsing). Brave strips listed parameters | — | [Privacy CG spec §5](https://privacycg.github.io/nav-tracking-mitigations/); [WebKit, Safari 17.0 features](https://webkit.org/blog/14445/webkit-features-in-safari-17-0/) | 2023–2025 | High (Safari 17 list contents unknown) |
| 3.8 | First-party tracking now relies heavily on **parameter patterns**: Böttger et al. derive 181 rules from query-parameter key sets | The rules match 63% of a validation set that EasyList and EasyPrivacy match 0% of; 3% false positives on non-tracking URLs; 1 of 2,300 sites visibly broken | [Böttger et al. 2026, §5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16720) | 2026-06-15 | High |

**Implications:** Bounce tracking and UID smuggling are **navigation-layer** threats. A request matcher alone does not see them. Izcal needs a navigation-chain recorder in the browser process (originator, redirectors, destination, activation), with deletion bound to the partition key (NW-D7). Partitioning is what makes this a threat. The CG draft is written for browsers that block all third-party cookies and partition third-party cookies and storage; Izcal partitions both but does not block partitioned third-party cookies, so it meets two of the three conditions and applies the algorithm to first-party state, recording the deviation (corrected after the phase 8 fact-check, FC-M3). Lists lag behind (41% missing in 2022), so Izcal should combine a behavioural classifier fed by its own crawler with a published, evidence-backed parameter list (`$removeparam` in the 6.15 syntax). Deletion timing must be fuzzed so it does not become a cross-site signal (PRIV-2), and every deletion is a recorded B1 input.

**Implications:** Izcal controls its own resolver (`hg-dns`), so CNAME uncloaking costs it nothing extra (NW-D26). The chain must be matched at every hop, and IP-range evidence (the "A record" evasion) has to be a first-class, reviewed rule type. CNAME uncloaking does not stop a reverse proxy or server-side tagging. Against those, the only levers are request **shape** (paths, query-parameter key sets: 3.8), **script behaviour** (what a first-party script reads and sends), and **partitioning and cookie policy**: cap the lifetime of cookies set by responses whose resolved IP or CNAME is third-party, as Safari does. Server-side forwarding happens off-device and cannot be blocked. Izcal can only make it visible ("who received what", 6.19) and reduce what the first-party request carries.

## 5. First-party trackers and ad scripts served first-party

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Fingerprinting is often first-party-served or bundled into the site's own JavaScript (single-page apps), which defeats URL and DNS defences | 49–52% of canvas-fingerprinting sites | [Luo et al., IMC 2025](https://www.sysnet.ucsd.edu/~voelker/pubs/canvas-imc25.pdf) | 2025 | High |

| 5.3 | First-party analytics cookies (`_ga`, `_gcl_au`, `_fbp`) are set by third-party scripts through `document.cookie` and are readable by every same-site party. **Implications:** The unit of decision has to be the **script**, not only the request. The script is identified by its URL and content hash and by what it does (attributed API calls, storage writes, outbound data). This matches FP-D7, which already does script-attributed interventions. Izcal should consider an ITP-like lifetime cap on script-set first-party cookies as a class (c) protection, with a recorded P1 analysis and a published compatibility cost.

**Implications:** For Izcal, filter-list fingerprinting is a **design constraint**, not a footnote. Success criterion 5 in the frame says the user's list set must not be observable cross-site. That argues for:
- a small number of default list configurations per container, so users sit in large anonymity sets;
- user-added lists shown in the UI as a fingerprinting cost;
- cosmetic hiding whose effect is not visible through fetch timing. The engine controls lazy-image and background-image fetch behaviour and `display` computation, so it can hide a cosmetically blocked element without changing the page's network behaviour;
- treating blocked-endpoint `<img>` probes as a PRIV-2 surface.

Blocked requests already fail as indistinguishable network errors (NW-22). Izcal's matcher sees every channel (WebSocket, WebTransport, service-worker fetches, WebRTC signalling over Fetch) in the network service. That closes the extension-API gaps. Silent anti-adblock (8.3) is invisible breakage and has to be measured, not left to user reports.

**Implications:** Content features (URL strings) are what adversaries control, so classifiers built on them fail. Flow features (who stores which ID, who sends it where, redirect chains) are robust because changing them costs the tracker its function. Izcal's crawler can compute those features natively from its own attributed graph. They belong in the **list-building pipeline**, offline, with human review and published evidence (NW-54). They do not belong in an on-device classifier: AG-0 and B10 allow on-device classifiers only through the ML inventory. Labels must come from Izcal's own behavioural definition, not from GPL or CC BY-SA lists (OD-L5).

**Implications:** No standard governs content blocking itself. Fetch, the CG navigation-tracking draft, Storage Access and CHIPS govern the partitioning and navigation layers Izcal relies on. Izcal should send GPC and set `navigator.globalPrivacyControl` uniformly per container default, so the signal is not an extra bit (PRIV-2). It should not implement RWS, Topics or Protected Audience: they were retired by their own sponsor. Chrome's market share still means most of the web assumes third-party cookies work, so partitioning breakage remains Izcal's compatibility cost to measure.

## Threat-model table

Layers: **N** = network request matcher (URL, type, party); **D** = DNS/CNAME and IP uncloaking (`hg-dns`); **C** = cosmetic (user-origin sheet); **S** = script/API-level (attribution, noise, targeted restriction, budgets); **P** = partitioning and cookie policy; **V** = navigation (redirect chains, parameter stripping, storage deletion).

| Threat | Mechanism | Layers that can defend (primary first) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party cookie tracking | Third-party cookies and storage keyed by the tracker | P, N | 1.1–1.5; partitioning removes the shared jar |
| Cookie syncing | Third-party IDs exchanged in URLs between trackers | P, N, S | 1.3, 1.7 |
| Fingerprinting (third-party script) | Readback of canvas, audio, WebGL, timing, sensors | S, N | 2.3–2.8; lists remove about 5% in practice |
| Fingerprinting (first-party or bundled) | Same, from a site-served or bundled script | S only | 2.8, 5.1 (49–52% first-party) |
| Bot-detection fingerprinting | Same; site depends on it to work | S (noise that preserves the bot signal), never N by default | 2.10; PRIV-1 CAPTCHA cost |
| Bounce tracking | Top-level redirect through a tracker that writes first-party state | V, P | 3.1–3.6 (2.7% of paths) |
| UID smuggling and link decoration | ID in the query string carried across sites | V (strip and skip), P | 3.1–3.3, 3.7, 3.8 (8.1% of paths; 41% of smugglers missing from lists) |
| CNAME cloaking | Publisher subdomain CNAMEs to the tracker | D, P (cookie cap), N | 4.1–4.5 (9.98% of top 10K; leaks on 95%) |
| A-record or NS cloaking | Subdomain points at tracker IPs | D (IP evidence), P | 4.4, 4.5 |
| Reverse-proxy and server-side tagging | Tracker behind a first-party path; forwarding off-device | N (path and parameter shape), S, P (limit what first-party requests carry) | 4.4, 4.6, 4.7 (> 54% FPT/SST; lists match 27.5%) |
| First-party cookie leaks to same-site trackers | `document.cookie` cookies readable by any same-site script or endpoint | P (lifetime caps), S | 4.2, 5.3, 5.4 |
| Malvertising: forced redirects | Ad frame navigates the top level without user activation | V, N (ads list), S | 6.1, 6.3 (81% of malicious ads, vendor data) |
| Malvertising: malicious creatives and exploits | Exploit or deceptive download from the ad chain | N, process isolation, Safe-Browsing-class checks | 6.1, 6.2 |
| Cryptojacking | Wasm hash loop and WebSocket Stratum, throttled, randomized URLs | S (behaviour, CPU budget), N | 7.1–7.3 |
| Anti-adblock (visible or silent) | Bait elements and requests, then a wall or quiet reporting | C (hide without a side channel), S, N (exception lists) | 8.1–8.3 (6.7–30.5%) |
| Filter-list fingerprinting | Scriptless probes of cosmetic and network rules | C (side-channel-free hiding), N (uniform list sets), PRIV-2 | 8.4, 8.5 (84% of lists, median 48 users) |
| Alternative channels | WebSocket, WebRTC, WebTransport, service worker | N (all channels in the network service), V (SW activation as a bounce signal) | 8.7 |
| Ad reinsertion and circumvention | First-party re-serving, rotating paths, obfuscated creatives | C, S, N | 8.8 (Low) |
| Classifier evasion | URL and content mutation; first-party collusion | Flow-feature classification offline (crawler) feeding reviewed lists | 9.2–9.4 |

## Questions for black-box measurement

1. **Safari's tracking-parameter list (Private Browsing)**: which parameters does Safari 17+ strip? Measure with a corpus of decorated links. The list is not published.
2. **Firefox's stripped-parameter set and its stateless bounce tracking timing (v145+)**: measure deletion delay and which bounces it catches, to calibrate Izcal's grace and fuzz values against the CG draft's suggested about 1 h.
3. **Chrome's bounce tracking deletion delay** with third-party cookies blocked: "shortly after the next redirect" is not quantified in prose.
4. **Safari's CNAME and IP-cloaking cookie cap**: is the 7-day cap applied when the response's IP, not only its CNAME, is third-party? Measure with an A-record setup.
5. **Brave's and Firefox's CNAME uncloaking depth and caching**: how many hops, and does it follow NS delegation? Black-box with controlled DNS.
6. **Filter-list fingerprinting resistance** of current blockers and browsers: replay the "Double-Edged Shield" scriptless probes (CSS animation, `<img>`, container queries) against Brave, Firefox and Safari built-in protections, and later against Izcal's cosmetic engine.
7. **Fingerprint-noise behaviour**: does Safari 26 or Firefox add noise to canvas, audio or WebGL readback in normal windows? Measure stability per site and per session with the IMC 2025 test canvases (an input corpus, not code).
8. **Server-side GTM detectability**: for a sample of sites, does a first-party collect endpoint carry Google Analytics or Measurement Protocol-shaped query keys (Böttger-style)? Measure Izcal's request-shape rules for recall and breakage on a held-out crawl.
9. **Silent anti-adblock prevalence in 2026**: repeat the differential-execution idea with Izcal's own recorder (blocked vs not blocked) on the top 10K. The last number (30.5%) is from 2018.
10. **Malvertising forced-redirect rate**: the share of top-level navigations initiated by cross-origin ad frames without user activation, measured on an Izcal crawl. Vendor numbers have no published method.
11. **Cryptojacking in 2026**: sustained Wasm hashing plus WebSocket mining protocols on a 1M crawl. No post-2020 measurement was found.
12. **Breakage from blocking first-party fingerprinting by bot-detection vendors** (Akamai, PerimeterX): CAPTCHA and denial rates under Izcal's noise compared with targeted restriction (PRIV-1).
