# Izcal phishing protection: warning and interstitial UX

Compiled 21 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred: peer-reviewed papers, specifications, vendor security write-ups and support documentation. No third-party source code was read or cited.
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only.

Scope: the user-facing design of a phishing and URL-reputation system: interstitial effectiveness, warning content, lookalike/IDN display, post-bypass state, downloads, reporting, agents, accessibility and transparency.

| 1.12 | Users rationally reject security advice when its lifetime cost exceeds expected loss. **Implications:** Design for adherence, not comprehension: a full-page interstitial with a prominent safe action and a hidden bypass is the only intervention with field evidence of a large effect (37%→62%). Do not spend the budget on clever copy; spend it on layout, false-positive reduction and context. Keep a per-navigation, non-persistent bypass model as the baseline and measure whether repeated exposure habituates in Izcal's own field data.

## 2. What makes a warning work, and what does not

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | **Opinionated design** is the strongest documented lever: safe action as a primary button, unsafe action as a subdued text link, risk colour and an explicit heading. | +~30 percentage points adherence; nearly 30% more users chose safety. The safe button used the same blue primary style as other Google products; the unsafe path was dark-gray text. | Felt et al., CHI 2015. [paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/pub-tools-public-publication-data/pdf/43265.pdf) | 2015-04 | High |
| 2.2 | **Hiding the bypass** behind an "Advanced" disclosure works, but adds modestly and can hide the legitimate false-positive path. | Additional hurdles improve adherence by **2–15%**, depending on complexity. Chrome's malware warning hid "proceed" behind "Advanced"; Firefox's SSL warning required four clicks. | Felt et al., CHI 2015. [paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/pub-tools-public-publication-data/pdf/43265.pdf) | 2015-04 | High |
| 2.3 | **Specific risk text** helps only at the margin. Naming concrete data types ("passwords, messages, or credit cards") improved threat-source understanding but not the decision much. | Threat-source correct: proposal **49.2%** vs Chrome 36 **37.7%**. Text-only condition gained **1.2 points** adherence. | Felt et al., CHI 2015. [paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/pub-tools-public-publication-data/pdf/43265.pdf) | 2015-04 | High |
| 2.4 | **Plain, brief, non-technical language** is recommended but hits contradictory constraints: specificity fights brevity, and no tested wording produced high comprehension. | All five browser texts left overall comprehension low; the authors explicitly question existing warning-comprehension guidance. | Felt et al., CHI 2015. [paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/pub-tools-public-publication-data/pdf/43265.pdf) | 2015-04 | High |
| 2.5 | **Showing the URL** in the warning is necessary but not sufficient: users cannot reliably parse lookalikes. | Correct identification: **93%** of real URLs, **40%** of obfuscated/lookalike URLs, **58%** average across the sample. | Reynolds et al., "Measuring Identity Confusion with URLs", CHI 2020. [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/); [DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3313831.3376298) | 2020-04-21 | Medium |
| 2.6 | **Explaining the risk** matters because risk perception predicts heeding, especially for domain mismatch. It is not a substitute for design. | Respondents who understood domain-mismatch risk were more likely to heed. The single-page warning with explicit risk and black/red styling was most effective at the bank (**45%** bypass). | Sunshine et al. 2009. [paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec09/tech/full_papers/sunshine.pdf) | 2009-08 | High |
| 2.7 | **One primary action** beats multi-step questioning. Asking users "what type of site are you trying to reach?" failed and added friction. | Multi-page warning **60%** bank bypass vs single-page **45%**. Only **15 of 20** participants answered the context question correctly; some gamed it to reach the site. | Sunshine et al. 2009. [paper](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec09/tech/full_papers/sunshine.pdf) | 2009-08 | High |
| 2.8 | **Visual risk signalling** works, but colour alone is unreliable and the exact palette matters. Yellow performed worse than gray. | Gray proposal **58.3%** adherence vs yellow **53.3%** (5 points worse). Earlier work cited by the authors found red warnings drew attention faster than black or green. | Felt et al., CHI 2015. [paper](https://storage.googleapis.com/pub-tools-public-publication-data/pdf/43265.pdf) | 2015-04 | High |
| 2.9 | **Warning volume is a design parameter.** Most SSL warnings are false positives, which trains bypass; keeping warnings rare is part of effectiveness, not a UX nicety. | **20%** of Chrome SSL impressions were non-bypassable vs **1%** of Firefox's, partly due to pinned/HSTS sites. Herley's cost argument implies every low-value warning taxes the user. | Akhawe & Felt 2013; [Herley 2009](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1719030.1719050) | 2013-08; 2009-09-08 | High / Medium |

**Implications:** Izcal should ship one primary safe action, put the bypass behind a disclosure, state the concrete data at risk, show the full URL, and use a restrained risk palette (not yellow). Do not rely on better wording to fix comprehension, and treat every additional warning class as a tax on the credibility of the rest. **Not established:** controlled evidence that countdown timers, fear appeals or "dark-pattern" bypass links improve adherence; no study found. Countdowns also conflict with WCAG 2.2.1 (see §8).

## 3. Lookalike and IDN warnings, and URL display

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Users identify lookalike URLs poorly and are over-confident. The "40%" figure refers to obfuscated/lookalike URLs, not to real sites. | **93%** correct on real URLs; **40%** on lookalike URLs; **58%** average. | Reynolds et al., CHI 2020. [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/); [DOI](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3313831.3376298) | 2020-04-21 | Medium |
| 3.2 | Users' URL strategies are the failure: scanning for familiar names anywhere in the string, trusting all HTTPS links, and trusting the word "secure". | Qualitative strategies across the CHI 2020 sample; no single strategy was reliable on obfuscated URLs. | Reynolds et al., CHI 2020. [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/) | 2020-04-21 | Medium |
| 3.3 | Chrome uses per-label IDN display: Unicode normally, punycode on conversion errors, disallowed characters, BiDi violations, mixed scripts, multiple numbering systems, invisible characters, confusable skeletons, digit-only lookalikes, and skeleton matches to top domains. The stated rationale is to avoid over-punycoding, which harms non-Latin users. | Example rules: Latin/Cyrillic/Greek cannot mix; an all-Cyrillic "apple" under `.com` is shown as punycode; `googlé` vs `google` skeleton match triggers punycode. | Chromium IDN policy (prose doc). [idn.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 3.4 | Chrome's lookalike warning is a local, on-device interstitial for main-frame navigations. It blocks skeleton matches against recently engaged sites or top domains and has explicit exemptions. | Blocks against a **top 500** domain list and recently engaged sites; allows non-HTTP(S), safe redirect chains, medium+ site engagement, `domains.list`, and previously ignored hosts. Defensive registrations can trigger it; a single 301/302 to the canonical domain avoids it. | Chromium lookalike policy (prose doc). [idn.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 3.5 | Lookalike bypass is deliberately ephemeral and local: the ignore is stored per tab, not as a durable site exception. | "hosts the user previously ignored (stored per tab)". | Chromium lookalike policy (prose doc). [idn.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 3.6 | **Domain highlighting does not fix identity confusion.** Fourteen URL/EV formats, including domain-emphasis variants, produced no statistically significant improvement; removing the EV indicator changed almost no behaviour. | 1 field experiment plus 3 survey experiments; **14** format iterations; no significant improvement in login-page identity understanding. EV removal field test was "orders of magnitude" larger than prior work, with only one significant origin after correction (per prior note). | Thompson et al., "The Web's Identity Crisis", USENIX Security 2019. [paper page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/thompson) | 2019-08 | Medium |
| 3.7 | Showing only the registrable domain is not a reliable anti-phishing intervention. It made no significant difference vs the full URL, while Chrome's simplified-domain trial was abandoned for not moving security metrics. | Detection **71%** with the full URL vs **76%** with domain only, not significant (n=200). Chrome's domain-only trial ended June 2021 with no published metric values. | Mossano et al., KIT (via prior note); [KIT record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000163039/151499531) | ~2023; trial 2020–2021 | Medium |

**Implications:** The shell spec's rule (§4.2) — full URL with the registrable domain emphasised, no invented format — is supported as a cheap, harmless convention, not as a control: every tested format failed to improve identity judgements. Put the effort into an active lookalike interstitial with local skeleton checks, explicit examples ("this is not example.com"), and a per-tab bypass. Do not punycode broadly; follow UTS #39-style per-label rules so non-Latin users are not punished.

## 4. Persistent state after bypass, exceptions and revocation

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Chrome exposes a persistent dangerous state in the site-information panel, not only at the moment of warning. | Panel label: **"Not secure or Dangerous"**; guidance **"Do not use this site"**; a full-page red screen for Safe Browsing matches. | Chrome Help, "Check if a site's connection is secure". [page](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/95617) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 4.2 | The bypass path is deliberately a two-step disclosure, and the download path uses a separate explicit label. | Web: **Details → "Visit this unsafe site"**. Download: **Downloads → More → "Download dangerous file"**. | Chrome Help, "Manage warnings about unsafe sites". [page](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/99020) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 4.3 | Browsers differ on memory. Chrome historically warned every time and remembered nothing; Firefox offered a permanent certificate exception. Izcal must choose one model explicitly. | Chrome "warns the user every time and does not remember the user's past choices"; Firefox's SSL exception dialog could "permanently store this exception". | Akhawe & Felt 2013, §2. [paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/usenixsecurity13/sec13-paper_akhawe.pdf) | 2013-08 | High |
| 4.4 | Chrome's newer lookalike system uses the opposite default: a per-tab ignore expires, and engagement can suppress the warning. | Per-tab ignore; medium site engagement and `domains.list` bypass the check. | Chromium lookalike policy (prose doc). [idn.md](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/idn.md) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 4.5 | Revocation is documented generically, not for warnings. Chrome offers "Reset permissions" and auto-removes permissions from unused sites, but no warning-specific exception list or revoke flow is documented. | "Reset permissions" restores altered preferences; auto-revocation for unused sites. | Chrome Help, "Change site settings permissions". [page](https://support.google.com/chrome/answer/114662) | living, accessed 2026-10-21 | High (absence) |

**Implications:** Keep the dangerous state visible in the site panel after a bypass, but make it a state, not a repeated modal: re-warning on every navigation is the habituation trap. Store bypass as a per-tab, revocable exception with a timestamp; expose it in the site panel with a one-click revoke. **Not established:** whether a persistent post-bypass indicator reduces repeat visits or changes behaviour; no published study found.

**Implications:** A warning is addressed to a principal, not to a window. When an agent navigates, surface the warning to the operator with the agent's identity, the requested action and the reason for the verdict; require explicit consent for any bypass, and never let the agent click through on the human's behalf. Record the check and the decision in an audit log. DevTools should show the reputation verdict, list version and check time, and Settings should expose a local test harness for lists.

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9.1 | Safe Browsing's public scale claim is very large, but it publishes no warning counts or per-verdict accuracy. | "Keeping over five billion devices safer"; "half the world's online population is protected by Safe Browsing". | Google Safe Browsing overview. [page](https://safebrowsing.google.com/) | accessed 2026-10-21 | Medium |

| 9.3 | The clearest published "why was this listed" explanation is owner-facing: category, example URLs and first-detection date. [page](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044101) | accessed 2026-10-21 | High |
| 9.4 | Edge documents what a reputation check sends and stores: URL/file data to the service, results stored locally, not used to identify users, cleared with cache/history. This is a usable transparency pattern. | TLS-encrypted requests; local result storage; clearing browsing cache clears URL data; clearing download history clears file data. | Microsoft Edge SmartScreen docs. [page](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/deployedge/microsoft-edge-security-smartscreen) | 2026-07-08 | High |
| 9.5 | Risk perception predicts behaviour, so explaining the specific risk is a plausible trust lever; but no study found shows that showing the list source, date and evidence increases trust or appropriate behaviour. Herley's cost argument is the counterweight. | Sunshine: understanding domain-mismatch risk correlated with heeding. Herley: advice must cost under ~1 s/day to pay for itself. | [Sunshine et al. 2009](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/events/sec09/tech/full_papers/sunshine.pdf); [Herley 2009](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1719030.1719050) | 2009 | High / Medium |

**Implications:** Show *why* in the interstitial and site panel: source (local heuristic, list, server verdict), category, first-seen date, and the matched evidence (for lookalikes, the two names side by side). State what leaves the device and what is retained. Do not assume explanation builds trust: measure comprehension, trust calibration and bypass rate separately, because Herley's cost model predicts that even a well-explained warning will be ignored when it is usually wrong.
