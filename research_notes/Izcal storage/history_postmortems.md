# Izcal storage: history and postmortems

Compiled 20 October 2026. Primary sources were preferred (papers, specifications, maintainers' posts, release notes, issue trackers). This strand covers storage specifically; the accepted DOM spec's Section 2 already records the service-worker boot numbers, the WebSQL "spec named one library" lesson and the `localStorage` sync-IPC decision (DM-D11), so those are referenced, not re-argued.
Confidence: **High** = primary source read and the claim checked in its text; **Medium** = primary abstract or snippet, or a vendor claim without data; **Low** = secondary source only.

## 1. Cookies as the first client-side store (1994–)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Cookies were invented at Netscape in 1994 to give stateless HTTP per-client state (the shopping-cart problem). Lou Montulli's own account, `montulli.org/theoriginofcookies`, is now behind a login wall and could not be read this session. | — | [montulli.org](https://www.montulli.org/theoriginofcookies) | 1994; page undated | Low |
| 1.2 | RFC 6265 names three predecessors in its introduction: the Netscape cookie specification, RFC 2109 (Feb 1997) and RFC 2965 (Oct 2000) — a 17-year history of the same feature being re-specified because it began as a de-facto format, not a designed one. | 3 specs before 6265 | [RFC 6265](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6265.html) | 2011-04 | High |
| 1.3 | The classic limits are floor values: at least 4096 bytes per cookie, at least 50 cookies per domain, at least 3000 cookies total. They were sized for server-side session IDs, and every request carries them. | 4096 B; 50/domain; 3000 total | [RFC 6265 §6.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6265.html) | 2011-04 | High |
| 1.4 | Misuse as storage: because cookies were the only pre-2006 client-side store, sites stuffed user state into them. MDN's current guidance states cookies "shouldn't be used for storage" precisely because every write is sent with every request. | — | [MDN storage quotas](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria) | 2026-01-05 | High |
| 1.5 | RFC 6265bis (draft-ietf-httpbis-rfc6265bis-22, 1 Dec 2025) obsoletes RFC 6265 and is at the RFC Editor ("blocked: Final Approval Pending"). It folds in SameSite (default = Lax-equivalent), `__Secure-`/`__Host-` prefixes, a 400-day lifetime cap and Secure-overwrite rules — a quarter-century of retrofitting privacy onto a 1994 format. | 400-day cap; v22 2025-12-01 | [Datatracker rfc6265bis](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-httpbis-rfc6265bis/) | 2025-12-01 | High |
| 1.6 | CHIPS adds partitioning to cookies rather than fixing the storage model (see §9): partitioned cookies are capped at 180 per partition and 10 KB per embedded site. | 180; 10 KB | [Chrome CHIPS docs](https://privacysandbox.google.com/3pcd/chips) | 2023-12-15 | High |

**Implications:** The cookie began as a workaround and still cannot be re-designed, only patched (bis, SameSite, CHIPS). Izcal should treat cookie-jar state as what it is — per-origin server-state metadata with request amplification — and give it a first-class store from day one, so no later "cookie as storage" patch layer is ever needed.

## 2. Pre-standard stores: IE userData, Flash LSOs, Google Gears (1995–2011)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | IE's **`userData` behavior** (DHTML persistence, IE 5 era) let a page attach XML blobs to any element, persisted in the browser cache. Documented limit: 64 KB per page, 640 KB per domain. IE-only, forgotten once Web Storage shipped. | 64 KB/page; 640 KB/domain | [userData Behavior (archived MSDN)](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/ms531424(v=vs.85)), [mirror with limits](https://documentation.help/MS-Office-DHTML-HTML-CSS/userdata.htm) | MSDN pre-2009 | Medium |
| 2.2 | **Flash LSOs** ("Flash cookies", from Flash 6, 2002) gave 3rd-party-plug-in storage of up to 100 KB per domain by default (Adobe Settings Manager), survived cookie clearing, and worked across browsers — making them the tracking store of the mid-2000s. Samy Kamkar's evercookie (§9) later used them as one of a dozen respawn points. | ~100 KB default (vendor setting) | [evercookie](https://sa.my/evercookie/), [Adobe settings — verify by black-box measurement] | 2010-09-20 | Medium |
| 2.3 | **Google Gears** (launched May 2007) shipped three modules: a **Database** (SQLite-backed, queried with SQL strings), a **LocalServer** (resource cache for offline) and a **WorkerPool** (background script contexts). It was a browser extension because no browser would ship the features. | 3 modules | [Google Support (archived WorkerPool doc)](https://support.google.com/code/answer/69218?hl=en), InfoWorld coverage | 2007-05 | Medium |
| 2.4 | Gears' fate: Google deprecated it in February 2010 when it judged HTML5 would standardize the same features; Chrome removed Gears support in Chrome 12 (June 2011). The team's own blog post ("Gears vs. HTML5") is offline and web.archive.org was unreachable this session — the fate is documented, the exact farewell wording unverified. | deprecated 2010-02; removed 2011-06 | [Technologizer, "The Permanently Unfulfilled Promise of Google Gears"](https://www.technologizer.com/2010/02/22/the-permanently-unfulfilled-promise-of-google-gears/) | 2010-02-22 | Medium |
| 2.5 | Gears shaped HTML5 storage concretely: WorkerPool became Web Workers (2009), LocalServer's resource cache became AppCache (2009) and later the Service Worker model, and Gears' SQLite database is the direct ancestor of Web SQL Database — Ian Hickson's Web SQL spec is generated from the HTML spec's storage section and shipped with the same SQL-string shape. | — | [W3C Web SQL Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/webdatabase/), [WHATWG archive](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-whatwg-archive/2008Dec/0377.html) | 2008–2010 | Medium |

**Implications:** Every pre-standard store was either single-vendor (userData), outside the browser's security model (Flash) or a stopgap extension (Gears). The pattern that recurs: a private solution becomes de-facto standard, then has to be retro-fitted with privacy (§9). Izcal gets to choose the data model before adoption pressure exists.

## 3. WHATWG Web Storage: localStorage / sessionStorage (2006–2009–)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Web Storage (originally "DOM Storage" in the HTML5 draft of 2006) is a synchronous key/value string store per origin, deliberately shaped like a dictionary so that any page could replace its cookie-state code in an afternoon. Quota settled at 5 MiB + 5 MiB per origin (10 MiB total) across browsers, enforced with `QuotaExceededError`. | 5 MiB + 5 MiB | [MDN storage quotas](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria) | 2026-01-05 | High |
| 3.2 | The synchronous design is the mistake that never got fixed: measurements show "LocalStorage fully blocks the DOM while you're writing data" in Chrome, Firefox and Edge. 10,000 insertions: Chrome in-memory 10 ms vs LocalStorage 527 ms; Firefox 177 ms — with the article's own correction caveat: the LocalStorage figures were later found inaccurate (a test-suite bug stored `"[object Object]"`), and corrected LocalStorage "performs more poorly", so the published numbers understate the cost. Firefox keeps the whole area as an in-memory dictionary persisted to disk — fast reads, jank on the write flush. | 10k writes: ≥ 527 ms (Ch), 177 ms (FF) | [Lawson, "IndexedDB, WebSQL, LocalStorage — what blocks the DOM?"](https://nolanlawson.com/2015/09/29/indexeddb-websql-localstorage-what-blocks-the-dom/) | 2015-09-29 | High (direction), Medium (figures) |
| 3.3 | Why it stayed synchronous: the API predates a worker-and-promises platform, and by the time the cost was understood, compatibility pinned it. A 2008 whatwg-list exchange shows the era's storage backends were already SQLite files inspected with third-party SQLite tools; the spec's author declined to say anything about it since "it doesn't affect interoperability" — implementation concerns were explicitly out of scope. | — | [whatwg archive, Feb 2008](https://lists.whatwg.org/pipermail/whatwg-whatwg.org/2008-February/056169.html) | 2008-02-08 | Medium |
| 3.4 | Event semantics were an afterthought: the `storage` event fires in *other* same-origin contexts, not the writer, and its delivery timing is weakly specified — ordering guarantees between a write and the event remain an interop sore point. Izcal's DOM spec already replaces this with a versioned snapshot + overlay protocol (DM-D11), which is the right family of fix. | — | [WHATWG Web Storage](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/webstorage.html) | living spec | Medium |
| 3.5 | Storage is still not available in dedicated/shared workers per the shipped API surface (the spec never exposed it there), so worker-side code must use IndexedDB — one reason worker-only storage stacks grew. | — | [WHATWG Web Storage](https://html.spec.whatwg.org/multipage/webstorage.html) | living spec | Medium |

**Implications:** A dictionary-shaped API won adoption precisely because it was synchronous and trivial; the cost (main-thread blocking, no worker access, fuzzy event semantics) was invisible in 2006 and structural by 2015. Izcal's snapshot-plus-async-writes design (DM-D11) keeps the trivial shape at the call site without the blocking — the lesson is that the *shape* survived and should be kept, while the *execution model* was the era's constraint and should not be inherited.

## 4. Web SQL Database (2009–2010): the most instructive postmortem

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Web SQL (April 2009) gave pages a real relational database: `openDatabase`, transactions, SQL strings, backed by SQLite. It shipped in Safari, Chrome and Opera. Firefox never implemented it. | shipped 2009; FF never | [Chrome, "Deprecating and removing Web SQL"](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2022 (post last updated 2022-08-31) | High |
| 4.2 | It was abandoned after 19 months: introduced April 2009, "abandoned November 2010". The W3C note states the impasse in one sentence: "all interested implementors have used the same SQL backend (Sqlite), but we need multiple independent implementations". The spec literally mandated "the SQL dialect supported by Sqlite 3.6.19". | ~19 months to abandonment | [W3C Web SQL Note](https://www.w3.org/TR/webdatabase/), [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2010-11-18 | High |
| 4.3 | Mozilla's refusal, quoted by the Chrome team: Mozilla did not think SQLite was "the right basis for an API exposed to general web content" — and proposed the transactional object-store design that became IndexedDB instead. | — | [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2022-08-31 | High |
| 4.4 | The Chrome team's later postmortem adds three reasons beyond the one-library problem: SQLite was not designed to run untrusted, malicious SQL (browsers must run exactly that); the dialect lock conflicted with shipping SQLite security updates; and the callback-based, SQL-string API shape aged badly. Verdict: "The Web SQL specification cannot be implemented sustainably, which limits innovation and new features." | 3 additional reasons | [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2022-08-31 | High |
| 4.5 | Removal was a decade-long tail even for willing vendors: removed from third-party contexts in Chromium 97, insecure contexts 105–110, deprecated everywhere at 115, fully removed in Chromium 119 (Dec 2023). WebKit had removed it in 2019. A web standard that dies leaves user data and site code behind for ~13 years. | removed: Ch 119 (2023); Wk 2019 | [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2022–2023 | High |
| 4.6 | Measured at the time, Web SQL was also fast: in Chrome it beat IndexedDB ~2x (213 ms vs 572 ms for 10k inserts); in Safari ~100x (173 ms vs 10,658 ms — Safari 9's IndexedDB was catastrophically slow). Performance was never the reason it died. | 213 vs 572 ms; 173 vs 10,658 ms | [Lawson 2015](https://nolanlawson.com/2015/09/29/indexeddb-websql-localstorage-what-blocks-the-dom/) | 2015-09-29 | High |
| 4.7 | The successor Chrome recommends for SQL-shaped needs is not an engine feature but a web-page technology: SQLite compiled to WebAssembly backed by OPFS, which "outperforms Web SQL in many cases" and is roughly on par in the SQLite team's own benchmark ("generally about as fast as Web SQL"). | — | [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql), [Chrome SQLite-Wasm post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/sqlite-wasm-in-the-browser-backed-by-the-origin-private-file-system) | 2022/2023-01-11 | High |

**Implications:** This is the founding postmortem for Izcal's storage spec: a standard must be implementable from its text alone, must not embed a third component's dialect and version, and must tolerate adversarial input in every layer beneath it. Note the second-order lesson: the winning *platform* answer was to expose a lower-level primitive (OPFS) and let the market build databases on top — Izcal's engine should aim for that layering, not for baking in a query language.

## 5. IndexedDB (2009–2024)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Origin: Oracle's Nikunj Mehta proposed "WebSimpleDB" on the W3C public-webapps list in 2009; Mozilla pushed the design that became IndexedDB as the SQL-free alternative to Web SQL, with async APIs and explicit transactions. The REC's editor list shows the coalition: Mehta, Sicking (Mozilla), Graff (Microsoft), Popescu/Orlow/Bell (Google). | — | [W3C IndexedDB 1.0 REC](https://www.w3.org/TR/2015/REC-IndexedDB-20150108/), [public-webapps archives](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-webapps/) | 2009–2015 | Medium |
| 5.2 | Dates: 1.0 became a W3C Recommendation 8 Jan 2015; 2.0 on 30 Jan 2018; **3.0 is still a Working Draft (13 Aug 2025)** — sixteen years from the 2009 proposal to the still-pending third edition. | 2015-01-08; 2018-01-30; WD 2025-08-13 | [W3C IndexedDB 3.0 WD](https://www.w3.org/TR/IndexedDB-3/) | 2025-08-13 | High |
| 5.3 | Why asynchronous: methods "return immediately without blocking the calling thread" and multiple requests can be active per database, delivered via a dedicated database-access task source. Why transactions: atomic, durable protection against "application and system failures", with overlapping read-write transactions serialized while read-only ones run concurrently. Why object stores instead of SQL: the spec's own contrast with Web Storage is in-order key retrieval, value search and duplicates — it needs indexes and ranges, not a query language; implementation "typically with persistent B-trees". | — | [W3C IndexedDB 3.0 WD](https://www.w3.org/TR/IndexedDB-3/) | 2025-08-13 | High |
| 5.4 | The async promise did not hold: in 2015 IndexedDB "blocked the DOM for nearly the entire duration of the operation" in Chrome and Firefox (10k inserts: 572 ms Chrome, 823 ms Firefox); moving it into a worker kept speed and unblocked the DOM — and was the only storage API available in workers. | Ch 572 ms; FF 823 ms | [Lawson 2015](https://nolanlawson.com/2015/09/29/indexeddb-websql-localstorage-what-blocks-the-dom/) | 2015-09-29 | High |
| 5.5 | Early interop was rough: Safari's IndexedDB was so slow it lost to WebSQL by ~100x and to everything else by orders of magnitude (10,658 ms for 10k inserts in Safari 9); stable Safari support only arrived in 2017. Edge shipped a divergent early implementation. Wrapper libraries became standard practice to paper over the differences. | 10,658 ms | [Lawson 2015](https://nolanlawson.com/2015/09/29/indexeddb-websql-localstorage-what-blocks-the-dom/) | 2015-09-29 | Medium |
| 5.6 | Ergonomics were admitted debt: the API is request/event based, transactions auto-commit when the event loop turns — an await or a misplaced async call silently invalidates them — and promise support never entered the W3C text at all (the 3.0 WD contains none); the ecosystem standardized on promise wrappers (Jake Archibald's `idb`). | — | [W3C IndexedDB 3.0 WD](https://www.w3.org/TR/IndexedDB-3/), [web.dev IndexedDB](https://web.dev/articles/storage-for-the-web) | 2025-08-13 | High |
| 5.7 | IndexedDB 3.0's fixes are a list of the original design's omissions: explicit `transaction.commit()`, durability hints (`strict` flushes OS buffers before `complete`; `relaxed` does not), `getAll` with options, record snapshots and connection queues. | 3 changes | [W3C IndexedDB 3.0 WD](https://www.w3.org/TR/IndexedDB-3/) | 2025-08-13 | High |
| 5.8 | Persistence/eviction history: IndexedDB data defaults to best-effort and was always subject to LRU eviction under pressure. Chrome research found automatic clearing is rare; `navigator.storage.persist()` (Chrome 55, Firefox 57, Safari 15.2) makes storage user-clear-only, granted silently by engagement heuristics in Chrome/Safari and with a prompt in Firefox. Safari's aggressive cap: since iOS 13.4 / Safari 13.1 (March 2020), "ITP has aligned the remaining script-writable storage forms with the existing client-side cookie restriction, deleting all of a website's script-writable storage after seven days of Safari use without user interaction" — IndexedDB included; home-screen web apps exempt. | 7 days; Ch 55/FF 57/Saf 15.2 | [web.dev persistent storage](https://web.dev/articles/persistent-storage), [WebKit blog](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/) | 2020-03-24 | High |

**Implications:** IndexedDB proves both that Mozilla's objection to Web SQL was right and that the replacement under-delivered for a decade (still no promises in the standard; 3.0 unfinished after 14 years). For Izcal: design the async/transactional core for the promise-era from day one, keep the main thread out of it structurally (worker/broker side), and treat durability as an explicit, per-transaction choice rather than a v3 retrofit.

## 6. AppCache (2009) and its replacement, Service Worker + Cache API (2014–)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | AppCache (2009) was a declarative manifest for offline resources: list URLs in a text file, the browser caches them. Its documented gotchas: cached files are always served "even if you're online"; updates are gated on the manifest file itself changing; it "obeys regular caching instructions" so HTTP headers can silently break updates; "the user's cached version still points at the old manifest... so nothing updates, ever" if the manifest URL changes; uncached subresources fail even when online; "AppCache doesn't give us a way to remove implicitly cached items"; a manifest change mid-update aborts the update; cross-origin redirects are treated as failures. | 9 documented gotchas | [Archibald, "Application Cache is a Douchebag" (A List Apart)](https://alistapart.com/article/application-cache-is-a-douchebag) | 2012-05-08 | High |
| 6.2 | The maintainer-level postmortem: Archibald (2015) — with AppCache "we didn't have enough evidence, and created a high-level solution that couldn't be used the way developers wanted"; the 2014 replacement philosophy was "we gave up trying to solve offline, and gave developers the moving parts to go solve it themselves." | — | [Archibald 2015](https://jakearchibald.com/2015/if-we-stand-still-we-go-backwards/), [Archibald, "Offline Cookbook"](https://jakearchibald.com/2014/offline-cookbook/) | 2014-12-09 | High |
| 6.3 | Service Worker + Cache API inverted every AppCache decision: event handlers (`install`/`activate`/`fetch`) instead of a manifest, explicit named and versioned caches with developer-driven cleanup, failure handled in code, `event.waitUntil` making installation a promise that can reject, and the Cache API callable from pages without the worker. AppCache was removed from the engines by 2021 (already recorded in the accepted DOM spec's history table, HP 3.7). | — | [Offline Cookbook](https://jakearchibald.com/2014/offline-cookbook/) | 2014-12-09 | High |
| 6.4 | Lesson stated by the spec's own designers: a declarative high-level cache baked into the engine fails when the developer's needs do not match its one pattern ("If those were the patterns you needed, congratulations, you won the AppCache lottery"). | — | [Offline Cookbook](https://jakearchibald.com/2014/offline-cookbook/) | 2014-12-09 | High |

**Implications:** Izcal should implement caching as composable low-level primitives with explicit versioning and eviction hooks, never as one prescriptive declarative scheme — and should demand evidence of usage patterns before adding any higher-level declarative layer on top.

## 7. Quota and eviction history (2009–2026)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.1 | First era: small fixed quotas — 5 MiB + 5 MiB for Web Storage everywhere; Safari granted a fixed initial 1 GB per origin for the big stores, prompting the user for more "regardless of persistence mode". The W3C's 2012 Quota API draft institutionalized the split into TEMPORARY and PERSISTENT pools. | 5+5 MiB; 1 GB | [MDN storage quotas](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria), [W3C Quota API WD 2012](https://www.w3.org/TR/2012/WD-quota-api-20120403/) | 2012/2026 | High/Medium |
| 7.2 | Second era (current, "share of the disk"): Chrome gives the browser up to 80% of total disk and an origin up to 60% of total disk (reported as 60% of *actual* disk size regardless of free space); Firefox up to 50% of *free* disk space with a per-eTLD+1 group cap of 2 GB (web.dev, Sept 2024). MDN (Jan 2026) states Firefox best-effort as min(10% of disk, 10 GiB group) and persistent as 50% of disk capped at 8 TiB. | Chrome 60%/80%; FF 50% free / 10% / 10 GiB | [web.dev storage-for-the-web](https://web.dev/articles/storage-for-the-web), [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria) | 2024-09-23 / 2026-01-05 | High |
| 7.3 | Safari's move: from the old fixed 1 GB + prompts to about 60% of total disk on macOS 14+/iOS 17+ (embedded WKWebView apps ~15%, home-screen web apps ~60%); WebKit caps all origins combined at 80% of disk; cross-origin iframes get about 1/10 of the parent's quota. The older prose figure ("about 1 GB, prompts, +200 MB increments" — web.dev 2024) documents the transition. | ~60%; ~15%; ~1/10 | [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria), [web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/storage-for-the-web) | 2026-01-05 / 2024-09-23 | High |
| 7.4 | Quotas are now computed from *total* disk size rather than free space deliberately, "to resist fingerprinting" — a privacy constraint reshaping a storage-engine number. | — | [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria) | 2026-01-05 | High |
| 7.5 | Eviction model: default ("Best Effort") data is evicted LRU-by-origin under pressure, whole-origin all-or-nothing (all storage types together, "to avoid inconsistency"); persistent origins are skipped; Chrome caps the browser at 80% of disk and evicts best-effort origins when exceeded. Chrome research: automatic clearing is rare; user-initiated clearing dominates. | all-or-nothing per origin | [web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/storage-for-the-web), [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria), [web.dev persistent storage](https://web.dev/articles/persistent-storage) | 2020–2026 | High |
| 7.6 | Proactive eviction is now a privacy feature too: with tracking prevention on, Safari deletes an origin's script-created data after 7 days of browser use without interaction, and pre-13.4 behavior had already evicted without ceremony — the historical claim that Safari in the mid-2010s wiped idle sites' storage on ~30-day scales was not verifiable in primary prose this session (see Open questions). | 7 days | [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria), [WebKit ITP post](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/) | 2026-01-05 | High / (30-day claim: unverified) |

**Implications:** Quota policy drifted from "small fixed numbers" to "share of disk + eviction classes + fingerprinting constraints + privacy-driven proactive deletion". A new engine should specify quota as policy over a partition key (site-level, with per-bucket carve-outs) from the start, with LRU-versus-persistent as explicit classes and every number published as part of the spec, not discovered by developers through `estimate()`.

## 8. File System Access API and OPFS (2018–2023)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.1 | OPFS is "a storage endpoint private to the origin of the page and not visible to the user", part of the File System standard. It exists because user-visible file I/O is slow by design: security checks (mark-of-the-web, Safe Browsing), permission prompts, and write-through-temporary-file semantics. OPFS drops those costs: "there are no permissions prompts and no Safe Browsing checks". | — | [web.dev OPFS](https://web.dev/articles/origin-private-file-system) | 2023-06-08 | High |
| 8.2 | The performance story is the synchronous access handle: `createSyncAccessHandle()` gives in-place `read/write/flush/truncate` on a file, synchronous by design and therefore **restricted to dedicated workers** ("Because Workers can't block the main thread, sync APIs are allowed there"). This is the platform formally re-admitting a synchronous storage API — six years after declaring localStorage's sync design a mistake — under the worker-scoped rule Izcal's own storage design follows. | — | [web.dev OPFS](https://web.dev/articles/origin-private-file-system) | 2023-06-08 | High |
| 8.3 | Support: Chrome/Edge 86 (2020), Safari 15.2 (2021), Firefox 111 (2023) — three to six years from first ship to full interop. | Ch 86; Saf 15.2; FF 111 | [web.dev OPFS](https://web.dev/articles/origin-private-file-system) | 2023-06-08 | High |
| 8.4 | Demonstrated value: SQLite compiled to Wasm over OPFS is the Chrome-recommended WebSQL replacement, "generally about as fast as Web SQL" by the SQLite team's benchmark, with OPFS's in-place exclusive writes and the Worker plumbing hidden behind a promiser. Note the constraint leak: it requires COOP/COEP headers because it needs SharedArrayBuffer. | on par with WebSQL | [Chrome SQLite-Wasm post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/sqlite-wasm-in-the-browser-backed-by-the-origin-private-file-system) | 2023-01-11 | High |
| 8.5 | OPFS completed the platform's storage hierarchy: opaque key-value (Web Storage) → structured async (IndexedDB) → raw private bytes with sync access (OPFS). Each layer was added when the previous one's abstraction tax became measurable. | — | [web.dev OPFS](https://web.dev/articles/origin-private-file-system), [Chrome deprecation post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/deprecating-web-sql) | 2022–2023 | High |

**Implications:** The endpoint of fifteen years of storage history is a private, origin-scoped, byte-addressable file system with synchronous access from workers. That is a strong signal for what a from-scratch engine should make its *foundation* layer, with IndexedDB-class and KV-class APIs as layers above it — and with the sync-for-workers rule (never the main thread) as a constitutional constraint, not an add-on.

## 9. Partitioning and privacy: evercookie to CHIPS (2009–2024)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9.1 | evercookie (Samy Kamkar, 20 Sept 2010) demonstrated the tracking ceiling of per-origin storage: one identifier written to HTTP cookies, Flash LSOs, Silverlight storage, canvas-encoded PNGs, ETags, web cache, `window.name`, IE userData, HTML5 local/session/database storage, IndexedDB and more — "it only takes one cookie to remain for most, if not all, of them to be reset again". | 10+ mechanisms | [evercookie](https://sa.my/evercookie/) | 2010-09-20 | High |
| 9.2 | The defense that worked was not deletion but *keying*: the Privacy CG's storage-partitioning work item states plainly that origin-keyed state "is an acknowledged privacy and security bug" — cross-site reads leak visits via timing/XS-Leaks, and embedded trackers correlate users across sites — so state must be "keyed by more than a single origin or site". Coverage now spans cookies, HTTP cache, IndexedDB, Cache API, localStorage, BroadcastChannel, shared/service workers, Web Locks and more. | — | [Privacy CG storage partitioning](https://privacycg.github.io/storage-partitioning/) | ~2022– | High |
| 9.3 | Timeline of the shift: Safari ITP (2017) began partitioning/purging tracker domains' data; the Storage Access API (Feb 2018) let embedded content request its first-party *cookie* jar back behind a user gesture ("when the user interacts with them"); ITP 2.3 (23 Sept 2019) ran storage-partitioning experiments and capped link-decorated sites' storage at 7 days; full third-party cookie blocking (Mar 2020) deleted all script-writable storage after 7 idle days; Firefox moved to default state partitioning (ETP Strict/Total Cookie Protection); Chrome shipped third-party storage partitioning (M115, 2023) and introduced CHIPS opt-in partitioned cookies (Chrome 114+). | 2017→2023 | [WebKit SAA](https://webkit.org/blog/8124/introducing-storage-access-api/), [ITP 2.3](https://webkit.org/blog/9521/intelligent-tracking-prevention-2-3/), [full blocking post](https://webkit.org/blog/10218/full-third-party-cookie-blocking-and-more/), [CHIPS docs](https://privacysandbox.google.com/3pcd/chips) | 2018-02-21 → 2023 | High |
| 9.4 | CHIPS is the partition-key concept applied to cookies: a `Partitioned` cookie is double-keyed, "the host key plus a partition key (the scheme + registrable domain of the top-level URL)", sent only when the request's partition key matches; opt-in only, 180 cookies and 10 KB per partition. Google contrasts it with Firefox's default partitioning and Safari's abandoned heuristic partitioning ("citing developer confusion"). | 180; 10 KB | [CHIPS docs](https://privacysandbox.google.com/3pcd/chips) | 2023-12-15 | High |
| 9.5 | Storage Access API later grew beyond cookies to partitioned storage access, but the 2018 original is the design fossil: access is per-document, gesture-gated, and "does not relax the same-origin policy in any way". | — | [WebKit SAA](https://webkit.org/blog/8124/introducing-storage-access-api/) | 2018-02-21 | High |
| 9.6 | The storage key therefore became a triple (top-level site, frame site, opt-in access state) that every storage subsystem must honor — including caches and workers, which is why the accepted DOM spec keys service-worker registrations and Cache API entries by storage key. | — | [Privacy CG storage partitioning](https://privacycg.github.io/storage-partitioning/) | ~2022– | High |

**Implications:** Partitioning by top-level site is now a load-bearing invariant of the whole platform, not a cookie detail. Izcal should make the partition key part of the storage engine's addressing from the first commit — retrofitting it, as every incumbent did, touches every subsystem at once.

## 10. Storage Buckets (WICG 2022 → Chrome 122)

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10.1 | Motivation, from the explainer: user agents have "a binary choice of either persisting or deleting all the data stored by a site when faced with storage pressure"; buckets let sites "decide on storage trade-offs themselves upon storage eviction during low disk space instead of losing all data", and serve as "a centralized surface for future enhancements to storage APIs" (quota, persistence, expiration per bucket, not per API). | — | [WICG storage-buckets explainer](https://github.com/WICG/storage-buckets/blob/main/explainer.md) | 2022– | High |
| 10.2 | Shape: `navigator.storageBuckets.open(name, {persisted, quota, expires})`; buckets are independently evictable; `expires` timestamps guarantee deletion on open/list; Clear-Site-Data gains `storage:inbox`; policies are advisory hints. Constraints: names ≤ 64 chars, at least 10 buckets per origin, hard limit 10,000; default bucket holds existing `indexedDB`/`caches`. | 64 chars; 10 min; 10,000 max | [WICG explainer](https://github.com/WICG/storage-buckets/blob/main/explainer.md) | 2022– | High |
| 10.3 | Durability wobble: the explainer "cautiously removed" the durability hint from the first version (it "only applied to IndexedDB, which limits its utility"), yet the Chrome blog documents shipping it in Chromium 122 with `'strict'` vs `'relaxed'` — strict "minimizes loss risk at the cost of slower, more power-hungry writes", relaxed may drop "the last few seconds of writes on power loss". A reminder that durability semantics get renegotiated even inside one proposal. | Chromium 122 | [Chrome storage-buckets post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/storage-buckets), [WICG explainer](https://github.com/WICG/storage-buckets/blob/main/explainer.md) | 2022-11 / 2024 | High |
| 10.4 | Status: shipping in Chromium 122 (Feb 2024); Gecko positive, WebKit "no signals" — two years later still single-engine, the WebSQL pattern's shadow. Implementation currently covers IndexedDB only. | 1 engine | [Chrome post](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/storage-buckets), [WICG explainer](https://github.com/WICG/storage-buckets/blob/main/explainer.md) | 2022–2024 | High |

**Implications:** Buckets are the industry conceding that per-origin all-or-nothing persistence is the wrong granularity. Izcal can make the bucket (or an equivalent policy unit) a native record of the storage engine — eviction priority, durability class and expiry as engine metadata — instead of a browser-side convention over one engine.

## 11. What ran underneath: SQLite era, then log-structured stores

| # | Finding | Numbers | Source | Date | Conf. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11.1 | The embedded engine of the browser-storage era was SQLite: "Every Firefox, Chrome, and Safari web browser" ships it (SQLite project prose). Firefox adopted it wholesale — Firefox 3 (2008) replaced the Mork flat-file history format with a SQLite Places database, and DOM storage and later WebSQL sat on it too. | since 2008 (Places) | [sqlite.org/mostdeployed](https://www.sqlite.org/mostdeployed.html), [forensics.wiki Firefox history](https://forensics.wiki/firefox_history/), [MozillaZine Places](http://kb.mozillazine.org/Places.sqlite) | 2008 | Medium |
| 11.2 | WebSQL's death removed the one case where SQLite was *specified* into the web; it remained an implementation choice. Which engine each browser uses for IndexedDB/Cache today is an implementation fact to establish by black-box measurement for Izcal's spec, not from source. | — | this note's evidence rule | — | — |
| 11.3 | The log-structured turn: LevelDB (Google, 2011, Ghemawat & Dean) popularized the LSM store for exactly this niche — "a fast key-value storage library... an ordered mapping from string keys to string values", atomic batches, snapshots, Snappy compression, single-process access, explicitly "not a SQL database... no support for indexes". | — | [LevelDB README](https://github.com/google/leveldb/blob/master/README.md) | 2011 (bench: 2011-05-01) | High |
| 11.4 | LevelDB's own design doc states the LSM economics: a 4 MB in-memory log/memtable flushes to level-0 sorted tables; level L compacts into level L+1 when it exceeds 10^L MB; a worst-case level compaction "will read 26 MB and write 26 MB" ≈ 0.5 s at 100 MB/s — and its 2011 bench shows the sync-write cliff: fillsync 0.4 MB/s vs fillseq 62.7 MB/s. The doc also names the failure mode browsers hit: heavy writers piling up level-0 files until "reads [are] significantly" more expensive. | 26 MB read+write; 0.4 vs 62.7 MB/s | [LevelDB impl.md](https://github.com/google/leveldb/blob/master/doc/impl.md) | 2011 | High |
| 11.5 | The measured cost of LSMs for small values produced the next design: WiscKey (FAST '16) separates keys from values — keys stay in the LSM, values go to an append-only log — to minimize I/O amplification; with LevelDB's own microbenchmark it is "2.5×–111× faster than LevelDB for loading a database" and ~3x in general workloads, trading slower range scans against SSD parallelism. Read amplification, the paper notes, is in practice worse than write amplification. | 2.5–111x load; ~3x general | [WiscKey, USENIX FAST '16](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/fast16/fast16-papers-lu.pdf) | 2016-02 | Medium |
| 11.6 | The B-tree counter-candidate never went away: LMDB is marketed as "ultra-fast, ultra-compact, crash-proof", memory-mapped for "zero-copy lookup and iteration", ACID with MVCC, "no external process, background cleanup, compaction, application-level cache, logs, or crash recovery procedure", ~32 KB of object code. Its claims target precisely LSM weaknesses (compaction storms, write amplification) with a copy-on-write B+tree. | ~32 KB object code | [Symas LMDB](https://symas.com/lmdb/) | current | High |
| 11.7 | Ideas that survived into browser storage engines: append-only logs + sorted levels (write-optimized ingest, background compaction), atomic batches, MVCC snapshots for concurrent readers, durability hints (fsync policy) surfaced to applications, and key-value separation. The open question for Izcal is which amplification profile fits a browser's workload (many small records, high read ratio, strict per-origin isolation) — a measurement task, not a borrowing decision. | — | [WiscKey](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/fast16/fast16-papers-lu.pdf), [LevelDB impl.md](https://github.com/google/leveldb/blob/master/doc/impl.md) | 2011–2016 | Medium |

**Implications:** Browser storage converged on embedded KV engines after SQLite, then on LSM variants, with the literature's verdict being workload-dependent. For Izcal the transferable content is the *quantified trade-offs* (sync-write cliff, compaction cost, amplification) and the requirement to pick by measurement of Izcal's own access patterns — the engines themselves stay out of scope under M-1.

## Lessons learned (project → what happened → the lesson → how Izcal applies it)

| Project | What happened | The lesson | How Izcal applies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cookies | 1994 workaround became the universal store; 30 years of patches (2109, 2965, 6265, bis, SameSite, CHIPS) | A stopgap that wins adoption becomes permanent and gets patched forever | First-class separation of cookie state from client storage; never let one serve as the other |
| IE userData / Flash LSOs | Single-vendor and plug-in stores filled the standard's vacuum | If the platform doesn't provide storage, sites will accept worse storage — including unpartitioned tracking storage | Ship the real primitive early; partition keys in the engine core |
| Google Gears | Extension-based stopgap deprecated when HTML5 absorbed its features | Good API shapes migrate; their constraints (extension installs, single vendor) do not | Adopt Gears' proven shapes (worker pool, resource cache, local DB) as engine-native, spec-first pieces |
| Web Storage | Synchronous dictionary won; blocking cost structural by 2015, unfixable by compat | The call-site shape survives; the execution model is the era-specific part | Keep the trivial shape (snapshot reads), move writes async (DM-D11) |
| WebSQL | Dead in 19 months: spec named one library and its dialect version | Standards must be implementable from text alone | No third-party component in the normative text; adversarial-input tolerance at every layer |
| IndexedDB | Right idea, 14-year gestation, promise-less API, v3 unfinished | Async design must be built for the platform's concurrency model from day one | Promise-native, worker/broker-side engine, durability per transaction |
| AppCache | Declarative one-pattern cache, unfixable, removed 2021 | Declarative layers need evidence of pattern fit | Low-level primitives first; declarative layers only over measured usage |
| Safari eviction | 7-day idle deletion aligned storage with cookie lifetimes | Persistence is a privacy decision now | Persist/purge policy published in the spec, keyed by interaction |
| Storage Buckets | Single-engine after 2+ years | New storage surface areas stall without multi-engine buy-in | Spec and implement across engines (WPT conformance) before adding developer surface |

## Open questions

1. **Safari's pre-ITP eviction history** — the claim that mid-2010s Safari wiped idle sites' storage on ~30-day scales could not be confirmed in primary prose; only the 7-day ITP cap (2020) is documented. Needs a WebKit bug/prose search or a black-box study of old Safari.
2. **Which storage engine each incumbent uses today for IndexedDB, Cache API and OPFS** (and their on-disk formats) — an implementation fact to establish by black-box measurement only (file artifacts, behavior under stress), never from source.
3. **The original rationale for synchronous Web Storage** — no single maintainer statement found; the whatwg archive (2006–2009) needs a dedicated pass to quote the design reasoning in the author's words.
4. **Gears' own farewell text** — the Gears team blog post is offline and Wayback was unreachable this session; verify the exact deprecation wording and date from an archived copy.
5. **Adobe's documented Flash LSO default limit (100 KB per domain)** — verify against archived Adobe Settings Manager prose or by black-box measurement against a period Flash runtime.
6. **Storage Buckets in WebKit/Gecko** — explainer says WebKit "no signals"; check current standards positions and whether `durability` converged between the explainer and Chromium's shipped API.
7. **LevelDB-era performance in modern browser workloads** — WiscKey's numbers are storage-microbenchmark-shaped; Izcal needs its own workload benchmark (many small records, mixed reads/writes, per-origin isolation) before choosing an amplification profile.
