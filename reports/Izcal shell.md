The browser's shell is where a person decides whether to trust a site, what a prompt means, where their work went, and how to get it back. In 2026 the shells of every major browser still answer those questions with a moment: a small prompt, a lock or tune icon, a list of history rows with no provenance, a download that says "done" without saying where. The browser knows why a navigation happened, who asked for a capability, what it decided, what changed on the page and what it will do next, and shows the person almost none of it. Izcal already records those facts for its own correctness: a deterministic substrate, typed capability IPC, broker-decided consent, a semantic tree, storage facts and spec anchors. **The central idea of this spec is the shell as the browser's accountable surface: an engine-rendered UI with no ambient authority, whose every state is a typed query over broker-held facts, whose prompts are broker decisions drawn in a window the page cannot paint, and whose own state is recorded, replayable and explainable.** The shell is not a skin over an engine; it is a staffed component with a process model, a security boundary, a determinism contract and a budget.

The decisions that matter most: **(1)** the consent layer is redesigned around **broker-controlled prompt windows** — separate owned OS windows drawn by a jitless prompt renderer, placed and judged by the broker, with a durable **prompt ledger** (SH-D2, SH-D11); **(2)** history and bookmarks become **one store with provenance and health**, schema owned here and held by the storage service, closing Q-SG-6 (SH-D8); **(3)** the shell is **recorded at its capability boundary**, so a UI bug replays from a typed event stream and UI state rather than from pixels (SH-D10); and **(4)** the shell is a **staffed track** of 168–243 engineer-months† (6.8–7.0 on the stated pairing; the 6–8 band is a steady-state planning assumption, peak 10–12†) over 48–60 months† (C-7's settled Stage 1 exit), closing ledger gap G-21, with every overlapping track's count stated rather than double-counted (SH-D1). A fifth decision, **the Windows UI host owns the content window and hosts TSF and the accessibility surface while the broker owns every prompt window** (SH-D3), resolves the conflict between AX-D9 and ED-D5 and is the owner's to confirm because it adds an always-on process to every Windows session.

## 1. The problem and who has it

People do not use a browser to use its shell. They use the shell when something has gone wrong, when a site asks for something, or when they are looking for something they had. Those are the moments this spec is about.

| Who | What they are trying to do | What the shell does today | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anyone on a site that asks** | Decide whether to allow a capability, and understand what they gave up | A prompt names a capability, not a consequence; it disappears; nothing durable says what was allowed or what happened after | Notifications were 74% of Chrome's permission prompts and were granted 12% of the time on desktop ([Bilogrevic et al., USENIX Security 2021](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/bilogrevic)); Firefox showed about 1.45 billion prompts in a month and had 23.66 million accepted ([Mozilla, 2019](https://blog.mozilla.org/futurereleases/2019/11/04/restricting-notification-permission-prompts-in-firefox/)); 89% of 1,880 people held misconceptions about the padlock ([von Zezschwitz et al., IEEE S&P Workshops 2022](https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/)) |
| **Anyone deciding whether a page is the one they meant** | Tell a real site from a lookalike | A URL and a neutral icon; users identify real sites 93% of the time and lookalikes 40% of the time, while feeling confident | ([Reynolds et al., CHI 2020](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/)); [gaps 1.5.6](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/gaps_opportunities.md) |
| **People with many tabs** | Not lose work; find what they had | Tabs are the only durable reminder; session restore fails silently; recently closed loses group structure | 55% of 103 surveyed users "can't let go" of tabs and about 25% had crashed from too many ([Chang et al., CHI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1145/3411764.3445585)); about 1.2–1.3% of restorations fail to restore tabs ([Chromium issue 40196304](https://issues.chromium.org/issues/40196304), Medium) |
| **People trying to re-find a page** | Find a page they saw before | History is a list with no provenance; bookmarks are saved and not used | Only 16% of bookmarked targets were retrieved through the bookmark facility and 4% through its menu ([Bergman, Whittaker & Schooler, 2021](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Out-of-sight-and-out-of-mind%3A-Bookmarks-are-created-Bergman-Whittaker/f2d422a4f13217806189e7683e9065b2f00a24a9)); users preferred re-searching to opening history ([UIUC Trailblazer formative evaluation](http://www.acm.uiuc.edu/macwarriors/projects/trailblazer/papers/formative_eval.pdf), Low) |
| **People who never open settings** | Have a browser that is right by default | The default is the product, but its reasons are invisible; settings sprawl | Fewer than 5% of several hundred Word settings files changed anything ([Spool, 2011](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/), Medium); Firefox retired its catch-all General page in June 2026 after usability work ([Mozilla](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/firefox-settings/), Medium; primary returned HTTP 403 at phase 8) |
| **Screen-reader, keyboard and low-vision users** | Use every browser surface | Browser UI has often been the least accessible part of the product; zoom and reflow are afterthoughts | 95.9% of the top million homepages had detectable WCAG 2 failures in February 2026 ([WebAIM Million 2026](https://webaim.org/projects/million/)); 44% of assistive-technology users report browser zoom, and 28% of a low-vision sample use more than 200% ([AVH report](https://www.avh.asso.fr/sites/default/files/rapport_usage_technologies_assistance_mars2018.docx), Medium); Firefox's Cache the World cut NVDA's load of a 12,000-row table from 128 s to 6 s ([Teh, 2022](https://www.jantrid.net/2022/12/22/Cache-the-World/)) |
| **People who refuse AI in the browser** | Keep AI off, and keep it off after updates | AI entry points appear in default UI; a model can download without asking | Chrome downloaded about 4 GB of Gemini Nano weights in 14 m 28 s on a fresh profile with no user input ([Hanff, 2026](https://www.thatprivacyguy.com/blog/chrome-silent-nano-install/)); Edge folded Copilot features back into default browsing in May 2026 ([gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2026/05/15/microsoft-edge-retires-copilot-mode-and-integrates-ai-features-across-desktop-and-mobile/), Medium) |
| **People whose browser will not start** | Recover without losing data | A crash dialog, or no window at all; the recovery path is often the least-tested code in the product | Firefox renamed Safe Mode to Troubleshoot Mode in 2021 because the old wording misstated what it disabled ([shell UI design 4.8](HGBA%20shell%20UI%20design.md)); Chrome has no safe mode |
| **Izcal's own engineers and support** | Reproduce a UI bug, explain a state, test the UI like a page | UI bugs are reported as screenshots; the shell is outside the recorded unit | A shell UI bug is not covered by DT-D2's recording, whose unit is the tab's browsing context group ([gaps 3.4, 4.4](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/gaps_opportunities.md)) |

**What Izcal's specs already decide.** Stage 1 D-S2 makes the shell HTML/CSS rendered by Izcal in a sandboxed shell renderer with zero ambient authority, CSS-only theming, a native recovery window after two failures, and W-1 components for scripted customization. HGBA-3 B1 and DET-1 give the deterministic substrate; B2 gives typed capability IPC; B8 gives the semantic tree; B9 and B10 give the agent principal and AI off by default. The security scope decides that the broker, not the shell, judges consent (CON-R1–CON-R5), that security prompts are drawn by a separate jitless prompt renderer with no page-derived strings except the broker-formatted origin (CON-R2), and that the prompt cannot be covered by page or shell content (CON-R5). The accessibility spec designs the window-owning UI host as the fallback for Windows AT injection (AX-D9) and decides that AT actions enter as full input sequences (AX-D11). The editing spec decides that TSF and third-party input-method DLLs run in that same UI host on Windows, always (ED-D5 (b)), which turns the UI host from a conditional fallback into an always-on process kind. Storage decides that the storage service holds history and bookmarks but the shell owns their schema (SG-D11, Q-SG-6). Networking decides that an interim OS trust store is disclosed in the site panel (NW-D24). Content blocking decides the per-site controls and the party counts the panel must show (CB-D19, CB-D20). Animation decides the R/S/X motion levels the settings page exposes (AN-D10). The shell UI design consolidates the visual and interaction direction (R1–R21, UI-1–UI-11) but is a design, not a spec: it has no process model, no staffing, no schema and no determinism contract. Closing that gap is ledger G-21.

**What success looks like.** A person can answer, from the browser alone and after the fact: what asked for this capability, in which container, for how long, what it protects, what happened when I allowed or denied it, and how to revoke it. A shell surface can be driven entirely by keyboard and by a screen reader, at 400% zoom, with no loss. A UI bug can be replayed from a recorded stream without the page's pixels. A shell process crash loses no tab, and a crashed shell is back within 2 s†. The shell stays inside 14 MiB† in the shell renderer, within a 16 ms† input-to-paint budget, and inside whatever Windows footprint baseline the owner accepts for FP-1 (9.5). No AI surface exists with AI off. No prompt can be drawn by page content, and no shell surface can approve one. Those are the SH targets of Section 8.

## 2. History: what came before and why it was built that way

The browser shell has been rebuilt every time the engine's process model changed, and each rebuild traded one kind of trust for another. The chronology below records the constraint that drove each design, not just its shape.

| Year | Project | What it did | Why that way | Fate and why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1993 | **NCSA Mosaic** | A single process drew the window, toolbar, URL field and page; the UI and content were the same native code | One process was the only practical model on a 4–16 MB machine, and there was no web engine separate from the application | Superseded by Netscape; the single-process assumption survived into every early browser and made the first security boundaries impossible |
| 1994–95 | **Netscape** | Native window chrome, a URL field, and the SSL padlock (1995) | SSL needed a visible signal; the browser was a native application and the page was a document | The padlock outlived its meaning: in 2022, 89% of 1,880 people still read it as "this site is safe" ([von Zezschwitz et al.](https://research.google/pubs/pub51481/)) |
| 1995–2001 | **Internet Explorer** | The shell integrated with the Windows desktop, history and favourites stores, and OS trust | The browser was a Windows feature; OS integration was the distribution strategy | The integration became an antitrust and security liability; the "browser as OS component" model was abandoned |
| 2000–04 | **Opera, Firefox, Safari** | Tabbed browsing, a search field beside the URL field, a bookmarks sidebar, a download manager window | Tabs answered window clutter; search answered the growing web; native widgets were what the platform offered | The tab strip, the combined address/search field and the download bubble are the surviving shapes |
| 2004–17 | **Firefox XUL** | The browser UI was XML (XUL) plus JavaScript, with chrome privileges; themes and extensions could change it | XUL let one codebase ship on three OSes and let extensions extend the UI itself | Firefox 57 (2017) removed legacy add-ons; about two-thirds of the catalogue was lost overnight, because a chrome-privileged UI cannot be sandboxed and multiprocess made its synchronous access unsafe ([extensions history](Izcal%20extensions.md)) |
| 2008 | **Chrome** | One native browser process, one sandboxed renderer per site, a tab strip, a combined omnibox and a "line of death" above the page | Renderer sandboxing was the security advance; native UI was the only way to own windows and stay outside the sandbox | The shape became the industry default; the native UI then had to duplicate the engine's accessibility and text stacks, and the line-of-death convention was imitated but not enforceable |
| 2011–17 | **Firefox Quantum/Photon** | Moved browser UI to HTML (browser.xhtml) rendered by Gecko, with a chrome-privileged document | One rendering stack, one accessibility tree, one set of web developers; faster UI iteration | It worked, but the UI document kept ambient authority and the accessibility rewrite had to follow: Cache the World (2021–23) made screen-reader loads up to about 21× faster on the largest tested tables, about 10× on large pages and 2–3× in everyday use ([Teh](https://www.jantrid.net/2022/12/22/Cache-the-World/)) |
| 2019–23 | **Chromium WebUI** | Settings, history, downloads and later the whole browser UI increasingly became WebUI pages with privileged bindings | WebUI is easier to build and theme than native views; the renderer already exists | WebUI re-created the XUL problem in a different place: a privileged renderer with bindings is ambient authority by another name; Edge's WebUI 2.0 migration (2024–25) was reported as a performance project, not a security one (Medium) |
| 2019–26 | **Permission UI** | Prompt bubbles replaced modal dialogs; quiet UI, gesture requirements, one-time grants, auto-revocation, then smaller and non-blocking prompts | Prompt volume and denial rates made interruption the problem; a gesture filters spam | The prompt is still the whole record; no browser ships a durable, searchable account of what was asked and what happened (gap) |
| 2022–25 | **Arc** | A Chromium browser with a rebuilt sidebar, Spaces, and a very different interaction model | Differentiation on chrome, for a team that believed tabs were broken | Arc's own letter reports that only 5.52% of DAUs regularly used more than one Space, Live Folders at 4.17% and Calendar Preview at 0.4%, and names a "novelty tax"; feature work stopped in 2025 ([The Browser Company](https://browsercompany.substack.com/p/letter-to-arc-members-2025)) |
| 2024–26 | **Edge, Chrome, Firefox** | Edge folded Copilot into default browsing; Chrome added AI Mode and image chips to the New Tab Page and a silent 4 GB model; Firefox shipped an AI kill switch that persists | AI was treated as a platform default, then as a switch when users objected | The counter-market is explicit: Firefox 148's master "Block AI enhancements" switch, Brave Leo opt-in, Vivaldi with no LLM features ([Mozilla](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/ai-controls/)) |
| 2026 | **Chrome 150/154, Firefox 157 (Nova), Safari 26** | Android site controls consolidated into a menu section (Chrome 150); HTTPS-first on by default for public sites (Chrome 154); Compact density restored as Window Density after a removal made without usage data (Firefox 157, late September); Firefox retired the "General" settings page for task-named groups after finding users could not predict where an option lived (June); Compact made the iOS default (Safari 26, September 2025) | Fewer trust entry points and fewer HTTP warnings; a measured reversal of a removal done without measurement; an admission that category-named settings had failed | Each change reduces a count, restores a control or regroups rows; none gives the person an account of what happened or why a state exists |

**Technology mismatches.** Five assumptions baked into existing shells no longer hold.

1. **The UI process can be trusted with ambient authority.** XUL chrome, WebUI bindings and native browser processes all assume the UI code is the browser. Izcal's shell is web content with zero ambient authority; the broker holds the secrets and judges every privileged request.
2. **A UI can own its window and be sandboxed.** Every serious engine sandboxes its renderers, so the trusted UI must live outside the sandbox. On Windows, third-party input-method and assistive-technology DLLs must load into the process that owns the window; putting that process in the broker puts untrusted DLLs next to every credential. The window owner must therefore be a **least-privilege UI host**, not the browser process (AX-D9 (b), ED-D5 (b)).
3. **A prompt inside the page window is a boundary.** The "line of death" is a convention enforced by drawing order, not by the OS. A separate owned window is a boundary the compositor cannot cross, and a broker hit-test against the rectangle it placed is a judgement the page cannot forge.
4. **The web is the only content that needs an accessibility tree.** Native UI duplicated the accessibility work and lagged. Rendering the shell as a page gives it the engine's semantic tree, keyboard model and test tools for free, provided the UI host exposes the window surface to AT correctly.
5. **A browser UI is a one-user, one-machine, always-foreground surface.** Multi-window, multi-monitor, mixed DPI, OS compositors (Wayland has no global coordinates), screen readers and input methods all change what "place a prompt above the page" means. The process model, not the drawing code, has to own that complexity.

The 2026 landscape is consolidation of site controls, fewer and smaller prompts, silent AI and friction removal in migration and updates. None of the major browsers competes on **explaining** browser state. The gaps below are the ones this spec acts on; the full evidence table is [gaps_opportunities.md](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/gaps_opportunities.md).

**What the incumbents show about differentiation.** Arc is the strongest warning: a feature that only 5.52% of DAUs regularly used more than one Space for cannot carry a browser, and its letter names the "novelty tax" directly. Chrome's vertical tabs (April 2026) and Firefox's tab groups (18 million cumulative users, vendor figure) are table stakes, not differentiators. The differentiation left is privacy posture and trust, which the incumbents' own prompt and AI controversies keep creating demand for. That is where this spec spends its effort: not new chrome, but an accountable one.

**The landscape in numbers.** Worldwide in September 2026: Chrome 66.36%, Safari 18.27%, Edge 6.04%, Firefox 2.8%, Samsung Internet 2.31%, Opera 1.77%; desktop Chrome 73.28%, Edge 10.46%, Firefox 5.31%, Safari 5.24% ([StatCounter](https://gs.statcounter.com/browser-market-share), Medium). A shell that wins trust with 2.8% of the market is still a shell for millions of people.

## 4. First principles

### 4.1 The tasks

Set aside the surfaces. A person uses a shell to do eight things:

1. **Go somewhere and come back**: navigate, go back, restore, find the tab again, recover a closed one.
2. **Decide about a site**: allow, deny, revoke, clear data, change a per-site rule, understand the connection.
3. **Understand an event**: why a page reloaded, why a prompt appeared, why a setting is the default, why a tab is using memory, why a page changed.
4. **Keep and re-find**: history, bookmarks, downloads, recently closed, search across them.
5. **Work in more than one context**: containers, windows, private windows, profiles later.
6. **Use the browser with their own body and technology**: keyboard, screen reader, magnification, zoom, reduced motion, forced colours.
7. **Recover**: from a crash, a bad update, a broken theme, a profile that will not start.
8. **Refuse**: keep AI off, avoid prompts, stop a nag, avoid telemetry, avoid lock-in.

### 4.2 The platform's primitives

Izcal's shell design follows from what the platform actually provides, not from what a browser UI usually looks like:

- **The engine renders HTML and CSS, and the shell is a page.** It gets the semantic tree, keyboard handling, text layout, zoom, forced colours and deterministic replay from the engine, not from a second UI toolkit (D-S2, B1, B8).
- **Every privileged action is a typed capability message.** The shell gets exactly the endpoints it is granted; it cannot open a file, a socket or another origin's store (B2, I-2, IPC-R12).
- **The broker owns consent.** It accepts an approval only for OS input it received and hit-tested against the prompt rectangle it placed, visible for at least 500 ms†, unoccluded by page or shell content (CON-R1, CON-R5).
- **The prompt renderer is separate, jitless and page-string-free.** It receives project JS and broker-formatted values, including the origin, and nothing else (CON-R2).
- **The OS window is owned by a process that holds no secrets.** On Windows that is the UI host (AX-D9 (b), ED-D5 (b)); on macOS and Linux the broker owns the window and the platform's accessibility surface (AX-D9 (a)).
- **Storage is a capability-addressed service with exact facts.** Usage, eviction reasons, write provenance and `persist()` decisions are queryable (SG-D4–SG-D7, SG-D11).
- **Extensions are capability tuples**, not booleans; growth is staged and re-granted (EX-D5).
- **Motion is graded R/S/X**, never removed, and the shell owns the setting (AN-D10).
- **The engine already holds the facts** the shell needs to explain itself: decisions, mutation records, semantic diffs, storage facts and spec anchors (B4, B5, B8, ANC-1, IIP).

### 4.3 Assumptions kept and broken

| Assumption inherited from existing browsers | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| The shell is a privileged native application | **Break** | It cannot be one and be safe; the shell is sandboxed web content, and the window belongs to a least-privilege host (D-S2, SH-D3) |
| The UI document is the browser and may hold secrets | **Break** | The shell never holds passwords, cookies, credentials or consent; the broker writes secrets into forms (CON-R4, SH-R1) |
| A prompt is drawn inside the page window above a line | **Break** | A separate owned window plus a broker hit-test is a boundary, not a convention (SH-D2) |
| The browser process can own the window, host IMEs and answer AT synchronously | **Break on Windows, keep on macOS/Linux** | Third-party TSF/AT DLLs must not share a process with credentials; macOS and Linux ATs do not inject (AX-D9, ED-D5) |
| The shell renderer may run an optimizing JIT | **Break** | The security scope makes the shell and prompt renderers jitless; the UI is project code and must fit the latency budget without an optimizing tier (SEC 6.7, SH-D4) |
| A prompt names a capability | **Break** | A prompt states the consequence, the scope, the container, what it protects, what it does not, and what happens on deny (6.4; SH-D2) |
| History and bookmarks are two lists | **Break** | One store with provenance and health answers "where did this come from and is it still what I saw" (SH-D8) |
| Settings are a list of knobs | **Break** | Every row states its default, why, effect and reset; no removal without measurement (SH-D15) |
| The UI is outside the recorded session | **Break** | UI state and the broker-to-shell stream are recorded at the capability boundary (SH-D10) |
| The recovery path can use the engine | **Keep the break from D-S2** | A native, minimal recovery window is the only surface that works when the engine does not (SH-D22) |
| Notifications are OS notifications first | **Break** | The broker decides, the shell presents in-product, and the OS notification is a delivery channel, not the record (SH-D17) |
| A command palette should replace the tab strip and omnibox | **Keep the tab strip and omnibox; test the palette** | Arc's 0.4–5.52% usage of signature surfaces is the warning; a palette is a Stage 2 trial, not a new paradigm (SH-D27) |
| The browser UI is English-only forever | **Keep for Stage 1, with all strings externalized** | Fluent bundles from the first commit; localization is a data change, not a code change (T12) |
| Telemetry is opt-out | **Break** | Telemetry is opt-in; crash reports are opt-out per crash (D-S4, SH-D29) |
| A theme may load remote assets | **Break** | User CSS is local and fetch-free; the shell image is sealed (SH-D6) |

## 5. Lessons learned

Each lesson is an idea or a constraint, never code or a template (M-1 part 3).

| Project or event | What happened | Lesson | How Izcal applies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Firefox XUL → 57** | A chrome-privileged UI could not be sandboxed; multiprocess broke synchronous extensions; about two-thirds of add-ons were lost | Privilege in the UI is a one-way door, and an API break is survivable only if the replacement reaches parity first | The shell has zero ambient authority from the first commit; extensions get typed capabilities before exotic surfaces (EX-D5) |
| **Chrome multiprocess** | Sandboxed renderers made the native browser UI a separate trusted world, duplicating accessibility, text and tests | A trusted UI outside the engine pays for every engine feature twice | The shell is a page; the UI host owns only the window, input and platform adapters, not the UI |
| **Chromium WebUI** | WebUI made settings and history easier to build but reintroduced privileged bindings in a renderer | A renderer with privileged bindings is ambient authority renamed | The shell reaches state only through typed endpoints; endpoints are bound to the shell origin (IPC-R12) |
| **Firefox Cache the World** | Synchronous AT pulls from renderers were up to about 21× slower on the largest tested table, about 10× on a large page and 2–3× in everyday use; caching in the parent fixed it | OS accessibility APIs are synchronous and pull-based; never make an AT query wait on a renderer | The shell's tree is computed by the engine and mirrored; the UI host answers from the mirror (AX-D3, AX-D4) |
| **Padlock and tune icon, 2022–23** | 89% of 1,880 people misread the lock; Chrome's tune icon was reported by the vendor as increasing site-control use without added confusion (vendor-reported, Medium) | A trust icon that implies more than the browser provides teaches the wrong lesson | No padlock, no trust icon; a neutral site-controls button and honest text (SH-D14) |
| **URL formats, 2019–21** | Fourteen EV and URL-format iterations produced no significant improvement in identity judgement; Chrome's domain-only field trial was reverted for not moving security metrics; people identify lookalike hosts 40% of the time | Formatting a URL is not a phishing control; it is a convention that costs almost nothing and stops nothing | Full URL with the registrable domain emphasised, per-label punycode, and the effort spent on an active lookalike warning instead (6.8; SH-10, SH-11) |
| **Notification prompts, 2019–21** | 74% of prompts, 12% granted on desktop; a gesture requirement raised first-time accepts to 52–56%; quiet UI cut unnecessary actions up to 30% | Volume is a design failure; a gesture is a filter, not a consent education | Prompts require activation; notifications use a quiet chip; one-time grants first; the ledger supplies the missing account (SH-D11, SH-D12) |
| **One-time grants, 2023** | Shipped widely; no vendor published uptake or regret data | A consent feature without measurement is an assumption | SH-13, SH-14 and SH-15 measure comprehension, coverage and regretted grants |
| **Tab overload, 2021** | 55% "can't let go"; about 25% crashed from too many; only 5.52% of DAUs regularly used more than one of Arc's Spaces | The problem is fear of loss and recovery, not the shape of the tab strip | Searchable recently closed, automatic snapshots, cold-tab discard, no forced grouping (SH-D27) |
| **Session restore** | About 1.2–1.3% of restorations fail, silently, and groups are lost | A silent recovery failure is worse than a visible one | Every restore failure states what was lost and what can be recovered (SH-21) |
| **Bookmarks, 2021** | 16% of bookmarked targets retrieved through bookmarks, 4% through the menu | A saved item that is never surfaced is a promise the browser does not keep | Provenance, health and "changed since saved" in one store (SH-D8) |
| **Find in page, 2003–20** | Firefox took about 17 years to match diacritics; Safari was reported to skip visible matches inside collapsed or `hidden="until-found"` content, and no browser said what it skipped | Matching defaults are invisible rules that outlive the release that chose them, and a count without a "what was skipped" line is a half-truth | Visible case, diacritics and whole-word options with diacritics-insensitive matching on by default; hidden content revealed, not skipped; skipped content disclosed; count announced (6.8, SH-32) |
| **Firefox Compact density, 2021–26** | Removed on an unmeasured assumption; restored in 2026 as Window Density (Mozilla/PCWorld, Medium for the version number) | Removing a setting without its own use measurement is a design error | No setting is removed without a measured use study; settings are versioned data (SH-D15, SH-22) |
| **First-run import, 2018** | Most users skipped import; slower import was associated with fewer later sessions | Import must be fast, local, visible and optional | Background import with preview, counts, undo and a local-only statement (SH-D19) |
| **Chrome Gemini Nano, 2026** | A ~4 GB model downloaded on a fresh profile with no user input; the off-switch was reported missing | AI-off must cover UI surfaces, downloads and model files, not just processes | Zero AI surfaces with AI off; no model download; the choice survives updates (SH-D16, SH-8) |
| **Arc, 2025** | Signature surfaces used by 0.4–5.52% of DAUs; the letter names a "novelty tax" | New interaction models do not carry a browser; trust and recovery do | No chrome as differentiation; a command palette only as a measured Stage 2 trial (SH-D27) |
| **Troubleshoot Mode wording** | Firefox's old Safe Mode wording misstated what it disabled; renamed in 2021 | A recovery option must say exactly what it turns off | Every recovery option states its effect and what it preserves (SH-D22) |
| **Wayland and multi-monitor** | Global window placement is not available on Wayland; mixed DPI and multiple monitors change prompt geometry | The window host must own placement and coordinate conversion, not the drawing code | The broker decides placement through per-OS window geometry; prompt hit-testing uses broker-held rectangles (SH-D2, measured at SH-M0) |

**The process kinds.** The Stage 1 process map gains three rows and one role change. The **UI host** becomes an always-on Windows process kind because ED-D5 (b) moves TSF and third-party input-method DLLs out of the broker whether or not accessibility needs it; AX-D9 (b), if AX-M0 selects it, gives it the AT adapters and the in-process MSAA/IA2 surface, while the content window and content-input path are its own for TSF regardless. It does not own prompt windows: the **broker owns every prompt window on every platform** and receives prompt input directly, so CON-R1's "input the broker itself received" stays true (SH-D3). On macOS and Linux the broker remains the owner of content windows too and hosts NSAccessibility and the sandboxed AT-SPI bridge (AX-D9 (a), AX-D12); there is no UI host there. The **prompt renderer** already exists in the security scope; this spec owns its documents, lifecycle and budget. The **recovery window** is a separate native process, not a document and not a mode of the shell. The **shell content GPU process** is the same device-less process GR-D28 already places in FP-1; it has a named owner and a budget, and it is **not on the prompt path**.

**The shell is a page, and its window is not its own.** The shell renderer draws the tab strip, toolbar, panels, settings and pages into a display list that the shell content GPU rasterizes; the compositor places that raster as the browser-UI layer above the page layers. The UI host (Windows) or the broker (macOS/Linux) owns the content window, presents the composited frame and captures OS input. The shell never calls a window API, never sees an OS event directly and never decides where a prompt goes. This is what makes the shell sandboxable and the prompt layer enforceable.

**Windows and the other platforms differ in exactly one place.** On Windows, the process that owns a content window must host third-party TSF and AT DLLs, so it is a separate least-privilege UI host even when no AT is running. On macOS and Linux, assistive technologies do not inject code into the browser, so the broker can own the content window and answer NSAccessibility or the AT-SPI bridge itself (AX-D9 (a), AX-D12); the shell renderer still draws the UI. The shell's own documents, endpoints, state model and tests are identical on all three. The difference is a platform adapter behind `hg-window`, not a branch in the shell.

**Why the broker owns the prompt window.** The prompt must not be paintable by page or shell content (CON-R5), and the broker must receive the input it judges (CON-R1). If the UI host or the shell owned the prompt window, a compromise there could either present false pixels or fabricate a click inside the affirmative rectangle; forwarding "raw input as untrusted data" does not restore provenance, because the forwarding process can synthesize the event. The broker therefore creates the prompt window itself on every platform, places it, receives its input directly, and the prompt renderer draws into that window with software rasterization. The prompt renderer never owns a window; the UI host is not involved. A compromised UI host can move or cover a content window, but it has no handle to the prompt window and no prompt input to forge.

**Failure and restart.** A shell renderer crash is contained: the broker keeps the tab model and the authoritative UI state, restarts the shell renderer in ≤ 2 s† and restores focus, open panels and scroll position from the shell-state snapshot (6.3). The content window never closes and no tab is lost. Two shell failures before first paint, or three within 60 s†, open the recovery window. A UI host crash closes the content windows; the broker relaunches it and rebuilds them from its state, without losing tabs, and **prompt windows are unaffected**. A prompt renderer crash dismisses that prompt as `aborted` (never as denied or allowed), records the failure in the ledger and lets the site ask again after a cooldown; it does not count as a denial. The recovery window is the only surface that survives a broker that cannot start; it is launched by a small platform launcher that is part of the installation, not by the broker.

**Startup.** The broker opens the profile first (keys, settings, container registry, tab model), then creates the content window owner and asks it for a window. The owner presents a flat browser-background surface immediately, so the person never sees a white flash or the previous frame. The broker launches the shell renderer with the sealed shell image and its endpoint handles; the shell paints the toolbar and tab strip from its initial snapshot; the compositor presents. Session restore runs in parallel and fills tabs progressively. The shell's first paint is a milestone (SH-1), not an afterthought: the window owner presents the background before the shell is ready.

### 6.2 The shell application

**One sealed image.** The shell is built as an immutable, content-addressed image: HTML documents, CSS, project JavaScript modules, Fluent bundles, inline SVG icons, the settings registry and the default themes. The image is sealed read-only, mapped clean and shared, and opened before the sandbox locks down. There is no runtime file access, no remote asset, no `@import`, no `url()` fetch and no third-party JavaScript. The image version equals the browser version; there is no live shell update, and a shell from an older version is never loaded.

**Documents and routes.** The shell is one internal origin, `izcal://shell`, with documents for the main window, settings, history, bookmarks, downloads, the prompt ledger, the extension manager and the about pages. Each document is a normal engine-rendered page with landmarks, a skip link, a visible focus style and a document title that names the browser and the surface. The main window document is the only one that receives the tab and navigation endpoints; a settings or history document receives the narrower endpoint set it needs. All documents come from the same sealed image and share the same CSP: `default-src 'none'; script-src 'self'; style-src 'self'; img-src 'self' data:; connect-src 'none'; frame-ancestors 'none'; base-uri 'none'; form-action 'none'`. Trusted Types are enforced for all DOM sinks.

**No framework, no build-time magic.** The shell uses standards: HTML, CSS, ES modules and the DOM. There is no UI framework, no virtual DOM, no CSS-in-JS and no transpiler in the shipped path (build tools that are not shipped are allowed under the toolchain exception, but the shipped JavaScript is the project's own and readable). The UI model is a small store per document: a read-only snapshot from the broker, a version per domain, and a render function that updates the DOM in place. Authoritative state is never optimistic; a click that closes a tab sends the request, and the tab disappears when the broker's event arrives. Local presentation state (which panel is open, focus, scroll) lives in the document and is snapshotted for crash recovery.

**Theming is CSS.** Built-in light and dark themes follow the OS by default. A user theme is a set of custom-property values edited in Appearance; an advanced field accepts CSS declarations limited to custom properties and ordinary properties on the shell document, validated at save time. The shell image rejects `@import`, any `url()` with a scheme, `behavior`, `-moz-binding`, and any at-rule that loads a resource. User CSS cannot reach the prompt renderer, which has its own sealed theme. A keyboard command and the recovery window can start with themes off. There are two densities, normal and compact, and container colours come from a palette checked for colour-vision deficiency, always paired with the container name.

**Native window frame.** The OS draws the window frame and title bar. The shell draws the tab strip and toolbar in the client area. Custom title bars are a Stage 2 experiment only if measurements show a benefit that survives the accessibility and window-management cost; by default the browser uses the platform's frame, snap layouts, window menu and title-bar accessibility. This is a deliberate departure from the 2026 fashion for merged tab strips: it keeps window controls, drag regions and system menus in the code the OS already tests.

**One shell renderer per profile.** All windows of a profile share one shell renderer. The UI's cross-window state (the tab model, the recent list, the ledger chip) is one trust domain; per-window renderers would duplicate memory and create cross-window consistency bugs for no security gain, because a shell renderer already sees every window's UI state. Reversal: if shell-renderer crashes or jank affect all windows at once in measurement, windows get their own renderer behind the same endpoint model (Q-SH-1).

**The shell's own inspection.** The shell renderer is an IIP target with the internal origin `izcal://shell`; DevTools can inspect it, with the same consent and audit rules as any target (DT 6.5). A person can open the shell's inspector from the about:diagnostics page. The shell's DOM and accessibility tree are never exposed to page content.

### 6.3 Shell state and capability endpoints

The broker is the source of truth for every browser fact. The shell holds a **read-only mirror** updated by typed events, plus local view state. Each domain has a monotonically increasing version; the shell sends the version it has, and a mismatch returns a full snapshot rather than a diff. On restart or reconnect the shell asks for all snapshots; it never assumes its previous state survived.

| Endpoint domain | What the shell may do | Producer | Events |
|---|---|---|---|
| `shell.window` | List windows, create and close a window, get and set bounds, request fullscreen, ask which display a window is on | Broker (window model); UI host executes | window added, removed, moved, resized, focused |
| `shell.tabs` | List tabs with title, URL display string, container, loading and crashed state; create, close, activate, move, pin, mute; search open tabs; list recently closed; restore | Broker (tab model) | tab added, removed, moved, updated, activated, crashed, restored |
| `shell.navigation` | Back, forward, reload, stop, navigate to a typed or pasted URL; read the current entry and its safety state | Broker (`hg-navigation`) | entry changed, history changed, load state |
| `shell.settings` | Read the registry with defaults, reasons, effects and scope; read and write values at global, container and site scope; reset; export and import settings | Broker (settings registry) | setting changed, policy changed, registry version |
| `shell.history` | Query by time, site, container and text; delete a row, a range or a site; clear; export | Storage service through the broker's typed catalogue interface | row added or removed (the shell redraws only when visible) |
| `shell.bookmarks` | List, add, edit, move, delete; read health and saved reason; check links on request; export | Storage service through the broker | bookmark added, changed, removed, health changed |

| `shell.containers` | List containers with name, colour and policy; create, rename, recolour, delete (with reassignment); assign a tab | Broker (container registry) | container added, changed, removed |
| `shell.extensions` | List installed extensions with name, version, enabled state and grants; enable, disable; revoke a grant; open options; open the directory page | Broker (extension service) | extension added, removed, changed, grant changed, approval pending |

| `shell.find` | Start, step, stop a find in the active tab; read match count and current match | Broker, coordinating renderers (ED-D17) | find result |
| `shell.import` | Preview what an import would bring and where; start, cancel, read progress; undo | Broker (importer) | import progress, done, failed |
| `shell.export` | Export history, bookmarks and settings in the documented open format, and credentials as CSV only after re-authentication | Broker and storage service | export progress, done, failed |

| `shell.why` | Ask a why-query about a shell or page state; get a plain sentence, a chain and a spec anchor | Broker (IIP) | answer or "record to answer" |
| `shell.telemetry` | Read the opt-in state and the published schema; open the local review page | Broker (D-S4) | telemetry state |

**Rules that bind every endpoint.** (1) Endpoints are bound to the shell origin and to the specific document set; a general renderer cannot request them (IPC-R12). (2) The shell never receives a password, cookie, token or page DOM through any endpoint; `shell.password` returns usernames only and the broker writes the secret (CON-R4). (3) Every write is validated in the broker against the same policy that governs the action, not against a shell-supplied claim. (4) Every query is cheap enough for the UI latency budget; a query that would scan the whole history returns a page and a cursor. (5) No endpoint accepts a page-supplied string as a trusted label; the broker formats every origin, title and filename for display (6.5). (6) The shell cannot navigate itself to web content; a web link opens a tab through `shell.tabs`. (7) `shell.apps` is bound to `izcal://shell` and to the app panel document; a renderer hosting an `app://<id>` document cannot request it, and app content reaches the shell only through typed intents (*Izcal app host* 6.8, A24).

**Shell-state snapshot.** The broker keeps, per window, a small snapshot for crash recovery: window bounds and display, active tab, open panel, panel scroll, focused element's stable id, find state and the shell document's route. It contains no page content and no secrets. The snapshot is updated at most 4 times a second† and on every interaction; it is written to memory only, not to disk, except window bounds and open-tab state, which the session store already keeps.

### 6.4 Consent, prompts and the prompt ledger

A prompt is a decision the browser asks a person to make on the browser's own surface. The security scope owns the decision rules (CON-R1–CON-R5); this section owns the surfaces, the classes, the wording, the record and the lifecycle.

**Prompt classes.** Every browser-issued prompt belongs to exactly one class. The class decides the surface, the risk level, the default focus, whether person-present evidence is required and how the ledger records it.

| Class | Examples | Surface | Risk | Default focus | Person-present evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Device classes (21 October 2026).** The prompt table gains three classes — `device-pairing`, `device-connection` and `device-write` — taking it from eleven to fourteen. All three are high-risk (CON-R7): the chooser is broker-owned and shows only candidates matching the page's filters, with no default selection and selection and confirmation as separate interactions; no page string appears except the broker-formatted origin and URL; protected classes are refused before a prompt. An AT-invoked approval is not accepted for any device prompt until AX-D11's person-present evidence exists; the AT action moves focus to the prompt only, and the chooser remains fully keyboard-operable. The capability-specific consequence text distinguishes a data write, a configuration write, firmware/SysEx and an NFC write.

**The prompt's anatomy.** A prompt never says only "wants to use your camera". In order, it states: the **origin** as the broker formats it (6.5), the **container** by name and colour, the **capability** in plain words, the **consequence** ("The site will be able to start audio and video while this tab is open"), the **scope** options ("Allow this time", "Allow on this site", "Block"), what it **protects and does not protect** in an expandable line, and what happens on deny ("The site will not be able to use the camera; it may still work without it"). A high-risk prompt names the asset and the actor ("the extension `ext://…` will be able to read every page you visit in Work, not only this one"). The wording rules are the shell UI design's: a label states a capability or a limit; vague reassurance is banned (WWW 2018). Every string comes from a Fluent bundle with typed placeholders; the broker fills the values, the prompt renderer never interpolates page text.

**Timing and occlusion.** A prompt becomes acceptable only after it has been visible and unoccluded for ≥ 500 ms† (CON-R1, CON-R5). An approval within 500 ms† of the prompt appearing, moving or changing text is rejected. The prompt does not move while it is open unless the window is resized by the person; a resize cancels the pending approval and restarts the clock. Low-risk prompts auto-dismiss after 30 s† with no decision; high-risk prompts stay until decided or the window closes. Expiry is recorded as "expired", never as a grant, and the site sees the same deny result as a refusal.

**Why 500 ms is not enough on its own.** The delay exists to break a carried-over click, but a determined page can still win a timing race: the Furious Clicker attack beat a 500 ms delay against a desktop browser 47% of the time ([security 4.4, 7.3](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/security_robustness.md)). Izcal therefore combines the delay with five other rules, and treats all six as one requirement: the broker hit-tests the event itself; no control is pre-selected and Enter does not approve; the prompt must have been **continuously** visible and unoccluded for the whole 500 ms†, with no gap; the input gesture must have **begun after the prompt appeared** (a pointer-down or key-down that predates the prompt is rejected even if the release lands on the affirmative control); a prompt that is hidden and re-shown, moved or resized restarts the clock and requires a new gesture; and **every** class rejects automation-synthesized input, not only the high-risk ones. Touch, pointer, keyboard, drag-and-drop and assistive-technology input all follow the same rules, with no exempt class. A drag that ends inside a prompt is never an approval.

**One prompt at a time.** Prompts queue; two are never shown at once and a prompt never bundles two capabilities. A queued prompt is recorded as queued and only becomes visible when the one before it is decided or expires. A third request while two are pending is **denied with a recorded `queued-overflow` result**; the site is told to retry after the visible prompt is decided, and the denial is not cached as a persistent deny. A site that is denied is not asked again for that (site, container) until the person clears the decision in the site panel; the same request after a deny is a bug, not a prompt. A **device chooser** is one prompt at a time: a second request for the same origin and device family waits for the open chooser; three denials in 60 s† trigger a 30 s† cooldown for that origin and family, during which the request fails with a plain error and no prompt.

**Quiet UI and prompt volume.** Notification permission is the largest prompt class and the least wanted. Izcal's rules: a site must have been interacted with before a notification prompt can appear; notifications use a quiet chip first, never a modal; one pending prompt per tab and at most two per profile (one visible, one queued); a site that is denied is not asked again for that (site, container) unless the person clears the decision in the site panel; auto-revocation retires grants a site has not used for 30 days† (notifications) or 90 days† (other capabilities), with a visible "expired" state in the site panel and ledger, never a silent removal. These rules follow the evidence that a gesture requirement and a quiet UI cut prompt volume without a large grant loss: the measured quiet-UI experiments reduced unnecessary notification actions by up to 30% for a 3.7–5% drop in grants ([Mozilla](https://blog.mozilla.org/futurereleases/2019/11/04/restricting-notification-permission-prompts-in-firefox/); [Bilogrevic et al.](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity21/presentation/bilogrevic); [security 1.2](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/security_robustness.md)).

**Specific consent surfaces.**

- **`persist()`** is a real prompt, remembered per (site, container), revocable, never a silent engagement heuristic (SG-D6). Its ledger entry says what persistent storage protects (eviction) and does not (backup, privacy from the site).
- **Paste** is a prompt-class popup at the pointer or caret, drawn by the prompt renderer, not the shell renderer (an amendment to ED-D10's wording). It enables after 1 s†, dismisses after 5 s† **unless it has keyboard focus or pointer hover, in which case the timer is suspended and restarted on leave, and it can be extended with Enter or Space** (WCAG 2.2.1: the delay is a security measure, not an essential time limit, so it must be extendable); it has no persistent grant, and is skipped only when the OS clipboard's change counter equals the one recorded at a write by the same (origin, container, tab). A write by another application, origin or container always shows it.
- **Extensions** prompt through the same layer, with the extension origin and requested capabilities formatted by the broker; the shell's Extensions page can request a change and revoke, never approve (EX-D5, security scope 6.6a).
- **Phishing and certificate interstitials** are full-window prompt documents whose content and decision rules belong to the phishing protection spec and the networking spec; the shell owns the window, the accessibility, the timing and the ledger. The safe action is prominent and "proceed" sits behind Details, following the field result reported as raising adherence from 37% to 62%, labelled Medium because the paper's abstract describes the same change as "nearly 30% more total users chose to remain safe" ([Felt et al., CHI 2015](https://research.google/pubs/improving-ssl-warnings-comprehension-and-adherence/); [security 2.3](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/security_robustness.md)). The same warning is not repeated for the same event; attention drops sharply from the second exposure.
- **Credentials and payments** never put a secret in the prompt renderer or the shell. The prompt confirms the action; the broker writes the secret into the target renderer's form (CON-R4, SH-R1). Passkeys follow the same path.
- **File-system access** follows SH-R9: the prompt names the directory or file, and the grant produces **typed broker-minted handles** bound to that grant — never a raw path — which the broker can revoke; the broker refuses system and profile directories. The site panel shows the granted scope and a handle count, and the ledger records the scope, not the path.
- **The protected-media switch** names the OS engine and its patch level ("Playing protected video using Windows' media engine; Microsoft's patch level applies"), with one click back and a per-container memory (UI R10, SH-R4).
- **High-risk approvals** require person-present evidence recorded as a class in the ledger: a fresh OS input event in the foreground prompt, not synthesized by automation, and for AT actions the evidence AX-D11 defines. A prompt cannot be approved by a message from the shell or the prompt renderer.

**The prompt ledger.** Every prompt creates a ledger entry, whether it is shown, expired, denied, allowed, revoked or failed. The ledger is the durable half of consent: the prompt is the moment, the ledger is the account.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| `id` | Monotonic, no random component; stable across export |
| `time` | Recorded clock; UTC with the session's zone offset |
| `requestor` | Site origin (broker-formatted display string and canonical origin), extension `ext://<id>` with its name, agent task id, or "browser" for an internal prompt |
| `container` | Container id, name and colour at the time of the prompt |
| `capability` | Typed capability tuple (camera, microphone, geolocation, notifications, clipboard-read, persistent-storage, file-system with the granted scope, document-password, extension capability, agent action, protected-media, external-protocol, dangerous-download) |
| `scope` | Once, this site, this site in this container, always (only where the capability defines it), or "n/a" for interstitials |
| `class` and `risk` | The class table's values |
| `decision` | Allowed, denied, expired, dismissed, failed, aborted (prompt renderer or broker failure before a decision), revoked (a later event, linked to the original); a queue overflow is recorded as `denied` with reason `queued-overflow` |
| `evidence` | `os-input`, `keyboard`, `at-action`, `person-present`, `automation-rejected`; which rule accepted the input |
| `protects` / `does_not_protect` | The two strings shown, kept verbatim so the record explains what the person was told |
| `expiry` | Absolute time for a grant, or none |
| `uses` | Count and last use time for grants, updated by the broker, not the site |

**Device entries (21 October 2026).** A `device-pairing`, `device-connection` or `device-write` entry additionally records the device family, the sanitized device label, a reference to the device digest (never the digest itself), the capability set, the grant scope and expiry, the agent/task id where applicable, and the OS backend id. The record makes a device grant auditable without making the ledger a device-identifier database.

**App entries (registered 21 October 2026, *Izcal app host* A24).** An install, a capability-growth request and a revocation each create a ledger entry. The requestor is `app://<id>` with the directory name, or the file name and "unverified publisher" for a file install, and the `capability` field holds `app-install`, `app-growth` or `app-revocation`. The growth entry names the new capability alone — the same diff line the install verifier computed — so a grant that widened after install is auditable against the version that requested it; a revocation entry links to the entry it ends. The app id is a display label, never a secret.

**Retention and clearing.** The ledger is stored locally, encrypted at rest, in the storage service. Each entry lives under the key of the container it names (SG-D7's per-container AEAD discipline); a profile-scoped, encrypted index holds only entry ids, times and container ids so the ledger page can list across containers. This is a documented exception — the ledger is cross-container by design — and the storage spec's amendment table carries it. The default retention is 90 days†, configurable (7 days, 30 days, 90 days, 1 year, until cleared). "Clear browsing data" with "permissions and site data" selected clears the ledger and revokes the grants; clearing a single site revokes its grants and removes its ledger entries unless the person unticks "also remove the security log". The ledger is never uploaded, never exposed to page content or extensions, and is readable by DevTools only through a scoped IIP domain with the same consent as any inspection session. Export includes it only when the person selects it.

**The ledger's surfaces.** The prompt itself has "What this protects" and "Details"; Details opens the ledger entry in the main window. The site panel has a Permissions section with a "History" link filtered to that site. The ledger page lists entries by time, filters by site, container, capability and decision, and shows the use count. A grant row has "Revoke". A "Why did I see this?" link on any prompt opens the ledger entry after the decision, never before, so the explanation cannot become a second prompt. The ledger is searchable by origin and container name.

**Targets.** SH-13 (prompt comprehension), SH-14 (ledger completeness), SH-15 (ledger usability and privacy), SH-16 (prompt timing and occlusion). The ledger's cost is bounded by one entry per prompt and a retention policy; the first measurement is at SH-M2.

### 6.5 The trusted prompt layer and unspoofability

**What "trusted region" means now.** The shell UI design's trusted region (the area above the page's top edge, UI-10) remains the region for the browser's own chrome: the tab strip, toolbar, address bar and site panel. It is not the prompt surface. A prompt is drawn in a **separate OS window owned by the broker**, whose content is produced by the prompt renderer, and whose placement, size, lifetime and input acceptance are decided by the broker. This is a stronger boundary than drawing above a line: the page compositor has no surface to paint into, the shell renderer has no endpoint that creates or draws a prompt, the UI host has no prompt window to move, and the broker's hit test is against geometry it computed itself.

**The window.** The **broker** creates the prompt window on every platform. On Windows it is an owned popup (`WS_POPUP` with the content window as owner, no caption, a browser-owned frame) created by the broker; the broker also asks the UI host to disable input to the content window while the prompt is open, but it does not rely on that for consent. On macOS the broker creates a child window (`addChildWindow:ordered:`) of the content window; on Linux a transient window for the parent surface. On Wayland the compositor positions an `xdg-popup` relative to its parent, and the client does not control global coordinates, so the broker asks for a position and treats the compositor's result as authoritative. The prompt window moves with the browser window, hides when it is minimised, is not a global always-on-top window, and is modal to its content window: OS focus moves to it and content input is blocked while it is open.

**The surface.** The prompt renderer rasterizes its own prompt into a surface **in software, in-process**; no GPU process and no compositor is on the prompt pixel path (GR-D28). The surface is small (a prompt document, not a page), the budget is sized for software raster, and the pixels have exactly one producer, the jitless prompt renderer. The window has a browser-drawn frame, no page title, no page icon and no favicon. Its accessible name is the prompt's heading, not the page's title. The origin appears as text inside the document, formatted by the broker, never as an image the page supplied.

**Broker-owned prompt layout.** The broker does not accept the renderer's account of where the buttons are. Each prompt class has a **broker-owned template**: an ordered list of controls with typed labels, a broker-computed layout in CSS pixels (the same layout the renderer is required to produce), and the affirmative and deny rectangles derived from it. The broker sends the template and values to the prompt renderer; the renderer renders that template and may report its measured geometry only as a **divergence check**. If the renderer's reported control rectangles differ from the broker's by more than a rounding tolerance, the broker aborts the prompt (`aborted`) rather than judge it. Acceptance is always against the broker's rectangles.

**The acceptance path.** The broker receives prompt input directly from the OS on every platform; the UI host is not in this path. The broker: (1) checks that the event is inside the current prompt window's client rectangle in screen coordinates; (2) checks that the window is visible, focused and not covered by any Izcal-controlled surface or OS-reported external occluder; (3) checks that the prompt has been **continuously** visible and unoccluded for ≥ 500 ms† since it appeared, moved or changed text, and that the **gesture began after the prompt appeared** (tracked from pointer-down or key-down, not release); (4) checks that the event is a real OS event and rejects automation-synthesized input for **every class**, not only high-risk; (5) maps the point to the **broker's affirmative rectangle** and accepts only if it is inside that rectangle and the event targets the affirmative control id the broker assigned; (6) records the decision. Keyboard approval requires broker-observed focus on the affirmative control and a broker-observed key event, mapped by control id, not by screen point. An AT action is accepted only as the full keyboard or pointer sequence AX-D11 defines, and **only once AX-D11 is decided by the security owner**; until then high-risk AT approval is not accepted. The renderer's report of "pressed" is never consulted. A message from the shell, the UI host or the prompt renderer claiming approval is ignored (CON-R1). Input provenance is the broker's record of the session principal and the input queue that produced the event, not `event.isTrusted`: WebDriver-style automation input is trusted by the platform, so a trusted event from the automation principal is still not a person ([determinism 4.1–4.5](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/determinism_recording_testing.md)).

**Coordinates and multiple monitors.** The broker holds the prompt window rectangle and the display layout; the UI host reports content-window moves, resizes, DPI changes and display changes. A prompt is placed on the display containing the content window, centred on it, and clamped to the work area. On a display change while a prompt is open, the prompt is re-placed and the 500 ms† clock restarts. Screen coordinates are converted in one place, the broker, with the DPI scale of the display the window is on.

**What can still happen.** A compromised UI host cannot touch the prompt window or its input; it can move or cover the content window, and it can misreport content-window geometry, which moves a prompt but never approves one. A compromised shell renderer cannot draw in the prompt window at all; its address-bar display can lie, which is the stated shell-compromise residual (6.18), and the prompt's own broker-formatted origin is the authoritative display at decision time. A compromised prompt renderer can change what the person reads, but the broker judges only its own template rectangles; a renderer that reports divergent geometry is aborted. A compromised GPU is not on the prompt path. Another application can cover the prompt; the broker asks the OS for occlusion where the platform reports it and rejects approval while an external window covers the prompt, and where the platform cannot report external occlusion (Wayland) the residual is stated and the prompt is not approved without focus. A compromised operating system, a screen recorder or a malicious accessibility client is outside this boundary and is covered by the security scope's threat model. Those residuals are stated in the threat model and tested by the red-team suite (SH-11).

**Accessibility of the prompt.** The prompt window is a dialog/alertdialog in the accessibility tree, with a heading, a description, the origin and container as text, and buttons with accessible names. Focus moves to the least-privileged action; Escape dismisses without granting; the document is reachable by screen reader browse mode. The AT surface of the prompt window exposes only the prompt renderer's tree, never page content. High-risk prompts accept an AT action only when AX-D11's evidence is present; otherwise the AT action moves focus and the person presses the control with a full input sequence. The prompt is announced once; a live region is not used for a new window, because the window focus event is the announcement.

### 6.6 The site panel

The site panel is the one place every per-site control lives. It opens from a neutral **site-controls** button beside the address bar (no padlock, no shield, no brand colour), with a keyboard shortcut and a screen-reader name that includes the current site and container. It is a complementary landmark inside the main window document, not a separate window; Escape closes it and returns focus to the button. It never nests a modal inside itself; destructive actions ask in the prompt layer.

**Panel behaviour.** The panel is a list of rows, each with a label, a state and an action; a row that cannot act says why. The panel's data is fetched when it opens and updated by events while it is open; a site-panel query is bounded (counts and top parties, not the full decision log; "list" opens the blocked-parties page with a cursor). The panel does not show a favicon or a page-supplied icon in its identity row: the site's own icon is shown only in the tab, decoded and sandboxed, and never in a prompt. "Clear data for this site" is a destructive action with a summary of what will be removed; it uses the prompt layer when it is triggered from a page-controlled context, and a plain confirmation when the person opened the panel deliberately.

**Device indicator (21 October 2026).** The toolbar carries a device-active indicator: a per-tab icon while a device is open, a dot when a persistent device grant exists, and a click-through list of the tab's open devices with **Disconnect** and **Forget**. It is broker and shell UI, never page-drawable; the page cannot read, draw over or hide it (SH-35).

### 6.7 History and bookmarks: one store with provenance and health

**Ownership and closing Q-SG-6.** The storage service holds the records and executes the index operations; **this spec owns the schema, the index definitions, the query semantics, the retention rules and the UI**. That closes Q-SG-6 with the answer the storage spec anticipated, and it needs one storage-service interface addition: a typed **catalogue** interface for ordered range scans, prefix scans, token-index postings and multi-index candidates, with bounded result sizes and a cursor. The shell never opens a file or holds a storage key; it sends queries through the broker to the storage service.

**Why one store.** History and bookmarks answer the same question at two strengths: "where did this row come from, and is it still what I saw?" A bookmark is a visit the person deliberately kept; a visit is a bookmark they did not make. Keeping them in one record model with one provenance vocabulary means the answer does not depend on which list the person opens, and "changed since saved" can use the same fingerprint as "changed since last visit". The evidence says this is the primary surface, not an archive: 50–80% of browsing is revisitation, and in a 250-target study only 16% of bookmarked pages were reached through the bookmark facility and 4% through the folder menu ([Adar et al., CHI 2008](http://cond.org/chi1159-adar.pdf); [Bergman et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0961000620949652); [usability 5.1–5.2](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). A folder tree cannot carry that load, which is why the default history view is search and recent, with provenance and health on every row.

**Record types.**

| Record | Fields | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `page` | Canonical URL, scheme, registrable site, origin; **container id**; first seen; last seen; visit count; last title; last content fingerprint; health; last health check | One per **(canonical URL, container)** (fragment stripped for identity, kept on the visit). A page visited in two containers has two records, so clearing one container removes its title, fingerprint and health without touching the other; there is no global page row with a losing "last container" |
| `visit` | Page id; visit time (recorded clock); container id; **provenance**; redirect chain (each hop's URL and status); referrer visit id; source visit id; tab id (not persisted after the session); title at the time; content fingerprint at the time; load result | Private-window visits are never written; a private visit does not update the page record |
| `bookmark` | Page id; title; folder id; created; modified; **provenance** (the creating visit's provenance, or `import`/`typed` for a bookmark created without a visit); saved reason (optional, person-authored); container it was saved from; fingerprint at save; health; last check; optional extract (≤ 2 KB†, opt-in) | A bookmark is a page plus a saved intent; deleting a bookmark keeps the visit history |
| `folder` | Id, name, parent, order, created, modified | A tree, but the UI defaults to search and recent, not to the tree |
| `aggregate` | Container id; registrable site; day; visit count; last visit time | Retained 12 months† when the person keeps aggregates; contains no URL, title or fingerprint; cleared and exported with history |
| `download` | Owned by the broker's download store; the shell shows it | Not part of history; clearing downloads does not clear history |

**Provenance.** The provenance value is one closed vocabulary, owned by this schema and quoted identically by SH-D9, SH-19 and the export schema: `typed`, `link`, `redirect`, `form`, `reload`, `back-forward`, `restore`, `extension`, `agent`, `bookmark`, `import`, `external`, `unknown`. `unknown` is written only when an older migration cannot recover a value; it is never guessed. The record keeps the chain: a redirect chain with each hop and status, the referring visit for a link, the extension id for an extension-initiated navigation, the task id for an agent. The UI renders a sentence ("You opened this from a link on example.com", "This redirected twice from a short link", "Restored from your session on 19 October"), with the chain available under Details. The agent's origin set is shown; an agent-opened page is never attributed to the person. A schema test rejects any value outside the enum.

**Content fingerprint and health.** The renderer computes a fingerprint of the page's main content (document title plus the text of the main landmark, or the body when there is none, normalized for whitespace, truncated at 64 KiB†, SHA-256) and passes it as a typed value with the visit. The shell never receives the text. On every revisit, the broker compares the fingerprint with the stored one and sets `changed`; the history row and the bookmark show "changed since you last saw it". A diff needs the text, so it is available only for bookmarks whose owner opted into the 2 KB† extract, or from the live page's semantic diff against the saved extract. Link health is checked **on visit** and when the person presses "Check links"; there is no background crawl by default. Health values: `ok`, `redirects` (with the target's registrable site), `gone` (404/410), `error`, `unreachable`, `unknown`. A redirect across registrable sites is marked "now goes to a different site", which is the case that matters for a saved login or a saved article.

**Indexes and queries.** Indexes: visit time (descending), page URL hash, site, container, title tokens (lowercased, Unicode-normalized, stop-words removed; postings store page ids), bookmark folder and order, bookmark health. Query primitives: `range(index, start, end, limit)`, `prefix(index, prefix, limit)`, `postings(token, limit)`, `intersect(candidate_sets, limit)`, `delete_range`, `clear_site`, `clear_container`, `clear_all`. Ranking is computed in the shell from a bounded candidate set (top 200† by recency and visit count, then text score), so the storage service never runs a search engine. The query latency target is SH-20.

**Surfaces.** The address bar carries a **Star** control: one press saves the page to the last-used folder, a second press or the adjacent menu edits the folder and the saved reason; the star is a button, never a toggle with a hidden state, and its accessible name includes the page title. The **bookmarks bar** is hidden by default and toggled from the View menu or Settings; it is a toolbar (one tab stop, arrow keys), folders are menus, and a middle-click or Ctrl+click opens in a new tab. The **Bookmark manager** (`izcal://shell/bookmarks`) lists search first, folders second; each row shows provenance, health and "changed since saved", and drag-and-drop has a keyboard equivalent. The **History page** (`izcal://shell/history`) defaults to search and recent, grouped by day, with filters for site, container and date; every row opens the same provenance sentence and health state as the ledger, and a "Check links" action runs only on request. The **new-tab page** is local: no sponsored tiles, no remote favicons, no network request on open; it shows a search field that contacts the chosen engine only on Enter, the person's top sites from history (opt-out in Settings), and recently closed tabs. All four surfaces have empty, loading and error states, keyboard paths and a visible focus indicator; none is a dead end (SH-12).

**Retention and privacy.** History retention defaults to 90 days† (configurable: 7, 30, 90, 365, forever) and applies to visits; aggregates are kept for 12 months† and contain no URL or title; bookmarks are kept until deleted. Private windows write nothing; a private download is listed only for the session unless the person keeps the file, and the file's history entry is not written. Clearing history can clear a time range, a site, a container or everything; clearing a container's history leaves other containers' rows. The store is encrypted at rest under SG-D7: page, visit, bookmark and aggregate rows live under their container's key; only the profile-scoped schema metadata uses the profile key. It is included in export only when the person chooses. There is no sync in Stage 1; the export format is the lock-in answer (6.12). In-product, the History page says "History stays on this device" and "Private windows are not saved here".

**Schema versioning.** The schema has a version; migrations are forward-only, run in the storage service, keep the old store until the new one commits, and are preceded by an automatic export to a local file the person can delete. A migration failure leaves the old store readable and reports the failure in Settings > About > Diagnostics; it never silently starts empty.

### 6.8 Tabs, session, navigation and the Why? surface

**The tab model.** The broker owns tabs as records: stable id, window, opener, container, current entry, history index, title, URL, load state, crashed state, muted state, pinned state, group (Stage 2), and a sleeping flag. The shell renders the strip from a snapshot and applies events; it never reorders optimistically. A tab move is a broker operation that returns the new order.

**App windows and their chrome (registered 21 October 2026, *Izcal app host* A24).** An app window is a window record whose content is an `app://<id>` document, and the shell owns its chrome. The window model and `shell.window`/`shell.tabs` treat it like any other window, but the shell draws an **identity strip** carrying the directory name and icon bound to the app id and author key, plus the app id, and app content cannot cover or overlay it; there is no address bar. The strip and the window controls are shell UI, and a package that draws a fake address bar, a fake prompt or another app's identity is refused at review (A26; *Izcal app host* 6.8). A link to the web from an app opens a new browser tab and a link to another app opens that app's window with its own chrome, so the app window itself navigates nowhere and its chrome never describes a URL the content did not receive. App windows are listed and uninstalled in the app panel (`shell.apps`), and an app's grants are shown and revoked there.

**The tab strip.** Horizontal by default. The strip is a tablist with roving tabindex, arrow-key movement, Home/End, and a context menu reachable by the Menu key and by right-click. Each tab exposes its title, container name and colour, mute state, and a close button with an accessible name that includes the title. A tab that crashes shows a neutral "Page stopped" state and a Reload action; it does not vanish. A sleeping tab shows a moon glyph with the text "Sleeping"; activating it reloads and says so. Vertical tabs, split view and tab groups are Stage 2, one-click and persistent, never forced (UI R18, UP5). Pinned tabs are Stage 1. This shape follows the evidence: in a logged study 17 of 21 people switched tabs more often than they used Back, 57.4% of tab sessions contained at least one switch, and the opt-in organization features that reached the market were used by 0.4–5.52% of daily users ([Dubroy & Balakrishnan, CHI 2010](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/ravin/papers/chi2010_tabbedbrowsing.pdf); [Huang & White, 2010](https://jeffhuang.com/papers/ParallelBrowsing_HT10.pdf); [usability 1.1, 1.3, 1.7](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Tab switching is navigation; the strip must be fast and searchable before it is organizable.

**Recently closed and tab search.** Every tab close, window close and group close is recorded with its container and its last title; the recently-closed list is searchable and restores the whole set (for a group, its structure, not only its links). The list survives a crash and a restart. Tab search searches open tabs by title, URL and container, with keyboard selection; it is not a web search and never sends a query anywhere.

**Session store and restore.** The session store is written by the broker, atomically, every 5 s† when dirty and on every tab open, close, move or navigation. It contains window bounds and display, tab order, active tab, pinned state, container, group membership (Stage 2) and each tab's current entry. It contains no page content, no form values and no scroll position (page scroll restoration is a renderer responsibility and is opt-in per site). On start, restore is progressive: the window and strip appear first, then tabs load in a deterministic order (active, then left-to-right). A failed restore is never silent: the shell shows a "Session restore" notice with the count restored, the count that could not be restored, the reason for each failure (crash, unreadable entry, profile lock, out of memory) and a "Show recently closed" action. Restore never re-grants a permission: one-time grants do not survive a close, a crash or a restore, and a persistent grant that was auto-revoked is re-asked only when the site requests it again. Restore is a small, versioned, deliberately lossy state model, not a recording: form values are not stored by default and scroll restoration is the renderer's opt-in, so the restore notice says when a form or scroll position was not restored rather than implying the page is exactly as it was left. Restore attempts for one session are bounded; after three failures the broker offers a fresh session and the recovery window instead of retrying a corrupt file. SH-21 measures this.

**Cold-tab discard.** A tab may be discarded only when its renderer reports no unsaved form state, no active download, no audio capture and no pending permission prompt. Discarding keeps the tab record, its title and its history index; activating it reloads and shows a one-line "This tab was sleeping to save memory; reloading". The person is never asked to close tabs to save memory; the browser does the work (UP2, UI R18).

**The address bar.** The display string is produced by the broker from the canonical URL: the registrable domain is emphasized, the rest is shown in full, and on focus the full URL is selected for editing. The broker applies the IDN and bidi display rules from the networking and security specs: mixed-script domains show their punycode form, bidi controls are isolated, and the display cannot be reordered by a page. There is no EV treatment, no padlock and no colour-coded trust. Plain HTTP shows a neutral "Not secure" chip; a certificate error shows "Connection not secure" and opens the interstitial if the person proceeds. The container chip (name plus colour), permission chip, protected-media label, Tor route label and JavaScript mode label sit before or after the display string as the design specifies (4.2). Autocomplete candidates come from open tabs, history, bookmarks, typed URLs and the chosen search engine, in that order, with a provenance label on each; sponsored or AI-generated suggestions do not exist. Typing a search sends it to the person's chosen engine only after Enter, never on each keystroke. A late suggestion never silently becomes the default match: the selected row is visibly stable, and a suggestion that arrives after the person has started to act changes nothing until they move the selection. The address bar never shows a page-supplied title as the URL. The display is honest but not a phishing control: fourteen tested EV and URL formats produced no significant improvement in identity judgement, Chrome's domain-only field trial was reverted, and people identify lookalike hosts correctly only 40% of the time, so the security work belongs in the active lookalike warning and the site panel, not in typography ([Thompson et al., USENIX Security 2019](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity19/presentation/thompson); [Reynolds et al., CHI 2020](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-identity-confusion-with-uniform-resource-locators/); [usability 2.5–2.7](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). The address bar is shell UI, so a compromised shell renderer can display a false origin; that residual is stated in 6.18 and is why SH-11 covers page-controlled spoofing, not shell compromise, and why the broker's own prompt shows the authoritative origin at decision time.

**Error pages.** Navigation errors are shell documents, not page content: `izcal://shell/error` with a broker-formatted URL, a plain reason, the error class, "Try again", "Go back" and Details (the network error code and the TLS alert, if any). A page cannot navigate to the error document; the broker produces it. The error page shows the container and the route, so a Tor or proxy failure is not mistaken for a site failure.

**Fullscreen and zoom.** Fullscreen hides the shell chrome, keeps an exit control reachable by keyboard and by pointer at the top edge, and announces the state; the person can always exit with Esc and F11. Page zoom is per site and global, 25–500%, with a reset; shell UI text zoom is separate (100–200%) and never scales page content. At 400% page zoom the page reflows under WCAG 1.4.10; the shell's own settings, history and panel surfaces remain usable at 320 CSS px equivalent (SH-27).

**The find bar.** Find is broker-coordinated (ED-D17): the shell draws a find bar in the main window, the broker sends the query to every renderer of the tab's browsing context group, and results are merged. The bar shows the current match and count, next/previous, case, diacritics, whole word and regular expression (with a clear invalid-pattern state); matching is diacritics-insensitive by default, the options are visible rather than hidden in a menu, and the bar remembers its options per profile and its query per tab while the tab is open. A match inside collapsed `<details>`, `hidden="until-found"` or otherwise clipped content is revealed and scrolled to, not skipped; if a match cannot be revealed, the bar says so ("2 matches are inside collapsed content") instead of returning a shorter count without explanation ([usability 7.4–7.5](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). The count is a polite live region, so a screen reader hears it change. It says "Find cannot see text drawn in canvas" when a page reports canvas text, rather than silently returning zero. Escape closes the bar and returns focus to the page. Find never searches the shell itself; the settings, history and ledger pages have their own search fields. The find bar is shell UI above the page edge, not a prompt; it cannot be imitated by the page because it is in the shell's layer. (A page can still draw a fake bar in its own area; the shell's bar is in browser chrome, and the browser does not claim the page area is trusted.)

**The Why? surface.** The shell's distinctive product is not more chrome; it is an accountable browser that can say why the state on screen is what it is. "Why?" is one surface, reachable from a keyboard shortcut, from the context menu of a tab, a site-panel row, a prompt's Details, a settings row ("Why this default?"), a download row, an error page and About > Diagnostics. It answers a bounded set of questions about the current state: why this navigation or reload happened; why this prompt appeared and what decided it; why this setting has this value and who set it; why this tab is using memory or was discarded; why this request was blocked; why this page changed since the last visit; why this download was flagged; why the session did not fully restore.

An answer has three layers, in this order: **one plain sentence** ("This tab reloaded because the site's service worker updated and the page asked to reload"); **the chain**, as typed facts with their producer and time, each traceable to a broker record (navigation log, prompt ledger, setting source, resource attribution, blocking decision log, storage eviction log, semantic diff); and **the anchor**, the specification rule or internal decision that produced the fact, shown only when the person asks for it. The chain never contains page-supplied prose; titles and origins use the broker's display types (6.5). Every answer ends with what can be done next (open the ledger entry, open the blocking decision, open the relevant setting, open DevTools with the same query) and a "Copy" action with an origin-only option.

The rules are as important as the answers. An answer is never fabricated: if the broker did not record the fact, the surface says "not recorded" and offers "reload to record" for the next occurrence, which starts a scoped diagnostic capture with the person's consent. Live answers are limited to the current state. Recorded answers state their source and that source's retention: session-scoped facts (the current navigation log, resource attribution) are deleted with the session; ledger and history answers come from the retained ledger (90 days†) and history store (90 days†) and say so; blocking answers come from the blocking decision log with its own retention. The answer never implies a shorter life than the record it reads. The surface is local, makes no network request, and is never exposed to page content or extensions. It is not a prompt: it never asks for a decision, and it cannot approve anything. The target is SH-33: a checked sample must be correct, and the median live answer must arrive within 1 s†. This is ranked gap 2 in the gaps note and the strongest use of the broker's typed facts ([gaps O2, 6](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/gaps_opportunities.md)).

**Downloads, updates, default browser and notifications** are specified in 6.10–6.13; **settings** in 6.9; **onboarding, recovery and about** in 6.14; **extensions** in 6.15.

### 6.9 Settings

**The model.** A setting is not a row in a list; it is a documented default with a reason, a scope, an effect and a reset. Every row in the registry carries: a stable id, its group, its type and allowed values, its default, the reason for the default in one sentence, what changes when it is switched, which other settings or surfaces it affects, its scopes (global, container, site), whether it can be set by policy, whether it needs a restart, the version it was introduced in, its measurement status (observed, inferred, not measured), and the test or study that justifies keeping it. A row without a reason is not shipped.

| Group | What it contains | Default posture |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy and security** | Tracking protection level, HTTPS-first, GPC, do-not-track (explained as a request, not a promise), fingerprinting protection, safe browsing, clear-on-close choices, certificate trust disclosure, **Set as default** | The protective option is the default; each row says what it does not protect |
| **Containers (Stage 2 editing)** | The named containers, their colours, policies, network routes, JavaScript mode, deletion and reassignment | Stage 1 shows a **read-only name-and-colour chip** and four fixed defaults; editing, routes, deletion and per-container policy are Stage 2. No blank policy editor (UI R7) |
| **Permissions and site settings** | Per-site permissions, the ledger, auto-revocation periods, clipboard, storage, camera/mic/location defaults, file-system grants with their scope. Stage 1 has **no notification row**: delivery and quiet UI are Stage 2 and the group stays absent until they ship | Ask by default; one-time first; grants are per (site, container) |
| **Devices** | Per-family policy, the **Experimental device APIs** per-origin test-site list (max 20† origins, cleared on profile reset, locked in managed profiles), the identifier-exposure default, all device grants and "clear all" | Off by default; no API is generally exposed in Stage 1; NFC is Android-only; grants expire after 90 days† since last use (maximum 1 year†) |
| **Appearance** | Theme, density, tab strip position (Stage 2), UI text size, container colours, user CSS | Follows the OS; two densities; compact kept because its removal was a measured-later mistake elsewhere |
| **Passwords and autofill** | Password manager on/off, autofill of saved credentials, passkeys, import/export credentials. **Payment autofill is Stage 2** and has no row until it ships (D-S3, Stage 1 13.2) | Origin-bound fill; no cloud; OS key store |
| **Downloads** | Download directory, ask where to save, auto-open (off by default), dangerous-type behaviour, retention of the list | No auto-open; the safest action is prominent |
| **Accessibility** | Motion level R/S/X, page zoom default, UI text zoom, forced colours, keyboard options, caret browsing, focus indicators, screen-reader hints | OS preferences respected; no essential motion; every motion can be stopped |
| **Language and writing** | UI and page languages; spellcheck languages; which languages use the interim OS spelling service and which use an Izcal lexicon (ED-D8); autocorrect and writing suggestions | **Stage 2**, hidden until the ED-D8 Stage 1 amendment is applied; when it ships, spellcheck is in the person's languages, the OS tier is named per language and never script-visible, and autocorrect and suggestions are off by default |
| **AI** | The single switch; when off, the page states that AI features are off and lists nothing else | Off; no entry points, chips, model downloads or promotions; the choice survives updates (UI R4, B10) |
| **Media and DRM** | Protected-media mode, per-container memory, autoplay, captions/audio description where the engine exposes them | Protected media asks before switching; autoplay follows the OS/browser rule |
| **Updates and recovery** | Update channel, relaunch policy, session restore, crash reports, recovery options, profile reset | Update without asking when no relaunch is needed; relaunch states what is restored |

`about:version`, `about:build`, `about:licences`, `about:credits`, `about:diagnostics`, `about:policies` and `about:shell` are separate pages reached from the menu, not settings rows (6.14). Every settings row and every About page is registered in the registry, and a registry test fails on a row or page that exists in code but not in the list.

**Why the groups are task-named, and why the defaults carry the weight.** Fewer than 5% of a sampled set of users had changed any application setting, and when Firefox made tracking protection the default, adoption went from about 20% opting in to effectively everyone ([Spool, 2011](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/), informal; [Mozilla, 2019](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/?p=62557); [usability 3.1, 3.3](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Firefox's 2026 redesign split "General" into Appearance, Accessibility, Languages, and Tabs & browsing because users could not predict where an option lived, and settings search was added in an earlier reorganisation too ([Mozilla, 2026](https://blog.mozilla.org/en/firefox/firefox-settings/); [usability 3.5–3.6](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Two consequences shape this section: the protective value of the browser is delivered by defaults, not by asking people to find a switch; and settings names must describe a task, not an implementation category. No vendor publishes settings-open, settings-search or AI-control comprehension rates, which is why SH-22 attaches a measurement to every change rather than assuming use.

**How a row reads.** The visible row is the setting's name, its current value and a "Default" marker when unchanged. Opening it shows the reason, the effect in plain terms, the scope selector where it applies, "Reset to default" and "What this will change". A protective setting that the person turns off says what protection is lost and offers "Turn back on"; it does not moralise, and it does not re-ask. Policy-set rows are visible, marked "Set by your organisation", and cannot be changed; the policy's source and the administrator's contact are shown where the policy provides them. A setting that no longer exists leaves a migration note rather than disappearing silently.

**Search.** The settings page has a search field that matches names, synonyms, descriptions and reasons, and shows the group and the current value in the result. The search index is built at build time from the registry, so it never needs a network or a background service. A search result opens the row with its section expanded.

**No hidden settings.** There is no `about:config` general-purpose editor. Diagnostic and experimental settings live in a separate "Diagnostics" page behind an explicit warning, are listed with their risk and are reset on upgrade unless marked persistent. A policy can set only registry settings, never an arbitrary preference. Every setting write is a typed broker operation with validation; a page or extension cannot write settings.

**Measurement.** The registry records, per setting, whether its use is observed from opt-in telemetry, from a lab study, or not measured. SH-22 requires that no setting is removed, renamed or re-defaulted without a measurement plan approved before the change and a published result. The Compact-density lesson is the precedent: removal on an unmeasured assumption, restoration after measurement ([Bugzilla 1693028](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1693028)).

### 6.10 Downloads

**Where they appear.** A download starts in the toolbar-anchored bubble, not a bottom shelf, so it is visibly browser UI and not page content, and the bubble has room for a warning without covering the page's own controls. The downloads page lists all downloads with the same fields. The bubble is shell UI; the two-level warning prompt for a dangerous file is a prompt window.

**What a row says.** File name; source origin and container; size; progress and rate; state (starting, downloading, paused, completed, failed, blocked, removed); the **verdict** (safe, unknown, suspicious, dangerous) with its reason, its confidence and its source (Izcal's local list, the OS reputation interface, or the signer's status); the **signer** on platforms that expose one; the **real destination**, including a sandbox remap ("Saved to ~/Downloads, not the folder the page requested"); what **Open** will do (which OS handler, and that the file leaves the browser's sandbox); and **Show in folder**, **Copy path**, **Remove from list** and **Delete file** as separate actions. "Remove from list" never deletes the file, and "Delete file" never silently removes the list entry.

**Rules.** Safe types download without a prompt; executable and script types, and archives containing them, never open automatically and require a prompt to open (SH-R7). Every file gets the OS origin mark: Mark-of-the-Web on Windows and `com.apple.quarantine` on macOS; Linux has no common equivalent, and the residual is stated in the UI (SH-R6). The mark is defence in depth, not proof: it has been bypassed in the wild (CVE-2022-41091, Magniber, on CISA's known-exploited list), so it never substitutes for the warning or for the no-auto-open rule ([security 6.3–6.8](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/security_robustness.md)). The browser never launches a downloaded file itself; it hands it to the OS only after the person presses Open. A file whose type is ambiguous is opened only through the OS handler with the warning that the OS will decide. A download from a private window is listed only for the session; if the person keeps the file, the file is kept but no history row is written. The download list is not a history record and is cleared separately.

**Honesty about checks.** A verdict is a claim with a source. "Safe" means no list matched and no signature problem was found, not that the file is harmless; the row says so in one line. "Suspicious" and "Dangerous" carry the reason (matched list, unsigned, signer mismatch, type mismatch, low reputation) and the confidence. The browser does not invent a reputation score it does not have. This follows the evidence that reported download failures are destination and comprehension failures, not missing warnings ([Apple Developer Forums 769229](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/769229), Low; [gaps 1.4](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/gaps_opportunities.md)).

### 6.11 Updates, relaunch and the default-browser ask

**Updates.** The updater verifies signatures under UPD-R; the shell never decides whether an update is authentic. When an update can apply without a relaunch, it does, and the About page shows the new version after the next start. When a relaunch is required, a non-modal update row appears in the toolbar and in About: "An update is ready. Restarting will restore your 14 tabs and 2 windows. Restart now / Later." "Later" is respected for 7 days†; after that the row says "Restarting today at the next safe moment" and the browser restarts when no form has unsaved input and no download or upload is active, or at the next start. A relaunch states what will be restored and never claims that a session was saved when it was not. The 7-day default and the forced 2–4 a.m. window in Chrome's enterprise policy are the closest published precedent ([Chrome Enterprise policy](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/7679871), Medium); Izcal's policy is visible and does not use a silent forced window.

**Default browser.** The ask uses the OS-sanctioned API exactly once, at the end of onboarding, with "Not now" as a real answer and no visual pressure. On Windows, the OS's default-apps surface controls file associations; the ask opens that surface rather than pretending the browser can set itself. A "no" is final: no update, crash, launch or new-version page re-asks. Settings > Privacy and security has a "Set as default" row for a person who changes their mind; it opens the same OS surface. The browser never claims to be the default when it is not, and it does not re-check on every start in a way that nags.

### 6.12 Import, export and no lock-in

**Import.** Import is offered during onboarding and from the browser menu ("Import bookmarks and settings"). Before anything is written, a preview states: the source (Chrome, Firefox or a file), the counts and types (bookmarks, history, passwords, settings), what will **not** be imported and why (cookies never; Chrome passwords only from a CSV the person exports; unsupported settings), the destination container, and the fact that the whole operation is local. Import runs in the background with progress, can be cancelled, and can be undone as a unit for 24 h†. A slow import never blocks first paint; the onboarding import is one of four skippable screens and the person can continue browsing while it runs. The importer follows SH-R2: no cookie decryption, no App-Bound Encryption bypass, passwords only from a CSV the person exports, bookmarks and history from disk; its file access is documented for endpoint-security vendors so it is not mistaken for an infostealer.

**The password manager, bounded.** Stage 1 ships a basic manager as a UI and broker service over the **OS credential store** — macOS Keychain and Windows Credential Manager through their native APIs, and the freedesktop **Secret Service D-Bus API** on Linux, called directly as an OS interface rather than by linking `libsecret` (LGPL-2.1-or-later), which is therefore not a dependency — not an Izcal-owned credential database, which reconciles UI R16 with the Stage 1 design's "no custom credential store in Stage 1". Save and fill are origin-bound and container-scoped; the broker writes the secret into the target form after consent; the shell and the prompt renderer see usernames and origins only; revealing a password requires a fresh OS authentication; and export is a CSV behind re-authentication. Passkeys go through platform authenticators. There is no sync, no cloud and no breach-check service in Stage 1. The manager's settings are the Passwords and autofill group (6.9), and every credential prompt is a ledger entry (6.4).

**Export.** The export format is an open, documented, versioned JSON Lines schema (`izcal-export` v1) with one record type per line and a published JSON Schema. It covers pages, visits, bookmarks, folders, settings (with defaults and scopes), containers and, when selected, the prompt ledger. Credentials export as CSV only after a re-authentication prompt, with a warning that the file is plaintext. The export is deterministic: the same store produces byte-identical output for the same schema version and clock values. The UI states plainly: "Your data stays on this device. There is no Izcal account and no sync in this version. Export is the way to take it with you." Sync is not silently absent; it is named as out of scope in the product (Q-SH-5).

### 6.13 Notifications

Notification permission is the largest prompt class, and a permission for a feature that does not exist is a lie. In Stage 1, sites that request notifications are told "notifications are not available in this version" and no permission prompt is shown. Stage 2 adds delivery: the broker decides, the shell presents the in-product notification centre and the quiet chip, and the OS notification service is a delivery channel, not the source of truth. A granted site can be muted, snoozed or revoked from the notification, the site panel or the ledger. A per-site digest is opt-in and never delays a notification by default (UI R12). Auto-revocation retires a notification grant after 30 days† without use, with an "expired" state visible in the panel. Izcal does not send its own promotional notifications, ever.

### 6.14 Onboarding, first run, recovery and about

**First run.** The first window is usable before onboarding finishes. Onboarding is four skippable screens, in this order: Welcome (one line: tracking blocked by default; no account, no telemetry); Bring your stuff (import preview and a Later action); Containers (the four named defaults, explained; no renaming in Stage 1); Things to know (protected media uses the OS engine; Linux DRM availability; the default-browser ask). **AI is not mentioned in onboarding**: with AI off it has zero surfaces (SH-D16, SH-8), and the only AI control is the Settings row. The whole flow has a target of ≤ 90 s† for ≥ 90%† of lab participants (SH-6). The import screen exists because first-run choices become long-term choices: when import was automatic in a Firefox prototype, only 22 of 67 participants noticed it unprompted, 57 of 67 kept the imported data, and 14–67% raised privacy concerns depending on the design, while slower import was associated with fewer later sessions ([Davidson et al., CHI 2018](https://research.mozilla.org/files/2018/05/Will-Automatically-Importing-User-Data-Help-Overcome-the-Blank-Slate-Problem.pdf); [usability 4.1, 4.7](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). So import is visible, after first paint, previewed, undoable, and states what is copied in one sentence: in a 2019 Pew survey, 36% never read a privacy policy before agreeing, and only 22% of those who read one read it all the way through ([Pew, 2019](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2019/11/15/americans-and-privacy-concerned-confused-and-feeling-lack-of-control-over-their-personal-information/)). There is no account, no sign-in, no sync, no newsletter and no telemetry prompt; telemetry is off and lives in Settings > Privacy, where it can be reviewed. Crash reports are opt-out per crash, not a first-run decision.

**Recovery.** The recovery window is a native process, separate from the engine and from the shell, launched by the installation's launcher when the broker cannot start, when the shell fails twice before first paint (or three times in 60 s†), or when the same session fails to restore three times. It is titled "Troubleshoot" and every option states its effect and what it preserves:

| Action | What it does | What it preserves |
|---|---|---|
| **Start without hardware acceleration** | Starts the browser with the software rendering path for this session | Everything; the setting is not changed unless the person ticks "remember" |
| **Start with themes and extensions off** | Starts with user CSS off and extensions disabled for this session | Everything; the person can re-enable them |
| **Reset profile settings** | Restores registry defaults; does not touch bookmarks, history, passwords, containers or open tabs | User data; settings return to defaults |
| **Send the crash report** | Shows exactly what would be sent (filtered stacks, no recordings) and sends only on confirmation | Nothing is sent without the confirmation; dumps are deleted after 30 days† |
| **Quit** | Closes the browser | Everything |

The window is reachable with the keyboard, exposes native controls to the platform's accessibility API (on Linux, through the sandboxed AT-SPI bridge, AX-D12), and works when the engine, the shell renderer and the GPU process are all unavailable. It cannot browse, cannot open a file, cannot read page content and cannot reset the profile without a second confirmation that names what is kept. It writes a local log line for each action, and the next normal start shows "Recovery was used" in About > Diagnostics with the actions taken.

**About pages.** `about:version` (browser, engine, shell image hash, OS, channel), `about:build` (build id, compiler, sandbox tiers), `about:licences` (the MIT inventory for shipped code and data, generated from the build), `about:credits`, `about:diagnostics` (process list with PIDs and budgets, sandbox tiers, log tail, profile path, update channel, a "record this UI bug" action and a "copy diagnostics" button), `about:policies` (enterprise policies in force and their sources) and `about:shell` (the shell's registry version and the active theme). These pages are local, unlisted and never linked from page content.

### 6.15 Extensions in the shell

**Stage 2 only.** Stage 1 ships no extensions (D-S3, Stage 1 13.2, extensions spec), so no extension surface exists in Stage 1: no toolbar slot, no menu row, no badge, no Extensions page entry beyond "Extensions are not available in this version, and no extension code can run." The design below is the Stage 2 surface, owned by this spec and staffed with the extensions programme.

The shell owns the extension toolbar and the extension surfaces that are browser UI. It does not run extension code. A Ring A extension gets: a toolbar action button with an icon and an accessible name (extension-supplied icon **data**, decoded and drawn by the shell, labelled as extension-provided under AX-32; zero pixels are drawn by extension code); a popup hosted in an extension-origin renderer, not in the shell; an options page in an extension-origin renderer; menus; a tab badge; and a site-panel row showing what the extension can read on this site and what it used recently. It also owns the surfaces the extensions spec hands over: the **new-tab and homepage override prompts** (through the prompt layer, never a silent override), the **recommendation tier badge** and the **command editor** (EX-D15's remaining handoff; A-EX-12 stays open until these exist). The toolbar overflows into a keyboard-reachable menu; every action has a keyboard path and a context-menu equivalent. An extension cannot inject into the shell, read its DOM, draw in browser chrome, receive accessibility data or approve a consent. The shell's Extensions page lists installed extensions, versions, enabled state, granted capabilities and pending update grants; it can enable, disable and revoke, and it can **request** an install or a growth prompt, never approve one (EX-D5, security scope 6.6a). A pending growth grant is shown in the toolbar with a persistent marker until the person decides or disables the extension.

### 6.16 Accessibility of the shell

**The standard.** The shell conforms to WCAG 2.2 AA as applied to software by WCAG2ICT (2024), to UAAG 2.0 where it applies to a browser UI, and to the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices for its toolbar, tablist, dialog, menu and landmark patterns. The web pages of the shell are tested with the same automated and manual suites as any page; the OS-level windows and the prompt windows are tested with NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver and Orca in the AT lab each release (UI-1, UI-2). The shell's own tree follows the same mirror-and-cache path as content (AX-D3, AX-D4): the validated mirror lives in the **broker**, and an assistive-technology query is answered from it — on Windows through the UI host's read-only mapping of that mirror, never by waiting on the shell renderer — because synchronous cross-process pulls are the measured failure mode ([Cache the World, 2023](https://blog.mozilla.org/accessibility/firefox-113-accessibility-performance/); [usability 8.15](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). The UI host's placement is the AX-D9 measurement's current design assumption, not a decided option; if AX-M0 selects AX-D9(a), only the adapter's host changes and the broker mirror stays.

**Input methods and third-party DLLs.** TSF and IME DLLs run in the UI host (ED-D5(b)). What they can reach is bounded by **ED-D6**: only the focused, non-credential editable's **±2,048 UTF-16** window, never a hidden tab, and credential fields are redacted by construction. The bound is tested with a credential field and a hidden tab; it is what keeps the always-on host from becoming an unbounded page-text channel.

**Keyboard.** F6 cycles between the toolbar, the tab strip, the page and the open panel; Shift+F6 cycles backwards. Inside the toolbar, arrow keys move between controls and Tab leaves the toolbar; the tab strip uses roving tabindex, arrow keys, Home/End and Ctrl+Tab for tab switching. Every action has a keyboard path, including tab context menus (Menu key or Shift+F10), the site panel (a shortcut and a focusable button), download actions, settings, the ledger and the recovery window. Shortcuts are discoverable in Settings > Accessibility > Keyboard and in a shortcut sheet on `?` when focus is in the shell. No shortcut requires a chord that a screen reader reserves, and no single-letter browser shortcut collides with screen-reader browse-mode keys (NVDA and JAWS use H, K, F, D, L, T and the number row for navigation); conflicts are tested with NVDA, JAWS and VoiceOver ([usability 8.8](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Pointer targets are at least 24×24 CSS px (WCAG 2.5.8), including tab close buttons, toolbar icons and prompt buttons; a denser control carries a documented exception or is removed.

**Focus and announcements.** Focus is visible with a 2 px outline at 3:1 contrast in both themes and in forced colours. Opening a panel moves focus into it; closing it returns focus to the control that opened it. An asynchronous update that removes the focused element moves focus to the nearest stable ancestor, never to the document body. A shell surface may not obscure the focused control: the find bar, download bubble, ledger panel and prompt are placed so the focus indicator stays visible, or they move when they would cover it (WCAG 2.4.11). Status changes that are not focus changes (a download completed, a find result count, a tab closed) are announced through a polite live region; errors are assertive. The shell does not steal focus on load, on update or on a background event.

**Zoom, reflow and text.** Page zoom reaches 400% and reflows under WCAG 1.4.10; the shell's own text zoom reaches 200% and its settings, history, ledger and panel reflow at 320 CSS px without two-dimensional scrolling. Shell layout uses logical properties, `rem` for text, and no fixed-height text containers. Text spacing (WCAG 1.4.12) is tested by overriding line height, letter and word spacing. The shell uses the system UI font and the engine's font fallback.

**Colour and contrast.** Every shell surface meets 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for UI boundaries in both themes and in forced-colours mode. Forced colours and increased contrast are tested as separate modes: Windows High Contrast triggers `forced-colors` and overrides author colours, while macOS Increase Contrast does not, so neither is treated as a substitute for the other or for the normal theme ([usability 8.12](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Information is never carried by colour alone: the container chip has a name, the blocking counts have labels, the status rows have text. Container colours are checked for deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia and always paired with the name (SH-9).

**Motion.** The shell honours the OS reduced-motion setting as level R by default; Settings > Accessibility offers R (OS), S (opt-in, discrete motion between keyframes) and X (stop motion now, per page) per AN-D10. Shell transitions are ≤ 150 ms† and never essential; the shell has no parallax, no autoplaying video and no flashing content. "Stop motion now" is reachable from the site panel and by keyboard.

**Cognitive and language.** Plain-language strings at a target reading level of grade 9† for prompts, settings reasons and errors; consistent placement; no timeouts except prompt expiry, which is extended on request; errors say what happened, what was preserved and what to do next; destructive actions name what they delete. The shell's `lang` attribute follows the UI locale, and each Fluent message carries its own language.

**Strings and bidirectional text.** Stage 1 is English-only, but the string layer is Fluent-shaped from the first commit: identifiers, placeables and selectors, never sentences assembled from fragments, so a translator can inflect and reorder without a code change ([Project Fluent](https://projectfluent.org/); [usability 9.1–9.2](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Runtime text is isolated wherever it meets shell text: every tab title, URL, bookmark name, site name and container name is rendered inside an element whose direction is determined from the text, with `<bdi>` or equivalent isolation, and attributes that cannot carry markup use isolate control characters. Without this, a Hebrew or Arabic title can visually reorder the punctuation and numbers around it, so a tab appears to say something it does not ([W3C, inline bidi](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/inline-bidi-markup/); [usability 9.3–9.4](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/usability_accessibility.md)). Layouts are pseudolocalized before Stage 1 ships, with a planning allowance of about 30% string expansion, so no surface depends on English string widths.

**The prompt and recovery windows.** The prompt window is a dialog with a heading, description, origin, container and buttons; focus moves to the least-privileged action; Escape dismisses; screen readers announce the window by its heading and role. The recovery window uses native controls on Windows and macOS and a small native window with AT-SPI on Linux; both are tested in the AT lab.

### 6.17 Determinism, recording and testing

**The shell is deterministic given its inputs.** The shell renderer runs under the substrate's scheduler and clock. In production the clock is the system clock; in simulation and recording it is the virtual clock. Locale, time zone, UI language, theme, font image, window geometry and the broker's event stream are inputs. The shell's authoritative orderings (tabs, history, ledger, recently closed) never depend on hash-map iteration or wall-clock races; ids are monotonic; timestamps come from the recorded clock. A shell state is a function of the snapshot plus the ordered events it has received.

**Two rings, one recording.** The shell keeps a **content-free diagnostic ring** from process launch: timings, event counts, state-transition names, memory and queue depths, and UI-state hashes — no titles, no URLs, no input text, no page strings. It is memory-only, bounded, and may be attached to a crash report after the person sees the fields. It is not a UI recording. A **UI recording** follows DT-D2's explicit mode: it starts when the person starts recording (at the next shell-renderer launch, or at the next navigation for a live session), is navigation-scoped, and ends when they stop. This keeps the accepted DevTools rule (DT-D2, DT-1(f)) and separates diagnosis from consent to record content.

**UI recording at the capability boundary.** A UI recording is not a screen capture and not a copy of every page renderer. It records: the initial shell-state snapshot; every typed event the broker sends to the shell, with its sequence number and the broker's clock; every shell request and its response; every prompt event (queued, shown, decided, expired); window geometry and display changes; and the UI-state hash at each checkpoint (each event batch and every 500 ms†). Page renderers are recorded separately by DT-D2 when the person records a tab; the two recordings share a session id and a timeline. The recording contains titles and URLs, which are sensitive, so it is encrypted at rest, listed in About > Diagnostics, deletable, and never attached to a crash report (SH-R5). A shared bundle defaults to **origin-only** redaction: titles become their registrable site and a per-session hash, URLs become their origin and path hash, and form values are never recorded. The segment header carries the engine configuration, the substrate clock and scheduler mode, the seeded entropy stream and the sealed font inventory, because a replay without the same font image and clock is not exact. Page network belongs to the tab recording as one archive per page load, not to the shell stream; the bundle's manifest declares that boundary. A canary string is inserted into the recording in test builds to prove the redaction removes it from every field.

**The UI-state hash.** The hash is computed over the shell's canonical state, not over pixels or DOM serialization: the route and document id; the open panel or dialog id; the focus target's stable control id and its ancestry; the tab list (ids, order, active id, container ids, pinned, frozen); the visible row ids of the current list and the list's scroll offset in whole rows; the setting ids and values shown; the prompt id and class when one is open; and the theme, density, UI text size and font-image id. It excludes wall-clock display strings (relative times are rendered from the recorded clock, not the system clock), caret blink, hover, transition progress, animation phase and anything derived from the system clock or random ids. Two states with the same fields are the same state; a checkpoint that differs only in an excluded field is reported as a rendering difference, not a state divergence.

**Replay.** Replay starts a fresh shell renderer in the deterministic configuration, loads the recorded initial snapshot, feeds the event stream at the recorded logical times and compares the UI-state hash at each checkpoint. The first mismatch is reported with the event sequence number, the expected and actual state names, and the spec anchor of the state machine. Nothing after a divergence is shown as exact. The recording UI says "exact until event N" for a redacted bundle. The fallback, if process-level replay cannot be made exact, is a broker-fact replay: the same event stream rendered by a fresh shell at a fixed default geometry, which reproduces state bugs but not pixel bugs.

**Cost and scope.** The recording is a typed stream, not pixels: the target is ≤ 1 ms† p95 per UI event on the reference device and ≤ 4 MB† for a 10-minute session, measured at SH-M4 alongside DT-15. If the cost is exceeded, the diagnostic ring shrinks, not the recording; if redaction cannot preserve replay, the bundle carries the redacted fields only and says so. "Record this UI bug" starts an explicit recording from About > Diagnostics or a keyboard shortcut, with the content-free diagnostic ring attached to its header; the recording is memory-only until the person saves it, the saved bundle is encrypted at rest and deleted with the container, and the person can attach a note, view the redacted stream and send it only with consent.

**Testing.** Every shell surface has a scripted path: a document route, a sequence of inputs, an expected state hash and an accessibility assertion. The suite runs in three tiers: **T1** on every change (contract tests for every endpoint, keyboard walkthroughs, automated WCAG checks, deterministic replay of 50 scripted sessions); **T2** nightly (the full 200-session replay, visual diffs of shell documents at three widths and two themes, forced-colours and reduced-motion variants); **T3** each release (manual AT lab with NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver and Orca, a red-team prompt-spoofing suite, performance and memory on the reference devices, and a SUS study). A test that flakes above 1%† is fixed or quarantined with an owner and a date, never ignored. The automated accessibility checks use Izcal's own semantic tree and rules, with a test-only third-party checker (MPL-2.0, test-only under HGBA-2 2.3.2) as a cross-check; no third-party tool is shipped. Pixel diffs are on the shell's canonical raster, so they are stable across devices (GR-D3); where determinism is claimed the oracle is exact (reftest-style), and a tolerance is used only for anti-aliasing with the tolerance published with the test. Automated accessibility checks are a floor, not a verdict: the vendor study covers well under two-thirds of issues by volume and the W3C says no tool alone can decide, so the manual AT lab in T3 is the release gate. Tab, window, restore, discard and crash behaviour is tested as a generated state machine with invariants (no lost tab, no duplicate tab, no prompt without a principal, bounded restore attempts), not as hand-written cases alone ([determinism 7.1–7.14](../research_notes/Izcal%20shell/determinism_recording_testing.md)).

### 6.18 Security and privacy

**Threat model.** The shell's threat model is the security scope's TB-12 and TB-1, extended with the prompt window, the UI host and the shell's display strings.

| Adversary | What they can try | What stops it | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Page content** | Draw a fake prompt; overlay the shell; spoof the URL; change the title; supply a favicon; navigate the shell | Prompt windows are separate OS windows; the shell layer is above page content; URL and title display are broker-formatted; favicons are decoded sandboxed and never used in prompts; the shell cannot be navigated by a page | A page can draw anything inside its own rectangle; the browser does not claim the page area is trusted |
| **Compromised shell renderer** | Call endpoints it was not granted; approve a prompt; read page data; forge a label | IPC-R12 binding; the broker validates every write; the shell has no approval endpoint and no prompt window or prompt input; endpoints return no page DOM or secrets; origin strings are broker-formatted; jitless + sandbox | It can display a false origin or other false information in the shell (the stated shell-compromise residual); the broker's prompt origin is authoritative at decision time; a restart/replay shows the recorded truth |
| **Compromised prompt renderer** | Change the prompt text; claim approval; report inverted geometry; read another prompt | It can change only its own prompt's pixels; the broker judges only its own template rectangles, aborts on divergent geometry, and maps input itself; one process per prompt; no page strings | It can mislead the person about the consequence; the ledger keeps the text hash and the broker's canonical capability |
| **Compromised UI host** | Move or hide a content window; read the accessibility mirror; inject content input; misreport content geometry | No prompt window or prompt input; no grants, credentials or consent state; the broker validates content input and hit-tests prompts itself | It can deny service, observe page names/text in the mirror (the AT surface already exposes them) and misplace a prompt, which never approves it |
| **Compromised shell content GPU** | Paint false shell pixels or a false prompt | It is not on the prompt path: the prompt renderer software-rasterizes its own surface; prompt pixels never reach the GPU process | It can corrupt or fake shell chrome, which is the shell-compromise residual; it cannot alter a prompt |
| **Malicious extension** | Inject into shell; read shell state; approve a prompt; widen grants silently | No extension API for browser UI; no AT data; grants are per origin and staged; approval only by the broker | It can act on pages it was granted |
| **Malicious IME/AT DLL** | Read process memory; log input | It runs in the UI host, not the broker; the broker's code-integrity mitigations stay on; TSF is out of the credential process. **ED-D6 bounds it**: only the focused, non-credential editable's ±2,048 UTF-16 window is exposed; never a hidden tab, never a credential field, and the window is redacted by construction | It can read that bounded window and the accessibility mirror; stated in AX-D9/ED-D5/ED-D6 |
| **Local application** | Cover the prompt; synthesize input; launch with flags; read the profile | The broker rejects approaches outside its rectangle, rejects automation-synthesized input for **every** prompt class, and rejects approval while an OS-reported external occluder covers the prompt; AGT-R8; profile encryption | Where the platform cannot report external occlusion (Wayland), a cover is a denial-of-service or deception attempt that requires focus and is stated as a residual; screen capture and a compromised OS are outside the boundary |
| **Network attacker** | Serve a lookalike; spoof a certificate | Networking's TLS/CT/root program; phishing interstitials; IDN/bidi display | A correctly certified lookalike is a social-engineering problem; the interstitial and the address bar are the defence |

**The shell's display-string rules.** Every string the shell shows about a site comes from the broker and is typed: origin display (registrable domain emphasized, mixed scripts shown in punycode, bidi isolated, no page-supplied title as URL), container, file name (basename only, with the full path in the details row), and error text (a closed enum, not page text). A page title is page-supplied: it may appear in a tab, the window title or a row **explicitly labelled as the page's title**, is sanitized and length-bounded, and is never used as the site's name in the site panel, a prompt, the ledger or the address bar. The shell never inserts a page string into HTML without text-node escaping; the engine's normal DOM rules apply, and Trusted Types forbid `innerHTML` with page data. The prompt renderer receives only broker-formatted values; its input type has no page-string constructor (`OriginDisplay` is constructible only by the broker's formatter; G208).

**Favicons and window titles.** A favicon is page-supplied image data: it is decoded in the image decoder, rejects SVG with script and oversized images, is cached per (site, container) and is never shown in a prompt, the site panel's identity row or the recovery window. A favicon request is a network request to the site already being visited; bookmarks show a cached icon only, never a fresh fetch. The OS window title is "<page title> — <registrable site> — Izcal", so a page that renames itself "Gmail" is qualified by its site; the title is not used as a security signal.

**Privacy.** History and bookmarks are local, encrypted at rest, private-window-free and container-clearable (6.7). The ledger is local, encrypted and retained 90 days† by default. Telemetry is opt-in, aggregated, identifier-free, schema-published and inspectable; it contains no URLs, titles, search queries or prompt origins, only counts and closed enums. Crash reports are opt-out per crash, filtered-stack only, never include recordings, and are deleted after 30 days† (SH-R5). The shell sends no network request of its own; the only network requests it causes are the person's navigations and downloads. The content blocker's counts and the prompt ledger never leave the device. The trust-store disclosure (NW-D24) is a neutral line, not a warning, and is present on every site while the OS store is in use. Accessibility never becomes a page-observable signal (AX-D3): no web API sees whether an AT is connected, and the shell does not expose AT state to pages.

### 6.20 Alternatives

The full decision table with options, stars and reversal triggers is Section 9.4. This table records the architectural alternatives that shaped the design and why they lost.

| Choice | Options | Recommendation | Why the others lost | Reversal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Where prompts are drawn** | (a) Inside the page window above a line (the design's trusted region); (b) a separate broker-owned OS window drawn by a prompt renderer; (c) native OS dialogs | **(b)** | (a) is a drawing convention the page compositor can imitate and the shell renderer could forge; (c) cannot be themed, is inconsistent across OSes and cannot show the broker's rich typed values | A platform where a separate owned window cannot be kept above the parent (measured on Wayland/multi-monitor): fall back to **(c)**, the native path CON-R2 already names; a class that (c) cannot draw is denied, not drawn insecurely |
| **Who owns content windows** | (a) Broker everywhere; (b) a least-privilege UI host everywhere; (c) broker on macOS/Linux, UI host on Windows. Prompt windows are excluded: the broker owns those on every platform (6.5). | **(c)** | (a) puts TSF/AT DLLs next to credentials on Windows; (b) adds a process and a hop where no injection exists | AX-M0/SH-M0 measures macOS AT latency or a new injection requirement: extend (b) to macOS |
| **Recording the UI** | (a) Extend the tab recording to the shell and prompt renderers; (b) record the typed broker-to-shell stream and UI state; (c) screenshots | **(b)** | (a) records page content in the shell and is expensive; (c) cannot replay state and cannot be redacted | Replay diverges on more than 5%† of scripted sessions: add process-level capture for the shell renderer only |
| **Where history lives** | (a) Storage service (SG-D11); (b) a separate shell-store process; (c) SQLite/file in the shell | **(a)** | (b) duplicates encryption, eviction and clearing; (c) violates the storage discipline and MIT-only (SQLite is not MIT) | The storage service cannot meet SH-20's query latency after indexing: add a shell-owned index cache in the broker, never a second store |

## 7. M-1 compliance

**M-1 part 1: original work.** The shell application, its documents, CSS, project JavaScript, Fluent messages, icons, settings registry, prompt documents, ledger schema, history/bookmark schema, test harness and red-team suite are written by Izcal. No browser's UI code, template, XUL, WebUI binding or DevTools frontend is used, read or copied. No third-party UI framework is shipped.

A user-installed theme is data the person supplies, not a shipped dependency; a theme that loads a remote asset is rejected.

**M-1 part 3: evidence.** No claim in this spec or its notes cites a third-party project's source code, headers, commits, diffs or code reviews. The history and gaps rest on papers, vendor prose, specifications, issue discussions and black-box observations. The shell's UI patterns are described from published design prose and user studies, not copied from a browser's markup or styles. Where a behaviour is known only from a project's implementation, it is a black-box measurement at SH-M0 (for example, the OS window ordering and occlusion behaviour of each compositor, and the exact latency of the input path).

**M-1 part 4: web-visible behaviour.** The shell is browser UI, not web content, so no web-visible behaviour changes. Its internal documents still use standards (HTML, CSS, ARIA, Fluent) so that they are testable, accessible and portable. The two exceptions that touch the web platform are named and owned elsewhere: the `persist()` prompt (SG-D6) and the clipboard Paste confirmation (ED-D10), both of which are standards-visible behaviours specified by their own specs.

**Specification anchors.** HTML and CSS parsing and style for the shell documents; layout for text and reflow; fonts and text for string rendering and fallback; accessibility for the semantic tree and platform adapters; ARIA Authoring Practices for widget patterns; WCAG 2.2 and WCAG2ICT for conformance; UAAG 2.0 for browser-specific requirements; Fluent for message syntax; the OS window, input, TSF and accessibility interfaces as OS interfaces; ISO 8601 for timestamps; JSON Schema for the export format; RFC 3339 for recorded times.
