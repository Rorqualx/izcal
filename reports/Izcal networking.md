*Revision 0.1 (19 October 2026): first draft, written from the six research notes and the frame note by the synthesis author.*

HGBA-2 2.3.0 drops neqo, and the owner's direction excludes every library that does browser networking (hyper, h2, h3, quinn, quiche, reqwest, hickory-dns and the network stacks of other browsers). Izcal therefore writes its own HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, HTTP/3, QUIC, HPACK, QPACK, DNS, Fetch, cookie store, HTTP cache, WebSocket and WebTransport code from the RFCs and the WHATWG standards, and, because everything Izcal ships must be available under MIT (HGBA-2 2.3.2), its own TLS stack on MIT-licensed cryptography crates. The record says three things about browser networking. **Features fail when they depend on the other side behaving**: pipelining, TLS False Start, TCP Fast Open, OCSP Must-Staple and HPKP all died on middleboxes or operators, and HTTP/2's priority tree and server push died because the party sending the signal did not have the information ([HP 1, 2, 6][HP]). **Retrofitting a sandbox onto a network stack is the hard part**: Chromium's network service is still unsandboxed by default on Android, Windows and Linux in October 2026 (Chromium's own sandbox table), and Firefox's general socket process has been open since December 2016 ([SR 6.1][SR], [HP 4.4–4.5][HP]). **Most network-stack vulnerabilities that survive a memory-safe language are disagreements**: two parsers reading one message differently (request smuggling, client-side desync, URL and cookie confusion), or peer input reaching an unchecked assumption, an `unwrap`, an overflow or a state misuse (every Rust QUIC panic on record) ([SR 1.8–1.9, 2.1–2.7][SR]). **The central idea: one state machine per protocol, written once, sans-IO, driven only by the substrate, with every peer-supplied size charged to a budget and every piece of state keyed by its partition.** The same code then runs on real sockets, under the deterministic simulator and inside fuzzers, cannot panic on input, and cannot leak across (site, container). The decisions that matter most: (1) **network services per (top-level site, container)**, the network-side analogue of site isolation, so a hostile page reaches only its own top-level site's network state (NW-D3); (2) **sockets minted by the broker** against Local Network Access tickets the broker itself issued, so routes, local-network grants and bad ports are enforced outside the processes that parse network bytes, and those processes hold no socket rights (NW-D4); (3) **Izcal's own TLS stack, certificate verifier, path builder and root program**, with Izcal-generated revocation data, CT enforced and degradation instead of lock-out when data is stale, replacing Stage 1's rustls and platform-verifier-first rules (NW-D5, NW-D19, NW-D22); (4) **recording at the decryption boundary in a fresh, dedicated partition service**, so a recorded session replays every layer above TLS and QUIC packet protection bit-identically without ever recording a key (NW-D2); (5) **the strictest partition key any browser ships**, including the frame site and the container route (NW-D7). Networking is the second-largest component after the JavaScript engine: about **58–81 people†** at full strength, WebRTC transport, TLS and cryptography, content blocking, the root program and Izcal's own lists included (8–13† of them policy, CA-review and list-curation staff rather than engineers), reached through a hiring ramp, for **4–5 years†** to performance parity, about **200–340 person-years†** (Section 9).

## 1. The problem and who has it

| User | Task, in their words | What fails today |
|---|---|---|
| **People browsing** | "Pages load fast on any network, my sessions stay mine, and nobody follows me from site to site" | Every engine leaves some network state global: Chrome left HSTS, proxy and PAC DNS, the certificate verifier and DoH unpartitioned, and **no engine fixes the shared socket cap**, which lets one site time another's requests ([PP 1.8, 1.12][PP]; [xsleaks: connection pool](https://xsleaks.dev/docs/attacks/timing-attacks/connection-pool/)) |
| **People on hard networks** | "It works behind the hotel portal, the corporate proxy, on IPv6-only mobile and on a bad train" | 4.4% of clients could not use UDP in Google's 2016 data, mostly enterprise firewalls ([Langley et al., SIGCOMM 2017 §7.2](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3098822.3098842)); a failed load says "network error" and nothing more, because Fetch's network error has no reason field ([GO 1.1, 1.9][GO]) |
| **Web developers** | "Why did this fail, wait, miss the cache, use HTTP/2 instead of 3, get blocked by CORS?" | DevTools throttling is request-level, cannot simulate loss, and ignores TCP slow start, DNS and TLS ([3perf](https://3perf.com/blog/chrome-throttling/), [GO 4.1–4.3][GO]); HAR cannot represent streams, HTTP/3, partition keys, cache verdicts or service-worker hops ([GO 1.5][GO]) |
| **Site operators and CDNs** | "Behave like a browser and follow the RFCs" | New engines are blocked by bot management: Cloudflare Turnstile kept Ladybird off "a lot of sites" until May 2026 ([Ladybird, May 2026](https://ladybird.org/newsletter/2026-05-31/), [PP 4.4][PP]) |
| **Izcal's engineers and agents** | "Reproduce this exactly; prove the parser never panics" | Rust QUIC stacks keep shipping remote panics: three pre-authentication panics in quinn-proto from 2023 to 2026, one an `unwrap()` on a peer varint ([SR 1.8][SR]); external record/replay is off by 12–37% median page-load time because it must guess at a non-deterministic page ([Mahimahi, USENIX ATC 2015](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~ravian/publications/mahimahi_atc.pdf), [GO 3.2][GO]) |

**What Izcal's specs already decide** (full list with IDs in [FR 2][FR]):

- **[M-1][H2] (2.3.2).** Izcal builds its own HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, HTTP/3 and QUIC; neqo is dropped; allowed dependencies are generic infrastructure, cryptography primitives from MIT-licensed crates (the TLS protocol itself is Izcal's own), and OS interfaces. Everything that ships, code and data, must be available under MIT; the exceptions are Unicode and other standards bodies' data, test-only inputs, and OS interfaces and the toolchain (2.3.2). Every algorithm carries a `// spec:` anchor at a pinned revision; other stacks are black-box signals only. No third-party project's source code (code files, headers, build manifests, commits, diffs, code reviews of engines, libraries, tools or OS components) is evidence (part 3, 2.3.3): this spec and its notes cite only published prose, specification text, registry data and black-box observation.
- **[I-1 (b)][H2].** The network service partitions **all** its state by (top-level site, container), extending Fetch's network partition key. **FP-D8**: origin keying of renderers is always on.
- **[I-2][H2] and [Stage 1 G7][S1].** Renderers hold no sockets; renderer crates never depend on `hg-net`.
- **[HGBA-2 4.6][H2].** Persistent storage uses crash-consistent embedded stores, never custom persistence layers; io_uring is excluded from renderer and network-service designs. **FP-D16** (accepted) defines a regenerable-cache class that this spec extends to the HTTP cache (Section 6.10).
- **[HGBA-2 4.7][H2].** No kernel bypass; CPU crypto and UDP GSO/GRO inside the service; X25519MLKEM768 hybrid by default; crypto agility.
- **[B1, DET-1, DET-2][H3] and [JS-D15][JS].** No engine code touches sockets, clocks, threads or randomness except through the substrate; timing-dependent decisions are recorded inputs; production never waits for determinism.
- **[Stage 1 Section 6][S1].** CHIPS-style partitioned third-party cookies by default; GPC sent by default; per-container user-supplied routes (WireGuard, SOCKS, Arti) labelled honestly; content blocking in the network service; no raw TCP or UDP for web content. **Stage 1 13.2**: Chrome-compatible UA with Izcal's own token; platform verifier first, CT enforced, CRLs pushed (amended by NW-D5).
- **Security scope 0.3.** NET-R1 to NET-R7 (context from the endpoint, ORB before delivery, no panic reachable from network input, differential fuzzing, PKI, Local Network Access); **SEC-D13**: network sandbox required at M8 on Linux and macOS; SEC-34: cookies at rest under an OS-bound key.
- **Coding standard 0.3.1.** Network crates are **tier P**: abort on panic (CS-D1 (a)), no panic reachable from input, fallible capped allocation (CS-ER-9), one Fetch network error for every failure (CS-ER-13, CS-T11), no `tokio`, `async` only on an allowlist (CS-D8), and **Q-CS-2 decided: Izcal writes its own sans-IO HTTP/2 codec**.
- **[Footprint spec][FP].** Network service ≤ 8 MiB† idle inside the 64 MiB† baseline (FP-1); hidden pages get 0 wakeups (FP-17); a static visible page leaves the whole browser at ≤ 1 wakeup/s† (FP-18); HTTP cache 256 MiB† visible budget (FP-26); telemetry opt-in only (FP-D9).
- **[DevTools spec][DT].** `hg-net-observe` emits DNS, connection-decision, TLS and QUIC events (DT-D5), and the views must equal what the stack did (DT-18). Its "qlog from neqo" becomes "qlog from Izcal's QUIC" (Section 9.5).

**Success** is measured by Section 8: every protocol through its conformance oracles, most of which Izcal must build because no client-side suite exists for HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, DNS or proxies ([PR 10.3][PR]); zero panics reachable from network or IPC input; no observable difference across partitions on any listed axis; HTTP/3 at least as fast as Izcal's own HTTP/2 on a 1 Gbps link with a low-end CPU, where every shipping browser loses ([PT 1.3][PT]); the network service quiet when the user is idle; and bit-identical replay of recorded network input.

## 2. History: what came before and why it was built that way

### 2.1 Chronology

| Year | Project | What it did | Why that way | Fate and why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1995–2015 | **WinInet in Internet Explorer** | The OS HTTP library shared with other Windows applications; cache and cookies were OS-wide | One OS, one HTTP client, no site isolation | Retired with IE; shared OS-wide state is the opposite of partitioning ([HP 4.9][HP], Low) |
| 1999–2017 | **HTTP/1.1 pipelining** | Several requests on one connection, responses in order | Connections were expensive and HTTP/1.1 had no framing for multiplexing ([RFC 2616 §8.1.2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2616#section-8.1.2.2)) | Removed: head-of-line blocking by design, broken servers and transparent proxies, "known crashing bugs" ([Chromium](https://www.chromium.org/developers/design-documents/network-stack/http-pipelining/)); Firefox 54 removed it as "a maintenance burden with minimal value" ([bug 1340655](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1340655)) |
| 2008 | **Chromium `net/`** | Own cross-platform stack, single-threaded and callback-driven | "Greater control than would be available with higher-level system networking libraries", avoiding OS-library bugs ([Chromium design doc](https://www.chromium.org/developers/design-documents/network-stack/)) | Survived; became the Network Service |
| 2009–2016 | **SPDY** | Multiplexing, prioritization and header compression over TLS | Cut page-load time without changing networks or content: 27–60% faster in the lab, benefit gone above 2.5% loss ([SPDY whitepaper](https://www.chromium.org/spdy/spdy-whitepaper/)) | Became HTTP/2; removed with NPN in Chrome 51 ([Chromium blog](https://blog.chromium.org/2015/02/hello-http2-goodbye-spdy.html)) |
| 2010–2012 | **TLS False Start** | Send application data before the handshake finished (~30% faster) | Latency | Restricted to NPN sites after 18 months on a blacklist: a few SSL terminators failed, some nondeterministically ([Langley](https://www.imperialviolet.org/2012/04/11/falsestart.html)) |
| 2011–2024 | **CA distrusts by root programs** | Root programs removed or constrained CAs after misissuance or failed incident response: DigiNotar (2011), Symantec (2017–2018), Entrust (2024, SCT-dated) | A browser's root store is the policy that decides which CAs may vouch for any site | Each distrust took months of incident review, public discussion and a dated plan by a staffed program ([Chrome, Symantec](https://security.googleblog.com/2017/09/chromes-plan-to-distrust-symantec.html); [SR 4.7, 4.11][SR]): a root store is an operation, not a file |

### 2.2 Technology mismatches: assumptions that no longer hold

| Assumption baked into older stacks | Why it no longer holds |
|---|---|
| The OS owns transport, so the browser takes what the kernel gives | QUIC is in user space by design ([HP 3.1][HP]); the browser owns congestion control, ACK policy and receive cost, and loses on fast links when it gets them wrong ([PT 1.3][PT]) |
| The network stack is part of the browser process, with ambient access to files, proxy settings and the resolver | A sandbox retrofit then has to broker every one of those dependencies, ([SR 6.4][SR], derived from what a network process needs); Chromium's, out of process since 2019, is still unsandboxed by default on three platforms ([SR 6.1][SR]) |
| One process-wide connection pool and caches are shared state worth sharing | Shared state is a cross-site channel; partitioning costs a few percent ([PP 1.6, 1.14][PP]) |
| The server knows best (push, the priority tree) | Only the client knows its cache and its render order ([HP 2.4–2.5][HP]) |
| Revocation is a live query | OCSP is gone at the largest CA; revocation is pushed ([HP 6.6][HP]) |
| The platform verifier is the trust policy | Platform verifiers differ by OS, do no CT, and do no revocation on Linux ([SR 4.9][SR]) |
| Request-level throttling and external proxies are good enough to test networking | They cannot see QUIC or ECH without defeating them, and they mis-model packets ([GO 3.7, 4.1][GO]) |
| A memory-safe language makes the network stack safe | About 60% of curl's CVEs were not C memory mistakes, and every Rust QUIC panic on record came from peer input reaching an unchecked assumption ([SR 2.1–2.2, 1.8–1.9][SR]) |
| TLS is a library a browser takes from elsewhere | A browser's TLS needs are its own: ECH, QUIC's key interface, a handshake core DTLS can share, a ClientHello whose shape it must control, and recording at the decryption boundary; and under HGBA-2 2.3.2 no complete TLS stack found links only MIT-available code (Section 7) |
| `getaddrinfo` is enough | It returns no HTTPS records, no TTL and blocks; 99% of HTTP/3 page loads in the 2024 crawl were triggered by HTTPS records ([PT 1.7][PT], [PR 6.5][PR]) |

## 3. Today: the landscape and its gaps

**Deployment.** In 2025 Cloudflare saw HTTP/2 on 50% of requests, HTTP/1.x on 29% and HTTP/3 on 21%, nearly unchanged from 2024; post-quantum key exchange rose from 29% to 52% of human traffic during 2025 ([Cloudflare Radar 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/radar-2025-year-in-review)). About 20% of Firefox's HTTP traffic uses HTTP/3 ([Inden](https://max-inden.de/post/fast-udp-io-in-firefox/)). HTTP/1.1 stays a first-class protocol.

**The receive side decides HTTP/3's speed.** On a 1 Gbps testbed Chrome's HTTP/3 reached about 478 Mbps against more than 900 for HTTP/2 (−45%), because QUIC delivered 744K UDP packets to the client against TCP's 53–58K (receive offload did not coalesce QUIC's UDP in Chrome) and generating QUIC responses such as ACKs took 2.97 s and frame parsing 3.47 s of the network thread's 8.5 s ([Zhang et al., WWW 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.09423v2)). Of four QUIC stacks measured in 2026, only MsQuic used both GSO and GRO, which brought it to 8.3 Gbit/s, level with its kernel-bypass build; after packet I/O, **crypto and file I/O** become the bottlenecks ([Späth et al., NOMS 2026](https://www.net.in.tum.de/fileadmin/bibtex/publications/papers/spaethj-noms2026.pdf)). Firefox shipped Linux GSO and GRO, but disabled Windows URO and rolled back USO after driver crashes, and macOS has no UDP GSO ([PT 2.3–2.5][PT]).

**What current tools cannot see or do**, with the evidence in [GO][GO] and the DevTools notes:

| Gap | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Why** a request failed. Fetch's network error has no reason; the opacity toward the page is deliberate, but browsers do not tell the user either | [Fetch: network error](https://fetch.spec.whatwg.org/#concept-network-error); [GO 1.1–1.3, 1.9][GO] |
| The network partition key, cache verdict, reuse or coalescing reason and service-worker hop of a request | HAR has no fields for them and is marked "DO NOT USE"; WebDriver BiDi's timing section ends in "TODO: Add service worker fields" ([GO 1.5–1.6][GO]) |
| Packet- and protocol-level conditions per tab or per site | Chrome's throttling is request-level; CDP's loss and reordering parameters are WebRTC-only; BiDi standardizes only "offline" ([GO 4.1–4.5][GO]) |
| Exact replay of a page load | Best external fidelity was 12.4% median error; tools defeat TLS and strip ECH keys to see traffic ([GO 3.2, 3.7][GO]) |
| Who received what about the user | Privacy UIs count blocks, not flows; the client IP is the flow no browser shows ([GO 5.1–5.5][GO]) |
| Who is intercepting a request | Neither BiDi nor CDP records who intercepts or tells the user; BiDi interception cannot be scoped to a user context ([GO 8.1–8.4][GO]) |
| A conformance suite for an HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, DNS or proxy **client** | h2spec tests servers only and only RFC 7540; no stub-resolver or PAC suite exists ([PR 2.6, 6.6, 8.5][PR]) |

**What Izcal could do that no browser does** (ranked in [GO 9][GO]): typed failure reasons for the user and their own DevTools; exact network replay plus re-simulation under other conditions; protocol-level shaping per tab, origin and container; a published decision schema joined to qlog; a local "who received what" ledger; scoped and audited agent interception; one view of every partitioned store. Each is possible because Izcal owns the stack and the substrate, and each has a privacy constraint stated in Section 6.19.

## 4. First principles

### 4.1 Users' real tasks

| Task | What the stack must provide |
|---|---|
| "Load this page fast on this network" | The right protocol per path (HTTP/3 where UDP works and helps, TCP fallback within one connection-attempt delay), batched receive, cheap ACKs, a request scheduler that knows the page's render order |
| "Keep my identities apart" | Every piece of network state keyed by partition, including state other browsers leave global; containers that are a process boundary; routes that cannot leak |
| "Tell me what went wrong, in words I understand" | A typed failure reason for every failed fetch, shown to the user and their own tools, never to the page |
| "Do not drain my battery while I am not looking" | No keepalives for hidden pages, no periodic network work of Izcal's own, timers on the global wake grid |
| "Let me reproduce and fix this" (developers, Izcal engineers) | Recorded input that replays every layer above decryption; the same state machines under the simulator with faults |
| "Let my agent or test drive the network safely" | Interception scoped to origins and containers, typed by intent, visible and audited |

### 4.2 Platform primitives

- **Protocols, as normative text.** 43 RFCs in scope, about 1,490 printed pages and 2,336 MUST tokens; RFC 9110, 9000, 9846, 9113, 9114 and 9001 are about 43% of it ([PR 0][PR]). Most RFCs anchor at section granularity.
- **Fetch as the spine.** WHATWG Fetch has 136 algorithm blocks and about 1,362 steps ([PR 7.1][PR]): every HTTP, DNS and TLS call in Izcal is invoked from a Fetch step, so the network crate's seams mirror Fetch's (fetch params, obtain a connection, HTTP-network fetch).
- **OS I/O.** Non-blocking sockets per OS (epoll, kqueue, IOCP), UDP GSO and GRO on Linux, USO and URO on Windows (unreliable), no GSO on macOS; ECN on about half of Firefox's QUIC paths ([PT 2.3–2.8][PT]). io_uring is excluded ([H2 4.6][H2]).
- **TLS.** RFC 9846 (TLS 1.3, re-issued July 2026) and its extensions are normative text Izcal implements itself (NW-D22); RFC 9001 defines the key interface QUIC needs, and RFC 9147 the DTLS 1.3 record layer over the same handshake. The primitives are standardized too (FIPS 180, 197, 203; RFC 7748, 8032, 8439, 5869, 9180), and **MIT-dual Rust implementations exist for the symmetric and hash primitives and for ML-KEM** (RustCrypto), with **formally generated field arithmetic** for the curves (`fiat-crypto`). The public-key layer above that field arithmetic has no MIT-only implementation (Section 7). CPU AES and carry-less-multiply instructions (AES-NI and PCLMULQDQ, ARMv8 AES and PMULL) are on every reference device ([H2 4.7][H2]).
- **Izcal's own substrate.** `NetTransport` is held only by the network service (Stage 1 3.1); sequences run tasks to completion (CS-D8); the simulator owns a virtual clock and recorded network archives (Stage 1 3.2).

### 4.3 Izcal's principles that bind this design

P1 outranks P4 without exception: no network state is shared across partitions for a cache-hit gain, and no sandbox hole is accepted for convenience. P2 binds through the conformance oracles. P3 means every accelerated path (GSO, GRO, HTTP/3, 0-RTT) has a correct fallback, and every offload has a runtime kill switch. M-1 binds the code (Section 7). B1 binds the structure: the protocol code never touches the OS.

### 4.4 Assumptions kept and broken

| Assumption from existing stacks | Keep or break | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Networking runs in one separate process | **Keep the separation, break the "one"**: one service per (top-level site, container), plus a service per container for unpartitioned cookies and encrypted DNS (NW-D3) | Almost every piece of network state is already keyed by top-level site and container (Section 6.8), so splitting loses almost no sharing, and a compromise or abort reaches one top-level site's state, not every site's |
| The network process opens its own sockets | **Break** (NW-D4) | Route, Local Network Access and bad-port policy are then enforced by a process that parses no network bytes, against grants it issued itself; the network services need no socket rights in their sandbox |
| The OS resolver is the source of truth for names | **Break**, keep the OS as the source of configuration (NW-D8) | Only an own stub gets HTTPS records on every OS; OS configuration (servers, split rules) still decides where queries go |
| TLS comes from a library | **Break** (NW-D22) | MIT only (2.3.2); a sans-IO handshake core shared by TLS, QUIC and DTLS; Izcal controls its own wire shape |
| A browser consumes another program's root store | **Break** (NW-D5) | The store is policy; shipping another program's data is excluded by licence (2.3.2), and taking its decisions unreviewed is not a policy |
| The platform verifier is the trust policy | **Break** (NW-D5) | Uniform behaviour on every OS, CT and revocation everywhere, and verification as a pure function the simulator can replay |
| HTTP/1.1 pipelining, HTTP/2 push and the RFC 7540 tree | **Break**: none of them | Failed in deployment ([HP 1–2][HP]); `SETTINGS_ENABLE_PUSH = 0`, never `MAX_PUSH_ID` |
| One global socket cap | **Break** | It is an unclosed cross-site channel in every engine ([PP 1.12][PP]); budgets are per partition (Section 6.8) |
| Some state is safe to keep global (HSTS dynamic state, proxy DNS, DoH, the verifier's caches) | **Break**, except static data (HSTS preload list, root store, CT log list, revocation filters) | Each global piece is a supercookie or a timing channel ([PP 1.8, 1.22][PP]) |
| Throttling and replay belong outside the browser | **Break** | The network service owns transport and partition key, so both can be per tab and exact (Section 6.18) |
| `async`/`await` on a runtime is how network code is written | **Break** (NW-D1, CS-D8) | Sans-IO state machines on substrate sequences are deterministic, fuzzable and need no executor |
| Fetch failure reasons are opaque | **Keep toward the page; break toward the user** | The opacity is a security property ([GO 1.2][GO]) |
| A new browser should copy Chrome's wire fingerprint | **Break** (NW-D12) | Impersonation is dishonest and brittle; Izcal keeps an honest shape and decides the remaining choices (GREASE, UA) by measuring challenge rates, plus outreach |

## 5. Lessons learned

These are ideas and evidence, never code or templates (M-1).

| Project | What happened | Lesson | How Izcal applies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pipelining, False Start, TCP Fast Open, Must-Staple | Each depended on every middlebox or server behaving; each was withdrawn ([HP 1, 6][HP]) | A feature that fails when the other side misbehaves will be withdrawn | Prefer end-to-end, encrypted mechanisms with a safe fallback; QUIC always races TCP |
| HTTP/2 push and priority tree | The signal came from the party without the information ([HP 2.3–2.5][HP]) | Signals flow from whoever has the information | Client-decided mechanisms: Early Hints, RFC 9218 priorities, the request scheduler |
| Google QUIC | Logging every packet event found a decade-old CUBIC bug; header encryption kept middleboxes out ([HP 3.5–3.6][HP]) | Observability and encryption are survival features | qlog from the first QUIC commit; GREASE and version negotiation from the start |
| Chromium Network Service sandbox | Out of process since 2019, still unsandboxed by default on Android, Windows and Linux ([SR 6.1][SR]); every ambient OS dependency must be brokered before a network process can be sandboxed ([SR 6.4][SR], first principles) | Retrofitting a sandbox costs years | Sandboxed from the first commit; every OS dependency a typed request, served by the broker or a sandboxed OS-adapter process; sockets minted by the broker (NW-D4) |
| Firefox socket process | Open since December 2016 ([HP 4.5][HP]) | Moving code into a process late is the expensive direction | Network code is born in its process and under the simulator |
| Ladybird RequestServer on libcurl | Two years after adopting a generic client, response-cookie handling moved into the network process to stop a compromised renderer reaching other sites' HttpOnly cookies ([HP 4.8][HP]) | Browser semantics belong inside the network service, not layered over a generic client | Fetch's rules (ports, methods, credentials, redirects) are implemented where connections are made |
| curl and hyper | The cost was the foreign-language boundary; the gain was stricter parsing ([SR 2.3][SR]) | Strictness is the security win | Strict parsers everywhere; every "MAY accept leniently" resolved to reject unless the corpus shows breakage |
| quinn, quiche | Pre-authentication panics from `unwrap()` on peer varints and overflow on peer packet numbers ([SR 1.8–1.9][SR]) | Under abort-on-panic, one hostile site would end every site's networking | Checked arithmetic on every peer number, budgets on every peer-driven allocation, Kani proofs on the arithmetic kernels, and a service per (top-level site, container) to bound the blast radius |
| Request smuggling, client-side desync, URL and cookie confusion | Two parsers disagreed about one message ([SR 2.4–2.7][SR]) | Disagreement is the vulnerability | One URL parser, one header parser, one cookie parser in the whole browser; an HTTP/1.1 framing anomaly makes the connection non-reusable |
| Chrome Root Store | Platform verifiers gave inconsistent behaviour ([SR 4.1][SR]) | One verifier everywhere | NW-D5: one verifier and one Izcal root store on every OS |
| DigiNotar, Symantec, Entrust distrusts | Each removal of a CA took a staffed program months of review and a dated plan ([SR 4.7, 4.11][SR]) | A root store is an operation with people, not a file | The Izcal root program is staffed before NW-M2, with a published policy, review of each CA's audits and incidents, and a distrust process (NW-D5, Section 6.5.2) |
| Heartbleed, goto fail, SMACK | Unneeded features, a skipped check and flag-driven state machines broke TLS libraries (Section 2.1) | TLS bugs live in the implementation's structure | `hg-tls`: no feature without a user, the handshake as one transition table that rejects every other message, verification as a pure function, a constant-time rule for secrets, and an independent audit (NW-D22, NW-52) |
| Firefox 85 and Chrome partitioning | Measured cost ≤ 1.32% page load ([PP 1.14][PP]) | Partitioning is cheap enough to be the only mode | Every axis in Section 6.8, including those others left global; the remaining shared limits recorded as bounded channels |
| Connection-pool XS-Leak | A global socket cap is shared timing state ([PP 1.12][PP]) | Limits are channels too | Separate services per top-level site, budgets per top-level site then per partition, a randomized service capacity; the residual recorded as bounded |
| Firefox Windows UDP offload | Driver crashes and packet loss forced rollbacks ([PT 2.4][PT]) | Offloads are driver-dependent | Runtime probing and a kill switch per offload, self-detected failure falls back |
| Mahimahi, WebPageReplay, mitmproxy | External replay guesses and must break TLS ([GO 3.2–3.7][GO]) | Record inside the stack, above decryption | NW-D2 |
| Chrome DevTools throttling | Request-level, cannot model loss ([GO 4.1][GO]) | Model the network where the packets are | Link and protocol shaping in the service, per partition |
| Okta HAR breach | Exported logs carried session tokens ([DT 2.1][DT]) | Exports are data flows | Redacted by default through `hg-redact` (DevTools spec) |
| Privacy Sandbox, SDCH, TFO cookies, DNT | Cross-site state and fingerprintable bits were pulled ([HP 6][HP]) | New state is a privacy design problem first | Every new store joins the partition table before it ships |

Every protocol crate is a **sans-IO state machine**: it takes bytes, datagrams, timer expiries and the current time as inputs, and returns bytes to send, timer deadlines and events. Only `hg-net-io` connects them to `NetTransport` and the substrate's sequences. The same crates therefore run against real sockets, against `hg-netsim` under the seeded scheduler, inside fuzzers, and in replay.

### 6.1 Process placement, the socket broker and brokered OS access (NW-D3, NW-D4, NW-D20)

**Services per (top-level site, container) (NW-D3).** I-1 makes the container a policy context, and I-1 (b) keys every piece of network state by top-level site and container. Section 6.8's table shows that almost every row contains both T and C: cache, connections, DNS, Alt-Svc, resumption, HSTS, client certificates, CORS preflight, partitioned cookies, reporting, service-worker registrations. A service per (T, C) therefore loses almost no sharing, and it gives the network the property site isolation gives renderers: **a page reaches only its own top-level site's network state**. A hostile site that compromises the service it talks to as a top-level page gets its own partitions, not the user's bank's cookies; a hostile third party embedded by the bank reaches the bank's partitions, which it could already make requests in. The rows that are not keyed by T are served by two other services:

- **Container service** (one per active container): the **unpartitioned first-party cookie jars**, Storage Access grants, and the encrypted-DNS transport connections (DoH, DoT, DoQ) keyed (C, R). A partition service for T receives, from the broker, a bound endpoint that can read and write **only site T's jar**, plus the jar of each site S that holds a Storage Access grant under T (IPC-R2; the container service checks the binding on every call, IPC-R17). All third-party cookies are partitioned by default (Section 6.9), so no other unpartitioned access exists.
- **System service** (one): Izcal's own traffic (Section 6.13), never web content.

Read-only data (content-blocking automata, root store, CT log list, revocation filters, HSTS preload list, Public Suffix List) are sealed files mapped by every service; clean file-backed pages are not charged (footprint 6.2), so they cost each service only its page tables.

**Lifecycle.** A partition service starts when the broker commits the first navigation or fetch for a new (T, C), from a **spare** (an empty, pre-spawned network-service process that holds no state until the broker binds it, as FP-D1 permits for renderers), so a new top-level site waits for an IPC binding, not a process spawn (NW-47). It exits when no document, worker, download or WebRTC session uses (T, C) and its idle connections have closed. Navigation redirects across sites are issued by the broker's `hg-navigation` to the target site's service hop by hop, which is what Fetch's manual-redirect navigation model already does. Private windows are ephemeral containers with their own services and no disk state.

**Why not one service per container or one in all (NW-D3 options).** One per container (draft 0.1) puts every site of the container in one address space: a compromise or panic reaches every site's sessions, which is what every shipping browser accepts. One per (T, C) costs one more process per open top-level site. The comparison and reversal trigger are in Section 9.4; the cost is bounded by NW-35 and NW-47, and footprint's own precedent (FP-D8) is that isolation is not the lever for a memory target.

**Sockets are minted by the broker (NW-D4).** No network service may create sockets: on Linux each runs with an empty network namespace and seccomp denies `socket`; on macOS its SBPL profile denies network-outbound; on Windows it runs in an AppContainer without `internetClient` (Q-NW-2 measures this at NW-M0). It receives sockets as typed handles (IPC-R1) through one call:

`mint(kind {tcp-connect, udp-connect, udp-bound}, address, port, purpose {fetch, dns, proxy, webrtc}, lna_ticket?)`

The service's identity (T, C, route) is bound to its endpoint, so it is never in the message. The broker checks, against state it owns:

- **Bad ports**: the Fetch list ([Fetch §2.9](https://fetch.spec.whatwg.org/#bad-port)) for `fetch` and `webrtc`.
- **Route**: a container with a SOCKS or HTTP proxy route can only obtain sockets to that proxy; a WireGuard route only socket pairs into its route process; a direct container direct sockets.
- **Purpose `dns`**: only to resolver addresses and ports from the OS configuration the broker read itself, or the user's chosen encrypted resolver. **Purpose `proxy`**: only to proxies from the configuration the broker read, or returned by the PAC process **to the broker** (below).
- **Local Network Access**: a public, local or loopback **address class** ([LNA](https://wicg.github.io/local-network-access/)) for `fetch` and `webrtc` requires an **LNA ticket**. The broker issues a ticket per frame and per worker when it creates that context's `FetchEndpoint`, and reissues it when the user grants or revokes LNA for (site, container); it is an unforgeable token (a broker-held MAC over frame identity, granted address classes and an expiry). The renderer's request carries it as data, the network service forwards it, and the broker accepts a local or loopback mint only with a ticket it issued whose grant covers the address class. A compromised network service can therefore reach local addresses only for frames of its own top-level site that hold a grant, never with another site's grant.

The broker then creates the socket, binds it and (for TCP) starts a non-blocking connect, and passes the handle. A redirect, a DNS change, a QUIC client migration or a server's `preferred_address` that lands on a new address mints a new socket under the same checks, so LNA is applied **to the address actually connected**, every connection, which is also the DNS-rebinding defence NET-R7 asks for ([SR 7.3–7.4][SR]). Bound, unconnected UDP sockets exist only for WebRTC and are constrained differently (Section 6.12). The cost is one IPC round trip per new connection, measured by NW-44; Do53 queries reuse a small per-service pool of broker-minted UDP sockets with random source ports, refreshed on the wake grid, so port randomization does not cost a mint per query.

**PAC goes to the broker too.** The proxy-resolver utility process (Section 6.13) returns its result to the broker as well as to the network service; the broker validates it against the container's proxy policy and admits the returned proxy addresses for `proxy` mints. A PAC script on a hostile network (WPAD) therefore cannot be replaced by a compromised network service's invention, and WPAD is restricted (Section 6.13).

**OS calls on network data run in a sandboxed OS-adapter process, not in the broker.** The broker is unsandboxed and must parse no network bytes (IPC-R15), yet three OS services consume data a server controls: the **OS resolver** fallback (glibc's `getaddrinfo` parses DNS responses in the calling process), **SSPI and GSSAPI** (they parse `WWW-Authenticate: Negotiate` and NTLM challenges), and **client-key signing** (smart cards and OS key stores). Each runs in an **OS-adapter utility process per container**, sandboxed to the one OS service it needs, reached only by the container's services through broker-bound endpoints. Two policies bound what a compromised network service can ask of it (NW-D20):

- **Ambient authentication** (Negotiate, NTLM, Kerberos) only for hosts on an enterprise or user allowlist, per container; the default list is empty, so no public server can make Izcal produce NTLM responses or Kerberos tickets. The broker gives the adapter the allowlist; the adapter refuses other hosts.
- **Client-key signing** only for a (container, host) pair the user selected through the consent path (security scope CON-R1), with a per-handshake grant the broker issues and the adapter checks; the private key never leaves the OS store and never enters a network service.

User- and enterprise-added trust anchors are read by the broker and passed to `hg-pki` as opaque DER; the broker does not parse them. OS proxy settings, resolver configuration and network-change events are configuration data in formats the OS returns as structured values, read by the broker as today.

**Renderer ↔ network service.** A renderer reaches the partition service of its document's top-level site only through a `FetchEndpoint` bound per frame and per worker (IPC-R2), so initiator, site, container, partition key, frame identity and LNA ticket come from the binding or from the broker (NET-R1). A request carries the Fetch request's fields as typed data (method, URL as a validated `hg-url` type, header list, mode, credentials mode, destination, priority, referrer policy, integrity, keepalive), never a socket address. Bodies cross as read-only shared memory or a data pipe; responses stream back as a data pipe with flow control. ORB filtering happens before any byte reaches the pipe (NET-R2).

### 6.2 The protocol core: sans-IO, budgets and tier P (NW-D1)

**Shape.** Each protocol is a state machine type with three kinds of entry points: `on_bytes`/`on_datagram(now, data, metadata)`, `on_timer(now)`, and application calls (`send_request`, `reset_stream`). Each returns a bounded list of **actions**: bytes or datagrams to transmit, timer deadlines to arm, events for the layer above. There is no `async`, no executor, no callback into the OS and no clock read (time is a parameter). Values that must be unpredictable on the wire (QUIC connection IDs, stateless-reset tokens, PATH_CHALLENGE data, address-validation tokens, TLS randoms) are drawn from **`Entropy::crypto()`**, the OS CSPRNG in production; seeded named streams (`Entropy::stream`) are used only for values with no security or unlinkability requirement. `hg-net-io` runs one sequence per connection group and posts actions to `NetTransport`; that is the only code that touches I/O. This is CS-D8's model, so Q-CS-2's decision extends to every protocol and the CS-AS-4 allowlist needs no network entry.

**Budgets.** Every state machine holds a `Budget` (in `hg-net-wire`), charged **at allocation time** for every byte or object a peer can make it hold or queue: header-block bytes during HPACK and QPACK decoding (never after), CONTINUATION frames, reassembly gaps, ACK ranges, connection IDs, pending PATH_RESPONSE and RETIRE_CONNECTION_ID frames, queued control replies, decompressed bytes. A charge that fails is a typed stream or connection error, never a panic. Per-connection budgets roll up into per-partition and per-service budgets. The concrete limits are named constants in one module (NW-15), each exercised by a test at the limit and at limit + 1 ([SR 8][SR]); they start from the security note's proposals (R3–R15) and are validated against a crawl before freezing (Q-NW-9).

**Tier P discipline.** All network crates are tier P (coding standard): no `unwrap`, `expect`, indexing or unchecked arithmetic on peer-supplied values; varint, packet-number, ACK-range, flow-control and window arithmetic are small checked kernels with **Kani proofs** (bounded) of no overflow and no panic; allocation sized by peer values goes through `try_reserve` with a cap (CS-ER-9). A TLS error closes one connection. Overflow checks in release would turn an overflow into an abort, so the rule is explicit checked arithmetic, not a compiler flag ([SR 1, Implications][SR]).

**One parser per format.** `hg-url` (the browser's one URL parser, owned by the HTML and CSS parsing and style spec since the reconciliation, [P6][RN]), the header-field, Structured Fields and MIME type parsers in `hg-net-wire` (MIME types and sniffing per the WHATWG MIME Sniffing standard, used by ORB here and by strict MIME checks and `charset` parameters in renderers), and `hg-cookie-parse` (the cookie parser and serializer, split out of `hg-cookies`) are the only ones in the browser. The renderer's `Headers` object, `document.cookie`'s writer, the service worker, the cache and the network services call the same functions, so no two components can disagree about a message ([SR 2.4–2.7][SR]). **`ReputationCanonicalUrl` is one function over `hg-url`**, used by the phishing build and the client, with a shared golden corpus and one index hash; no crate adds a second URL normaliser (phishing spec PH-D3). Crates linked into both renderers and network services are declared tier P (coding standard tier table) and live in the shared sub-layer L2s, never in L5 (the style spec's G24, [P1][RN]).

### 6.3 HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 and HTTP/3

**HTTP/1.1** ([RFC 9112](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9112)). Strict response parser: the eight message-body-length rules of §6.3 in order; a response with both `Content-Length` and `Transfer-Encoding`, differing duplicate `Content-Length`, obs-fold, bare CR, NUL or whitespace before the colon is an error ([PR 1.3][PR]). **Any framing anomaly marks the connection non-reusable**, which closes the client-side desync class ([PortSwigger 2022](https://portswigger.net/research/browser-powered-desync-attacks)). No pipelining. No bytes after an `Upgrade` or CONNECT request before the 101/2xx ([RFC 9931](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9931)). At most 6 connections per (partition, origin, credentials) key, the common browser default ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/Guides/Connection_management_in_HTTP_1.x), [PT 5.5][PT]).

**HTTP/2** ([RFC 9113](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113), [RFC 7541](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7541)). Izcal's own sans-IO codec (Q-CS-2): frame layer, the seven-state stream machine, two-level flow control, HPACK with the decoded-size budget, extended CONNECT ([RFC 8441](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8441)) for WebSockets and WebTransport. `SETTINGS_ENABLE_PUSH = 0` and a PUSH_PROMISE is a protocol error; `SETTINGS_NO_RFC7540_PRIORITIES = 1`; priorities by RFC 9218 only. Control-frame floods (PING, SETTINGS, empty frames, WINDOW_UPDATE 0) are counted and answered with GOAWAY ENHANCE_YOUR_CALM ([SR 1.5][SR]). No timer-driven PING; a **liveness PING is sent with a request** that reuses a connection idle for more than 30 s† or after a network change, so a dead connection is detected without a keepalive (Section 6.17).

**HTTP/3 and QPACK** ([RFC 9114](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9114), [RFC 9204](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9204)). Control and QPACK streams; the encoder may use a small dynamic table, the decoder implements the full instruction set with `SETTINGS_QPACK_BLOCKED_STREAMS` and table capacity capped; never sends `MAX_PUSH_ID`; `initial_max_streams_bidi = 0` for the server ([SR R14][SR]). HTTP datagrams and capsules ([RFC 9297](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9297)) and extended CONNECT ([RFC 9220](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9220)) are part of the crate from the start, because WebTransport and MASQUE need them.

**Priorities and the request scheduler.** Priority is a field on every Fetch request from the loader to the wire. The renderer's loader sets it from destination, render-blocking status, `fetchpriority` and viewport position, and updates it (an image entering the viewport); `hg-layout-query` publishes per-frame viewport-intersection classes for pending image and iframe fetches to the loader, and each viewport-driven priority update and each lazy-load start is a recorded decision point (JS-D15). Image natural sizes are taken from the first decoded header, a recorded input, and full decode never changes layout. `hg-net` maps the priority to RFC 9218 urgency and the incremental flag, sends `PRIORITY_UPDATE` on change, and applies the same order to its own queues: which request gets a connection slot first and which stream's data is decrypted and delivered first. The client side matters: `fetchpriority=high` on Google Flights' LCP image moved LCP from 2.6 to 1.9 s, while in 2019 most servers' HTTP/2 prioritization was broken ([PT 5.1–5.3][PT]). NW-27 measures LCP against an oracle ordering.

**103 Early Hints.** Parsed by every HTTP version; preload and preconnect handled as HTML specifies for navigations ([PR 1.6][PR]), within the navigation's partition.

**Content codings.** gzip, deflate, br, zstd (window ≤ 8 MB, [RFC 9659](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9659)) and dictionary transport `dcb` and `dcz` ([RFC 9842](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9842)), decoded in the partition service by `hg-compress` under the decompression budget (output ratio ≤ 1:1,024† and an absolute cap of 64 MiB† per destination; at most two stacked codings). Dictionaries are cache entries in the partition that fetched them. Google Search measured 23% smaller HTML and 1.7% better LCP with dictionaries ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/search-compression-dictionaries?hl=en)). Decoding stays in the network service because ORB sniffing, the cache and dictionary matching need decoded bytes; the decoders are Izcal's own tier-P code in the shared sub-layer L2s, shared with the text stack's WOFF 1.0 and 2.0 support and the image decoder process's PNG inflate (NW-D6); `hg-compress` also holds the deflate and gzip encoders that Compression Streams and `permessage-deflate` need ([P5][RN]); the encoder uses fixed settings and produces bit-identical output across ISA extensions and thread counts (G55, canvas export). A gzip-magic `image/svg+xml` body and a local `.svgz` file are decoded in the partition service under this same budget, then follow the SVG spec's static-document path (SVG spec R13).

### 6.4 QUIC

**Transport** ([RFC 9000](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9000), [RFC 9001](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9001), [RFC 9002](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9002)). Izcal writes packets, frames, streams, flow control, connection IDs, path validation, migration, stateless reset, address-validation tokens, loss detection and recovery; `hg-tls`'s QUIC mode supplies the TLS handshake and the packet and header protection keys, exactly RFC 9001's interface ([PR 3.3][PR]), with no TLS record layer. Version 1 and v2 with compatible version negotiation ([RFC 9368](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9368), [RFC 9369](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9369)), greased QUIC bit ([RFC 9287](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9287)), DATAGRAM ([RFC 9221](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9221)). Connection IDs, stateless-reset tokens and PATH_CHALLENGE data come from `Entropy::crypto()`, so migrated paths are unlinkable as RFC 9000 §9.5 requires. An ACK of a packet number never sent is PROTOCOL_VIOLATION; all packet-number and window arithmetic goes through the Kani-proved kernels ([SR 1.9][SR]). The path abstraction and the ACK policy are designed so multipath and ACK frequency can be added without restructuring; under the one gate rule of Section 9.1 they are built against their pinned drafts and enabled after interop ([PR 3.6][PR]).

**Receive path first.** A browser is the receiver, and the receiver is where HTTP/3 loses ([PT 1.3][PT]). The design requirements: batch receive (GRO on Linux; `recvmmsg` where GRO is absent; Windows URO only after self-check), one long-lived receive buffer per service with **in-place decryption**, frame parsing without copies (frame parsing was the largest network-thread cost in the WWW 2024 measurement), and an **ACK policy** that acknowledges every second ack-eliciting packet at low rates but uses a larger ack-eliciting threshold and the maximum ACK delay at high rates within RFC 9000's rules, so ACK generation stops being a large share of receive CPU. Send side: GSO on Linux and Windows USO after self-check, pacing compatible with GSO by pacing per GSO burst ([PT 2.6][PT]).

**Datapath per OS (`hg-net-io`).** Each offload (GSO, GRO, USO, URO, `sendmmsg`/`recvmmsg`, ECN marking and reporting) is **probed at runtime, gated by a self-check** (for example, a missing segment size on a coalesced read disables URO for that socket) **and has a kill switch** in configuration. No offload is assumed. macOS has no UDP GSO and only undocumented batching calls, which Izcal does not use; the macOS receive cost is measured, not hidden (Q-NW-4). ECN is reported from the start: about half of Firefox's QUIC paths support it ([PT 2.8][PT]). Path MTU: 1,200 bytes initial, DPLPMTUD ([RFC 8899](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8899)) probing upward.

**Congestion control (NW-D9).** A congestion-controller trait with **CUBIC ([RFC 9438](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438)), HyStart++ ([RFC 9406](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9406)) and pacing** as the default; BBRv3 ([draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/)) and NewReno as built alternatives. A browser's congestion controller governs uploads, request bursts and WebTransport, and its ACK policy governs download CPU ([PT 3.5][PT]). HyStart++ halved retransmitted bytes in Microsoft's lab and had been on by default for all Windows TCP for over two years by 2023, unpaced, whereas Izcal's default is the paced variant ([PT 3.3][PT]). The default changes only on measurement (Section 9.4).

**0-RTT and resumption (NW-D14).** Session tickets and QUIC resumption state are keyed by the full connection key (Section 6.8), single-use, capped at 24 h†, and cleared with the partition's cookies and on any route change. 0-RTT carries only safe, idempotent requests without bodies (GET and HEAD), and a 425 Too Early replays after the handshake ([RFC 8470](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8470)). Most of QUIC's search-latency gain came from 0-RTT ([PT 1.1][PT]).

**Idle.** QUIC connections close after 30 s† idle; no PING keeps an idle connection alive (Section 6.17).

### 6.5 TLS, cryptography, ECH and the certificate verifier (NW-D5, NW-D6, NW-D19, NW-D21, NW-D22, NW-D23)

**Izcal's own TLS stack (NW-D22).** Under HGBA-2 2.3.2 the TLS protocol is Izcal's own. rustls was the plan through 0.3.1, but rustls 0.23.46 always links rustls-webpki and `untrusted` (ISC) and `subtle` (BSD-3-Clause), and on rustls `main` (0.24-dev) `webpki` becomes optional while `subtle` stays mandatory ([LA 2.2][LA]). rustls, BoringSSL, OpenSSL, NSS, Go's `crypto/tls` and other stacks are black-box test peers and oracles only (M-1 part 4, OD-L2); none is read as a design source (part 3). `hg-tls` is designed from what a browser client needs:

- **Scope.** TLS 1.3 from RFC 9846 is the protocol; RFC 8446 is not the anchor ([PR 5.1][PR]). A **TLS 1.2 compatibility mode** ([RFC 5246](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246)) exists because a browser that cannot reach TLS 1.2-only servers fails P2. It is limited to ECDHE key exchange, AEAD suites (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305) and the extended master secret ([RFC 7627](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7627)), with renegotiation refused and no RSA key exchange, CBC suites, static DH or compression. How many corpus origins still need TLS 1.2, or a suite outside that set, is measured at NW-M0 (Q-NW-13); a suite is added only on that evidence and with an owner decision. No SSL, TLS 1.0 or TLS 1.1.
- **Sans-IO, one handshake core.** Like every protocol here (NW-D1), `hg-tls` takes handshake bytes, records, time and entropy, and returns records, key-change events and verification requests. The **handshake core** (message codec, transition table, key schedule, extensions) is one module with three framings: the TLS record layer over TCP; **QUIC mode** (RFC 9001: handshake bytes in CRYPTO frames, secrets per encryption level, no record layer); and **DTLS** (RFC 9147 and RFC 6347, Section 6.12). WebRTC's DTLS is therefore not a second TLS implementation.
- **The handshake is a transition table.** Each state names the messages it accepts; any other message is an `unexpected_message` alert. The table is data, checked against RFC 9846's message-flow figures by a test that walks every path and every out-of-order message, which is the class of bug SMACK found (Section 2.1). Model learning (Section 6.19) checks the built code against the table.
- **Features with a user, and nothing else.** Full handshake with HelloRetryRequest; key shares X25519MLKEM768 (the default, HGBA-2 4.7), X25519 and P-256; PSK resumption with `psk_dhe_ke` only; 0-RTT under NW-D14; KeyUpdate; ALPN; SNI; certificate compression ([RFC 8879](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8879)) with `hg-compress`'s decoders; the record size limit ([RFC 8449](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8449)); `status_request` and `signed_certificate_timestamp` for stapled OCSP and SCTs; client certificates, signed through the OS-adapter process (Section 6.1). Not built: post-handshake client authentication (HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 forbid it), raw public keys, renegotiation, and any custom-extension API.
- **Verification is a request, not a callback.** The handshake returns a `VerifyRequest` (chain, SCTs, stapled response, server name, time) and waits; `hg-pki` answers it with a pure function (below). No verifier code runs inside the state machine.
- **Secrets.** Traffic secrets, key-schedule inputs and ephemeral private keys live in `hg-crypto` types that zeroize on drop and have no `Debug`, `Clone` or serialization; the recording boundary never sees them (Section 6.16, NW-39). One named, audited capability, `expose_bytes(sink)`, admits bytes out of those types only at the clone-wire writer and the export/wrap codecs (the WebCrypto spec's WC-D6, `hg-webcrypto`), and an `ast-grep` lint rejects it everywhere else.
- **Wire shape.** The ClientHello is Izcal's own design, GREASE included (Section 6.20).

**Cryptography (`hg-crypto`, NW-D6, NW-D23).** One crate is the only place in the browser that implements or imports a cryptographic primitive (G36). Its composition follows the licence rule and the constant-time surface:

Everything else is **verification on public data** (ECDSA, RSA and Ed25519 signatures on certificates and handshakes), where timing reveals nothing secret, so that code is written for correctness and speed, not constant time. Client-certificate private keys never leave the OS store (Section 6.1). The hand-written secret-dependent code is therefore X25519, P-256 and P-384 scalar multiplication, P-256, P-384 and Ed25519 signing, RSA private-key operations and the WebCrypto codecs' padding, integrity and error-path checks, all on generated field arithmetic except RSA's own bignum. ECDSA nonces are deterministic with added randomness ([RFC 6979](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6979), hedged), and RSA private-key operations are blinded. The rule for secret-dependent code: no secret-dependent branch, memory index or variable-time instruction, checked on each target ISA by a binary-level taint check over the compiled code and by statistical timing tests in the style of dudect ([Reparaz et al. 2016](https://eprint.iacr.org/2016/1123)) (NW-50).

**Correctness evidence for every primitive.** Wycheproof vectors ([C2SP Wycheproof](https://github.com/C2SP/wycheproof)), NIST ACVP vectors ([ACVP](https://pages.nist.gov/ACVP/)) where they exist, the RFCs' own vectors, and differential fuzzing against two black-box implementations per primitive (test-only, OD-L2) (NW-49). Bulk AEAD speed matters, because after packet I/O, crypto is the next receive bottleneck (Section 3), so NW-51 holds `hg-crypto` to the speed of the fastest black-box library on each reference CPU.

**Independent audit.** An external firm audits `hg-crypto`, `hg-tls` and `hg-dtls` before the first public release (NW-M5), and every high or critical finding is fixed before that release. A re-audit follows any change to the key schedule, the record layer or a primitive's implementation, and happens at least yearly† (NW-52). CONTRIBUTING's review item on hand-rolled cryptography gets one carve-out: `hg-crypto`, under this audit and NW-49 and NW-50 (Section 9.5).

**ECH** ([RFC 9849](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9849), [RFC 9848](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9848)). Offered whenever the HTTPS record carries `ech`; GREASE ECH otherwise; `retry_configs` handled with the public name authenticated. Per RFC 9848 §5.1, when SVCB resolution succeeded and every alternative endpoint advertises `ech`, Izcal **never falls back to a connection without ECH**, so the origin's name never appears in a cleartext SNI (the outer SNI carries only the ECH config's public name, RFC 9849 §6.1). ECH protects the name only if the DNS lookup is private, so DNS and ECH are designed as one feature (Section 6.6).

**The verifier (NW-D5) and path builder (NW-D19).** `hg-pki` is Izcal's own verifier, answering `hg-tls`'s verification requests:

- **Path building is Izcal's own** (NW-D19): RFC 5280 validation and RFC 4158-style path building with a signature budget of ≤ 100 checks (the class of RUSTSEC-2023-0053, [SR 4.8][SR]), signatures verified through `hg-crypto`. rustls-webpki is not linked. Draft 0.3.1 said it was "not linked" while rustls 0.23, then a dependency, linked it unconditionally; that was wrong, and with rustls gone nothing pulls it in. Its ISC licence would exclude it anyway. It serves only as a **differential test oracle** (M-1 part 4, OD-L2).
- **Roots from the Izcal root program** (NW-D5, Section 6.5.2): Izcal's own store, with trust bits and SCT-dated distrust constraints, delivered by the trust-data channel (UPD-R1), signed and versioned. Mozilla's CCADB data is not shipped.
- **One distrust model.** A distrust constraint is SCT-dated where the certificate carries SCTs and notBefore-dated otherwise; every decision of the root program is expressed in it.
- **Distrust is the root program's decision.** Another program's announced distrust is evidence that opens an Izcal review within 7 days†, decided within 30 days† (NW-53); Izcal never applies another program's decision without its own review. This answers Q-SEC-5's request for a policy process, with an owner (the program lead), a timeline and an appeal path for the CA.
- **OS trust changes are honoured both ways.** Roots a user or enterprise **added** to the OS stores are trusted, marked locally trusted and exempt from CT ([SR 4.2][SR]); roots the user or enterprise **distrusted or removed** in the OS store are distrusted in Izcal as well. Both are shown in the site panel and DevTools.
- **CT enforced** for publicly trusted certificates, with the published CT log lists as standards data (HGBA-2 2.3.2) and Izcal's log policy; the SCT-count policy follows the published Chrome and Apple policies ([SR 4.6][SR]).
- **Revocation pushed** (NW-D21): a CRLite-style filter (the clubcard format, implemented by Izcal from its published description) and a list of revoked intermediates and key-compromise leaves, **generated by Izcal's trust-data pipeline** from CT logs and CA CRLs (Section 6.5.1); no live OCSP; stapled OCSP honoured when present. Stage 1 13.2's plan (CRLs first, filters later) becomes both from NW-M2, because the largest CA has turned OCSP off and browsers do not query it live ([HP 6.6][HP]).
- **Stale data degrades, it does not lock users out.** When the CT log list or the revocation data is older than 10 weeks†, Izcal keeps the last revocation set, warns in the site panel and on `izcal://network`, and stops **enforcing** CT for public roots, so HTTPS keeps working on a machine that cannot update; a hard failure happens only on a signed kill record from the trust-data channel. P3 (graceful degradation) governs, and a stale set is still better than none.
- **A pure function.** Verification takes the chain, time and the data versions; it reads nothing ambient, so it replays exactly and runs under the simulator.

Why not the platform verifier first (Stage 1 13.2, NET-R6): it differs per OS, enforces no CT, does no revocation on Linux, and is not replayable ([SR 4.9][SR]); Chrome left platform verification for the same inconsistency ([SR 4.1][SR]).

#### 6.5.1 The trust-data pipeline

`hg-trust-build` (tier T) and an operated pipeline produce, sign and publish: **Izcal's root store**, built from the root program's published decisions (on each decision, at most weekly†, kill records at once); the CT log list (the published lists as standards data, filtered by Izcal's log policy); the revocation filter and intermediate list (every 6 h†, generated from all usable CT logs' entries and the CRLs those certificates name); and **Izcal's HSTS preload list** (Section 6.5.3). Outputs are signed under UPD-R1's keys, versioned, served as public files (cacheable, nothing per user, Section 6.13), and monitored (freshness, size, a canary of known-revoked certificates). The pipeline is staffed in the PKI and trust-data track (Section 9.3). HGBA-2 2.3.2 answers its licence question (Q-NW-3): Izcal ships only data it builds and standards data.

#### 6.5.2 The Izcal root program (NW-D5, NW-D24)

A root store is the policy that decides which CAs may vouch for any site the user visits, and the record shows it is an operation run by people, not a file (Section 2.1). Izcal's program has five published parts:

1. **Policy.** The CA/Browser Forum's Baseline Requirements as the floor; annual audits by qualified auditors; every TLS certificate logged in CT; incident reports within a published time; a public process for changing the policy itself.
2. **Inclusion review.** For each CA owner: audit statements, certificate policy and practice statements, incident history and CT issuance behaviour, reviewed by Izcal; each decision and its record published.
3. **Monitoring.** Misissuance checks on the CT stream the revocation pipeline already reads; incident tracking; annual audit renewal.
4. **Distrust.** A trigger (an incident, an audit failure, another program's announced distrust), a review that includes the CA's response, a decision with an SCT date, and a dated plan. Key compromise goes through the trust-data channel's kill record at once.
5. **Participation.** Izcal applies, when eligible, to the CA/Browser Forum as a certificate consumer and to CCADB as a root store operator, the channels through which CAs disclose to programs. What Izcal reads there, CCADB records included (audit statements, CP/CPS links, incident reports), informs its own decisions and is not shipped or copied as a data set (owner decision, 19 October 2026; Q-NW-3 closed).

**Bootstrapping, stated plainly.**

- **The seed.** The first store comes from reviewing, CA owner by CA owner, the CAs whose certificates the corpus and a CT sample actually rely on. Inclusion means Izcal reviewed that owner's current audits, policies and incident record itself. It is not a copy of another program's list, although most included roots will be roots other programs also trust, because they are the CAs the web uses. The initial review covers on the order of 50–70 CA owners† (count unverified) and costs about 1–2 person-years†, inside the program's staffing.
- **When the program is credible.** Credible means a published policy, every included root reviewed, the distrust process run at least once (as a drill if no incident occurs), and CAs answering Izcal's incident questions. That takes about 1–2 years† from staffing ([LA 2.3][LA]). Staffed before NW-M2 (months 18–24†), the program publishes its first store at NW-M2's exit and is credible by NW-M5, Izcal's first public release. Izcal has no users before that release.
- **Before the first store: OS trust stores, in development builds only (NW-D24).** From NW-M1 until the first Izcal store, `hg-pki` verifies against the anchors of the OS trust store, read by the broker through the OS's certificate-store interface and passed as opaque DER like user-added anchors (Section 6.1), with Izcal's own path building, CT and revocation on top. The reason is first-principles, not schedule: before its program has reviewed a CA, Izcal has no judgement of its own about that CA; shipping another program's judgement is excluded by licence; and the OS store is the one judgement already on the user's machine that Izcal neither ships nor copies (an OS interface under HGBA-2 2.3.2). Its cost, behaviour that differs by OS, falls only on developers and testers. Known gap: Windows fills its root store on demand, so an enumerated store can lack roots the OS would fetch; this is measured at NW-M1 (Q-NW-15) and is acceptable in development builds; if a public release ships on the OS stores under NW-D24's trigger, the gap must be closed for that release first.
- **After the first store**, Izcal's store is the only public anchor set; OS stores contribute only user- and enterprise-added anchors and distrust, as above.
- **If the program has not passed its credibility gate (NW-53) at the first public release**, that release ships on the OS trust stores, read through the broker as an OS interface exactly as in development builds, with Izcal's path building, CT and revocation on top, and the site panel says so for every site, until Izcal's store passes NW-53; from then on Izcal's store is the only public anchor set, as above (owner decision, 19 October 2026, NW-D24's trigger). The reason is security, not schedule: a store that has not passed its gate would vet CAs less carefully than the OS vendors' programs, so users are safer on the OS stores. It is never a silent fallback.

**Cost.** 4–6 people† ongoing: a program lead, CA reviewers, incident and compliance handling, and one PKI tooling engineer; the 1–2 person-years† of initial review are inside that. Also CA/Browser Forum and CCADB participation and legal review of the policy. The compatibility risk is a site whose CA Izcal has not included, which fails verification; NW-53 bounds it.

#### 6.5.3 Izcal's HSTS preload list (NW-D25)

**What the list is for now.** With HTTPS-First on by default for public sites (Section 6.7), a first visit already tries HTTPS first. A preload entry adds only what HSTS adds to that: certificate errors cannot be clicked through, there is no HTTP fallback behind an interstitial, and `includeSubDomains` covers names never visited. The list's job is therefore narrow: sites whose operators ask for exactly that.

**Inclusion by the operator's own statement.** The `preload` token in a `Strict-Transport-Security` header has no effect in any browser except as the operator's request to be preloaded; [RFC 6797](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6797) does not define it, and the published criteria of the existing list do ([hstspreload.org](https://hstspreload.org/)). Izcal publishes its own criteria: a valid certificate on the registrable domain; HTTP redirects to HTTPS on the same host; the header on the registrable domain with `max-age` of at least one year†, `includeSubDomains` and `preload`. Candidates come from a submission form and from the registrable domains in the CT entries the revocation pipeline already reads, each probed by an identified crawler at a polite rate. Chromium's list is not used, as a source or as a test input: it is another engine's source file (M-1 part 3) and BSD-licensed ([LA 2.4][LA]).

**Removal.** Every entry is re-checked every 12 weeks†. An entry that fails the criteria at two consecutive checks, or whose operator asks through the form (proved by DNS or a well-known file), leaves the next list, which reaches users within one trust-data update rather than one browser release.

**Difference from other browsers.** A domain preloaded elsewhere but not in Izcal's list still gets HTTPS-First and, after its first visit, dynamic HSTS; the difference is limited to the first visit's error handling. NW-54 measures the overlap black-box (which domains other browsers refuse to load over plain HTTP on a first visit).

**Cost.** 0.5–1 engineer-year† to build and about 0.5 person† ongoing, in the lists track (Section 9.3), plus the crawler and the form's hosting.

### 6.6 DNS and connection establishment (NW-D8)

**Own stub resolver, OS configuration (NW-D8).** `hg-dns` implements Do53 over UDP and TCP with EDNS(0), DoT ([RFC 7858](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7858)), DoH ([RFC 8484](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8484)) and DoQ ([RFC 9250](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9250)), EDNS padding, ID and port randomization, and SVCB/HTTPS records ([RFC 9460](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9460)). Where queries go comes from the **OS configuration** the broker reads: configured servers, search domains and split-DNS rules (macOS scoped resolvers, Windows NRPT, systemd-resolved per-link servers), so enterprise and VPN names resolve as the OS would. Names the stub cannot answer by protocol (mDNS `.local`, hosts-file entries, OS-only resolution of some VPN clients) go to the OS resolver API in the **OS-adapter process** (Section 6.1), flagged as unencrypted in the decision log. Encrypted DNS: **same-provider upgrade** by default, when the configured resolver is known to offer DoH or DoT or advertises it through Discovery of Designated Resolvers ([RFC 9462](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9462)); a user-chosen DoH resolver with a published policy at least as strict as Mozilla's TRR policy ([PP 5.1][PP]) as an option; enterprise policy and the `use-application-dns.net` canary disable an Izcal-chosen resolver, never a user's explicit choice. Encrypted-DNS connections are held by the container service, keyed (C, R); the partition service sends it queries through a bound endpoint and parses the answers itself. A routed container resolves **through its route** (SOCKS remote resolution, the WireGuard peer's resolver, the Tor route's resolver), never the host resolver ([PP 8.3][PP]).

**Queries.** For an HTTPS origin Izcal asks A, AAAA and HTTPS in parallel; for an `http:` URL, an HTTPS record with compatible parameters upgrades the request as RFC 9460 §9.5 describes, in one place with HSTS, HTTPS-First and mixed-content upgrades, in an order Izcal writes down from black-box observations and tests, because no specification defines it (Q-NW-8). The DNS cache, in-flight de-duplication, HTTPS records and ECH configs are keyed by the connection key (Section 6.8); TTLs are honoured and capped at 1 h†. NW-46 measures the query-count cost of keying by frame site.

**Happy Eyeballs v3** ([draft-ietf-happy-happyeyeballs-v3-04](https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-ietf-happy-happyeyeballs-v3-04.html), pinned). One establishment state machine in `hg-net-connect` handles: Resolution Delay 50 ms, Connection Attempt Delay 250 ms (bounded 100 ms–2 s, the draft's recommended range; its hard floor is 10 ms), QUIC preferred over TCP and IPv6 over IPv4, endpoints grouped by ALPN and ECH, waiting for the HTTPS record when ECH is wanted, NAT64 synthesis with prefix discovery ([RFC 7050](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7050)) for IPv4 literals on IPv6-only networks. Every timer is a substrate timer, so racing is exact under the virtual clock. The winner of a race is a recorded decision (Section 6.16). **Broken HTTP/3** is remembered per (partition, network) with exponential backoff from 5 min† (long enough that one page load's many connections do not each repay a failed QUIC attempt, short enough that a transient middlebox fault costs minutes, well inside Alt-Svc's 24 h default freshness, [RFC 7838 §3.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7838#section-3.1); other browsers' schedules are observed black-box at NW-M3, Q-NW-12, [PT 4.4][PT]), and forgotten on network change.

**Pools and coalescing.** A connection is identified by Fetch's key (network partition key, origin, credentials) extended by Izcal's key fields ([Fetch §2.6](https://fetch.spec.whatwg.org/#connections)). HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 connections are **coalesced only within one connection key**, when the certificate covers the new origin and the new origin's resolved addresses include the connection's peer address ([RFC 9110 §4.3.3](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#section-4.3.3)). §4.3.3 lets an ORIGIN frame remove the address check; Izcal deliberately honours ORIGIN only as a restriction, never as a licence to skip the check, because a coalesced connection to an address the user's resolver did not return is a route and partition surprise. Alt-Svc ([RFC 7838](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7838)) and broken-alternative state are partitioned. Each pool and reuse decision carries a reason code for the decision log.

### 6.7 Fetch in the network service

**Where each part of Fetch runs.** The rule: a step a compromised renderer could skip or forge runs in the network service. The renderer constructs the request from script and documents (Request, Headers, body streams, integrity metadata), runs CSP's pre-request checks (including `<meta>` policies, which only the renderer sees), and exposes the response to script. The partition service runs **main fetch** from the point the request leaves the client: scheme fetch for `http`/`https`, upgrade to HTTPS (HSTS, HTTPS records, HTTPS-First, mixed-content autoupgrade), **bad ports**, **Local Network Access** (with the broker checking the ticket and the connected address), **CORS** including preflight and the preflight cache, **CORP**, **COEP** including `credentialless` (credential stripping happens here), **ORB** before any byte is delivered, **redirects** with every check re-run per hop, credentials stripped on cross-origin redirects and at most 20 hops, **cookies**, the **HTTP cache**, `Sec-Fetch-*` metadata, Referrer Policy, `Origin`, GPC (`Sec-GPC: 1`), the Fetch-defined forbidden methods and request headers, and **content blocking** (Section 6.15). File URLs are never fetched by a network service ([SR 7, Implications][SR]). Each function carries its `// spec:` anchor into the pinned `fetch.bs` (commit `59afe34` at writing, [PR 7.1][PR]). **Speculative-parser fetches** ([ST 6.4][ST]) are ordinary subresource fetches through the frame's `FetchEndpoint`, marked speculative, at the priority the loader assigns, under every check of this section; their fate (used, reused by the real parser, wasted) is a decision-log entry ([P22][RN]).

**CSP's network-side check.** The partition service parses the `Content-Security-Policy` headers of the document responses it delivered (with the one header parser) and enforces their `connect-src`, `script-src`, `frame-src` and related fetch directives for that document's requests, as defence in depth against a compromised renderer of the same site. `<meta>` policies are enforced by the renderer only, and the network-side check cannot cover them; this limit is stated in the renderer's threat model. The broker never parses CSP (IPC-R15). The style spec states the same split for speculative and style-initiated requests ([ST 6.4][ST], [P16][RN]).

**ORB.** ORB is still not in the Fetch standard; Izcal anchors to the standalone ORB draft at a pinned commit and treats it as a spec risk ([PP 2.3][PP]). A blocked response becomes a **network error**, indistinguishable to the page from any other, which is ORB's fail-closed form and CS-ER-13's rule. ORB's **subsequent-range marker** is set only by the partition service, which owns the response's byte stream; no renderer can set or clear it (media spec R13).

**Opaque bodies go to the media process.** When an opaque response body is destined for the media process — an image or media response whose bytes the renderer may not read — the partition service delivers it directly to the media process on a broker-bound endpoint; the renderer receives only a handle and the typed header (G56; media spec MD-D5). The renderer never holds the encoded bytes.

**One throughput estimate per partition.** The partition service keeps the recorded throughput estimate for its (T, C) that media's `HAVE_ENOUGH_DATA` reads (media spec MD-D12); a browser-wide estimate would be a cross-site channel.

**HTTPS-First.** Public sites load over HTTPS first, with an interstitial before plain HTTP. The reason is first-principles: plain HTTP exposes every request to on-path observers and modification (P1), and the measured cost is small: in Chrome's experiment warnings appeared on well under 3% of navigations, and Google announced the same default for public sites in Chrome 154 ([Chrome Security, October 2025](https://security.googleblog.com/2025/10/https-by-default.html), for October 2026; Chrome 154 reached stable on 22 September 2026, and whether the default shipped there was not verified), which is evidence that the scope is deployable. Private addresses and single-label hosts are excluded ([PP 6.2][PP]). Izcal's HSTS preload list (Section 6.5.3) comes through the trust-data channel; dynamic HSTS is partitioned and may be set only for the loaded host or its registrable domain ([PP 1.19][PP]).

**Service workers.** A request from a controlled client goes to its partition service, which owns service-worker routing: it looks up the registration in the client's storage key and, when a fetch event must fire, dispatches it to the worker's renderer through a broker-granted endpoint; the worker's response returns through the partition service, which applies the requester's CORS, CORP and ORB rules before delivery. A service worker's own fetches carry the worker's partition key, as Fetch derives it from the worker's environment ([PP 7, Implications][PP]).

**Typed failure reasons.** Every network error carries, inside Izcal and never to the page, a `FailureReason`: the layer (policy, DNS, connect, TLS, PKI, HTTP framing, CORS, ORB, CORP, LNA, mixed content, content blocking, **reputation**, proxy, route, budget, cancelled), the Fetch step anchor, and layer data (the DNS rcode, the TLS alert, the CORS header that failed). **URL reputation is a per-hop check ordered before socket creation**, and its `reputation` layer carries the structured payload (list, version, entry, canonical URL, check time); the shell and DevTools render it, and the page sees only the generic failure (phishing spec PH-D4, PH-D5). It crosses IPC to the shell and to DevTools sessions with read capability on that origin; to the page it is one `TypeError` (CS-T11 tests byte-identical page-visible errors). The shell turns it into a plain-language "why this did not load" page, including the route and resolver in use and what the user can do (Section 6.18). This is [GO 9][GO]'s first opportunity.

### 6.8 Partitioning (NW-D7, NW-D18)

**The key.** Notation from [PP][PP]: T top-level site (schemeful), C container, F frame site, X cross-site-ancestor bit, N cross-site-main-frame-navigation bit, R route identity, n per-navigation nonce (credentialless iframes, fenced frames, recordings), O origin, Cr credentials flag. Every row keyed by T lives in the partition service of (T, C).

| State | Key |
|---|---|
| HTTP cache (memory and disk), dictionaries, Early-Hints preloads | (T, C, F, X, N, n) + URL |
| Connections (H1 sockets, H2 and H3 sessions, WebSocket and WebTransport over them), coalescing eligibility | (T, C, F, X, R, n) + O + Cr |
| DNS cache, in-flight queries, HTTPS records, ECH configs | (T, C, F, X, R, n) |
| DoH, DoT and DoQ connections to the resolver | (C, R), in the container service |
| Alt-Svc, broken-alternative state, HTTP/2-support state, QUIC address tokens | (T, C, F, X, R, n) |
| TLS tickets, QUIC resumption and 0-RTT state | (T, C, F, X, R, n), single-use, ≤ 24 h†, cleared with the partition's cookies |
| Dynamic HSTS | (T, C), host or registrable-domain scope only |
| HSTS preload, root store, CT log list, revocation filters, Public Suffix List | Global, static, read-only data |
| Fetched intermediates (AIA), stapled responses | (T, C, R) |
| Client-certificate selection | (T, C) for the remembered choice; signing per Section 6.1 |
| HTTP authentication cache and credentials entered in a prompt | (T, C, F, X) |
| CORS-preflight cache | (T, C, F, X) + O |
| Cookies | First-party jar per site in the container service, read by T's service only for site T and for Storage-Access-granted sites; partitioned third-party cookies (CHIPS and every third-party cookie by default) keyed (T, C, X), X matching CHIPS's cross-site-ancestor bit; Storage Access grants keyed (T, C, embedded site) |
| Service-worker registrations, Cache Storage | Storage key (T, C, X) + O, as the storage partitioning work defines |
| Reporting endpoints and queued reports | (T, C, F, X); delivered over a connection of the same key |
| Speculative connections, prefetch, prerender | The key of the navigation that would use them |
| WebRTC ICE candidates, TURN allocations, device IDs | (T, C, R) |
| Resource Timing and connection timing exposed to pages | Coarsened per Fetch's "clamp and coarsen connection timing info"; cross-origin details opaque |
| Favicons | Fetched for the shell in the page's (T, C) partition, never a global favicon cache |
| Downloads | The initiator's (T, C, F, X, R) for the request; the file is the user's (Section 6.14) |
| Proxy and PAC evaluation, proxy-host DNS | (C, R) |
| Web Push channel | The system service; per subscription's storage key for routing (Section 6.14) |

**Why F and not only X (NW-D7).** Chrome's published designs key its cache and its network state by (top-level site, frame site) ([PP 1.6, 1.7][PP]); whether shipped Chrome keys connections more coarsely is observed black-box at NW-M2 (Q-NW-12, [PP 1.9][PP]); Firefox and WebKit double-key by top-level site ([PP 1.15][PP]). With only X, two cross-site iframes of different sites under one top-level page share the cache and the pool, so one can probe what the other loaded. P1 outranks the extra misses, which Chrome measured at about 3.6% for its triple key against no partitioning ([PP 1.6][PP]). Izcal keys both the cache and connection-level state by F; the cost is measured on Izcal's corpus at NW-M2 (NW-46), and the reversal trigger in Section 9.4 applies only to connection-level state.

**R in every key.** A route change mid-session must not reuse state learned on the old route, or the user's real address would be linked to the proxied identity ([PP 8, Open risk 8][PP]). Since the route is bound to the container's services (NW-D4), a route change restarts the container's services with fresh state.

**Renderer caches use the same key.** This spec owns one partition key type, fixed at NW-M0. A renderer's in-process caches (parsed style sheets, attribute sets, decoded images, parsed fonts, shaped text, raster entries) and its glyph atlas key by the projection **(T, C, X, n)**: F is fixed by the renderer's site lock (origin under FP-D8), and N concerns only navigation requests. A renderer cache coarser than this key would re-create by timing the leak the network key closes, for example a credentialless iframe seeing what other documents of its site decoded ([P18][RN]). P18's projection assumes F fixed per process, which holds because content GPU processes are keyed like renderers (GR-D2, G68).

**Limits are a bounded channel, not a closed one.** Separate services per top-level site remove the shared pool between top-level sites altogether: an attacker page and a victim page under different top-level sites share no socket pool, stream limit or receive buffer. What remains shared is **the broker's mint queue** (served fair-queued per service), the OS's descriptor and ephemeral-port space (large, and per-process descriptor limits are per service), and CPU (inherent). Inside one service, budgets apply **per top-level page first and per partition inside it**, so a page cannot multiply its share by embedding many cross-site or credentialless frames; the number of live nonce partitions per top-level document is capped at 16†; each service's total capacity is randomized per session within ±25%† of its nominal value. The residual is recorded as **bounded** in footprint 6.18.1 (Section 9.5), and NW-20 tests it statistically. The connection-pool XS-Leak, open in every engine ([PP 1.12, 1.24][PP]), is closed across top-level sites by placement and bounded within one.

**Shared queues inside a service are scheduled per partition.** The receive buffer, the decryption queue and the connection-group sequences of one service serve its partitions round-robin, so one partition's traffic delays another's only within the bound above; they are recorded as bounded channels too.

**Clear-Site-Data under partitioning (NW-D18).** A site's `Clear-Site-Data` clears, **within the current container only**, its own state in every partition where it is the top-level site, plus the state it holds as a third party in the current partition. It never touches another container, because a site-controlled side effect across containers would let the site link the user's identities. The specifications are silent ([PP 7.4][PP]); NW-19's suite includes the cross-container case.

### 6.9 Cookies (NW-D10, NW-D16)

**Store.** `hg-cookies` implements parsing and storage from [6265bis-22](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-httpbis-rfc6265bis/) (pinned; not yet an RFC) and the same-site computation from Fetch §3.1.3, which now hosts it ([PP 1.5, 3.1][PP]); prefixes `__Secure-`, `__Host-`; limits from 6265bis (4,096 octets name plus value, 400-day cap, at least 50 per domain and 3,000 total). First-party jars live in the container service, partitioned jars in the partition services; both persist through the storage service (Izcal's store engine), encrypted under per-container keys that the broker unwraps from the OS key store (SEC-34). The layered-cookies draft that would move user-agent rules into HTML and Fetch is watched (Q-NW-7).

**Third-party cookies.** Partitioned by (T, C, X) by default (Stage 1 Section 6), which matches Firefox's Total Cookie Protection and is stricter than Chrome's 2026 default ([PP 3.7][PP]). Unpartitioned access only through the **Storage Access API**, with a user prompt; **no heuristic grants** (NW-D16): Firefox's 30-day opener and redirect heuristics re-link partitions without the user asking ([PP 1.16][PP]). Related Website Sets is dead and not implemented ([PP 3.6][PP]). Bounce tracking uses two rules, both in `hg-nav-protect` (content blocking spec 6.5, CB-D8): a site in an extended navigation that redirected and wrote state without user activation on that site in the last 45 days has its first-party state in the container deleted after a 1 h† grace period; and the 7-day† backstop clears storage of a site the user never interacted with as a top-level site, the WebKit and Firefox class of mitigation ([PP 3.8][PP]). A site holding a Storage Access grant is exempt while the grant lives. Cookies from a cloaked response are capped at a 7-day† lifetime (CB-D9), and cookie writes attributed to a restricted script get the same cap (CB-D10).

**`document.cookie` and the Cookie Store API without sync IPC (NW-D10).** For each (host, partition) a renderer hosts, the partition service maintains a **cookie view region**: the script-visible cookies that the host's own requests in that partition could carry (non-HttpOnly; host-only cookies of that host and domain cookies of its parent domains), with a version counter. Keying by **host**, not site, keeps origin keying (FP-D8) intact: a renderer for `a.example.com` never sees `b.example.com`'s host-only cookies, so the region holds nothing the origin's own requests would not carry. The rules:

- **Handle kind.** A new IPC-R1 handle kind, *producer-writable, consumer-read-only shared memory*: on Linux a memfd sealed with `F_SEAL_FUTURE_WRITE` after the producer maps it; on Windows a section duplicated without `FILE_MAP_WRITE`; on macOS a Mach memory entry with read-only protection (Section 9.5).
- **No double fetch.** The getter copies the region under a sequence lock (retry while the version is odd or changed) into private memory and parses the copy (IPC-R7).
- **Ordering with responses.** Before delivering a response that set cookies, the partition service publishes the new region version, and the response carries the minimum version the renderer must see; `await fetch('/login'); document.cookie` therefore shows the new cookies.
- **Writes.** The setter parses the string with `hg-cookies` (the one parser, linked into renderers and declared tier P), applies it to a local overlay for read-your-writes, and sends it asynchronously; the service's verdict (accepted, rejected by prefix rules or limits) comes back and the overlay entry is dropped when the version moves past it or the write is rejected. A rejected write is visible for at most one task turn; the Cookie Store API's change events fire from the service's verdict, never from the overlay.
- `document.cookie` leaves the sync-IPC allowlist (Stage 1 4.1) once NW-2's WPT cases and this ordering pass (Section 9.5).

### 6.10 The HTTP cache (NW-D11)

The HTTP cache implements [RFC 9111](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9111) plus stale-while-revalidate, `immutable`, Cache Groups ([RFC 9875](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9875)) and Fetch's cache modes, keyed per Section 6.8, with `httpwg/cache-tests` and WPT `fetch/http-cache` as oracles ([PR 1.2][PR]). **Range requests are cached as partial responses** under RFC 9111's rules, so a media seek or a resumed download reuses the stored ranges instead of refetching the object.

**Store (NW-D11).** The HTTP cache is a **member of the regenerable-cache class** (FP-D16): it is never the only copy of user data, so it needs no crash-consistent database. Its format is crash-only: a directory per (T, C) (a broker-granted handle), append-only segment files with self-describing records, and an index that is a **derived** structure rebuilt lazily from segments. Lookups never block on disk: the in-memory index answers "maybe present" and a miss proceeds to the network while the read is in flight. Chrome replaced its blockfile cache because a crash corrupted the whole cache and lookups averaged more than 25 ms ([PT 6.3][PT]).

**FP-D16's compromise rules hold.** The class exists because the writer may be compromised, and "encryption does not help, because the writer is the attacker" (footprint 6.9). So:

- **Security epoch.** Every record carries a **security epoch** that the **broker** issues to the service and rotates whenever that service crashes, fails a check, is killed for a bad message, or the browser is updated with a security fix to any crate in the network service's tier-P set (`hg-net*`, `hg-http*`, `hg-quic`, `hg-tls`, `hg-crypto`, `hg-pki`, `hg-compress`, `hg-http-cache`, `hg-cookies`, `hg-content-block`) or to one of `hg-crypto`'s pinned cryptography crates. Records from an old epoch are discarded, never repaired. A per-epoch MAC key held by the service detects corruption, not compromise.
- **Format and epoch binding.** The store binds to its **format version and security epoch**, not to the engine build: an ordinary update keeps the cache, a security update or a crash drops it. This keeps FP-D16's purpose (no persistence across a fix or a crash) without discarding 256 MiB at every release (Section 9.5).
- **Residual.** A compromised service can write entries for its own (T, C) until its next crash, check failure or security update; since a partition service holds only one top-level site's partitions, that is the blast radius, recorded in the network-service threat model.

**Budget.** 256 MiB† total (FP-26), shown in settings; each (T, C) capped at 10%† of the budget; eviction least-recently-used within the global budget. Eviction across top-level sites is a bounded channel (one site's fill can evict another's entries), recorded in footprint 6.18.1.

**Decoded copies** stay in the renderer's partition-keyed resource store (footprint 6.4); the HTTP cache keeps encoded bytes only.

### 6.11 WebSockets, WebTransport and deferred fetches

**WebSockets** ([RFC 6455](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6455), [RFC 7692](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7692)) over HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2 (RFC 8441) and HTTP/3 (RFC 9220), handshake through Fetch ("obtain a WebSocket connection"), with an accurate `Origin` and SameSite rules applied to the handshake (the browser's part of CSWSH defence, [SR 7.5][SR]); LNA applies from the start, a gap Chrome admitted ([PP 6.1][PP]). Autobahn's 521 client cases are the wire oracle ([PR 7.5][PR]).

**Frozen pages keep their sessions.** A WebSocket or WebTransport session of a frozen page stays open, held by the partition service with no wakeup of its own; incoming messages are buffered up to a budget of 1 MiB† per page and, when a message arrives, it wakes the page only under footprint 6.8's "live exchange" reason; without a declared reason the buffered data waits for resume. Sessions are closed only under memory pressure, through the same path as discard, because closing them changes what the page observes (a `close` event, lost server messages) and would break chat, mail and collaboration apps waiting idle for the server. This is added to FP-17's background compatibility suite (Section 9.5).

**WebTransport (NW-D13).** Over HTTP/3 and HTTP/2, pinned to draft-ietf-webtrans-http3-16 and -http2-15 and the W3C Candidate Recommendation of 30 July 2026 ([PR 4.4][PR]). Built with the HTTP/3 crate and enabled under the gate rule of Section 9.1: the pinned documents are in working-group last call, and the feature is enabled after it passes WPT `webtransport/` and interoperates with at least two independent server implementations; Safari shipped it on 24 March 2026 ([GO 7.1][GO]). LNA applies.

**`fetchLater`, `keepalive`, Background Fetch.** `keepalive` and `fetchLater` requests are owned by the partition service, so they survive the document; they belong to the activity class "a transfer the user started" for wakeups (footprint 6.8). Background Fetch is not implemented (single engine for seven years, [GO 7.8][GO]). **Speculative requests** (preconnect, prefetch, prerender) use the key of the navigation that would consume them and never carry cross-site cookies.

**Network Error Logging (NW-D15)** is not implemented: it reports a user's network failures to a site-chosen endpoint after the fact, and only Chromium ships it. The Reporting API's other report types are implemented, keyed per Section 6.8.

### 6.12 WebRTC transport

The security scope places ICE, DTLS, SRTP and SCTP in the network service, with media in utility processes and no WebRTC parser in the broker (security scope 6.10a), and Stage 1 M7 requires post-quantum key exchange for WebRTC's DTLS. Under M-1 and the owner's direction, a WebRTC transport library is browser networking, and Izcal's TLS stack already has the handshake core DTLS needs, so **Izcal builds all four** (NW-D6, NW-D22):

- **Crates** (tier P, sans-IO like the rest): `hg-ice` (ICE agent, STUN, TURN client, consent freshness, [RFC 8445](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8445), [RFC 8489](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8489), [RFC 8656](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8656), [RFC 7675](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7675)), `hg-dtls` (DTLS 1.2 and 1.3, [RFC 6347](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6347), [RFC 9147](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9147), on `hg-tls`'s handshake core and `hg-crypto`, with X25519MLKEM768), `hg-srtp` (SRTP keyed by DTLS-SRTP, [RFC 3711](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3711), [RFC 5764](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5764)), `hg-sctp` (SCTP over DTLS for data channels, [RFC 8261](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8261), [RFC 8831](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8831)). DTLS reuses `hg-tls`'s handshake core, key schedule and certificate fingerprint checks (Section 6.5); the `RTCCertificate`'s ECDSA P-256 key signs through `hg-crypto`'s constant-time code.
- **Placement.** WebRTC transport runs in the partition service of the page's top-level site; decoded media never enters it.
- **Typed media endpoints.** Decrypted RTP and RTCP leave `hg-srtp` as typed media packets on one broker-bound endpoint to the media process; the renderer sees neither the packets nor the endpoint (media spec 6.10, R13).
- **Sockets.** ICE needs bound, unconnected UDP sockets, which can `sendto` any address, so a per-address check at mint time would enforce nothing afterwards. The broker therefore binds WebRTC sockets **to specific interfaces**: a frame without an LNA ticket gets sockets bound only to the default-route interface, its host candidates are exposed only as mDNS names (security scope 6.10a), and the network service's ICE agent refuses to pair with private or loopback remote candidates without a ticket; a frame with a ticket covering local addresses gets the local interfaces. TURN relay sockets are connected sockets minted with the ordinary checks. Under a non-direct route, only relay candidates through the route exist (NW-21).
- **Partition.** ICE candidates, TURN allocations and device IDs keyed (T, C, R) (Section 6.8).
- **Recording.** DTLS application data after decryption follows NW-D2's boundary; SRTP media payloads are **never recorded** by default (they are media plaintext), only their sizes and timing.
- **Wakeups.** Consent-freshness checks run every few seconds by RFC 7675's design; they run only while the session has live tracks or an open data channel, which is footprint 6.8's "live exchange" declared reason, and are aligned to the wake grid within the RFC's tolerance.
- **Targets.** ICE and DTLS interop with at least two independent stacks, SEC-30's fuzzers, no host or local candidates without a grant, and the route-leak test (NW-21, NW-42, NW-43).

### 6.13 Proxies, routes, captive portals and Izcal's own traffic

**Proxies.** Per-container configuration: direct, the OS settings (brokered), PAC, HTTP and HTTPS proxies with CONNECT ([RFC 9110 §9.3.6](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#section-9.3.6)), SOCKS5 with remote resolution ([RFC 1928](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1928)), and MASQUE CONNECT-UDP ([RFC 9298](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9298)) so HTTP/3 can pass through a proxy that offers it; otherwise a proxy forces TCP. **PAC** is JavaScript, and IPC-R10 forbids an interpreter in a network service, so PAC runs in a **proxy-resolver utility process** with Izcal's jitless engine and no DOM, one per (container, route), at the strictest sandbox tier; its `dnsResolve` and `myIpAddress` calls go to the container's services as typed requests; HTTPS URLs reach `FindProxyForURL` with path and query stripped ([PR 8.1][PR]); its results go to the broker as well (Section 6.1). **WPAD** discovery runs only when the OS is set to auto-detect **and** the OS does not mark the network as public; the PAC source is shown in the site panel, because a PAC script keeps state across calls and sees every `http:` URL and HTTPS host of the container ([PR 8.2][PR]). Because PAC has no specification, Izcal writes its own PAC behaviour page from the Netscape text and black-box observations (M-1 part 2) before implementing it.

**Routes.** A container route is enforced by the broker's socket policy (Section 6.1). **WireGuard** runs in a **route process** per routed container, Izcal's own implementation of the protocol; its primitives (X25519, ChaCha20-Poly1305, BLAKE2s, HKDF over BLAKE2s) come from `hg-crypto`: Izcal's own X25519 and the `chacha20poly1305` and `blake2` crates (Section 6.5; Q-NW-11 answered); the network services receive socket-pair handles into it, so their traffic has no other path out. **SOCKS** routes are spoken by the network service over sockets that can only reach the proxy. **Tor** routes are SOCKS to a Tor client the user runs (Arti or tor), with per-top-level-site isolation through SOCKS credentials, as Tor Browser does ([PP 8.1][PP]); Izcal does not embed a Tor client (NW-D6), and keeps Stage 1's label: "hides your IP address, not your browser". Under a non-direct route, WebRTC is relay-only through the route, and every request a container's services make (AIA fetches, favicons, speculative connections, DNS) uses the route.

**Captive portals.** Izcal uses the OS's captive-portal state (brokered) and RFC 8908 and RFC 8910 where the network offers them, and infers a portal from TLS failures to many hosts on a new network with HTTPS-First; it runs **no probe endpoint of its own** by default. A detected portal opens in a credential-free, ephemeral container.

**Izcal's own traffic (NW-D17).** Only the system service talks to Izcal's servers. It follows the **OS proxy configuration** (and enterprise policy), never a container's route, because it is not acting for any container. Its endpoints are a published list: updates; the trust-data channel (Izcal's root store, the CT log list, revocation data, Izcal's HSTS preload list); Izcal's own content-blocking lists (Section 6.15), one bundle published as a TUF target with a witnessed transparency log and verified by the broker's update client (content blocking spec 6.9, CB-D17); the **phishing protection manifest, diff and bundle** (public, plain fetch, the same 15-minute† manifest poll and daily full sync for every client; no per-user query; a prefix-lookup mode is not Stage 1, phishing spec PH-D1, PH-D12); **model files as update-channel payloads** (a new payload kind on this list, not a new endpoint kind, verified by a second signing chain before the ML loader sees them; ML service spec ML-D7, ML-D12, NW-36); the security classifiers HGBA-3 B10 allows, if enabled; and, from Stage 2, the Web Push service for subscriptions the user granted (Section 6.14). **Model downloads are user-requested data**, listed and removable, never page-triggered and not web-observable; no per-user log exists. Requests that carry user-specific data — the content-blocking breakage report among them (CB-D18) — go through an **Oblivious HTTP** ([RFC 9458](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9458)) relay run by an independent operator (Q-NW-6); downloads of public files (Izcal's lists, trust data, update payloads) are plain cacheable fetches that reveal nothing per user. **User-added list URLs are not on this list:** a user-added list belongs to a container, so that container's network service fetches it through the container's route (content blocking spec 6.9). AI off means zero AI requests (AG-0). NW-36 checks the list.

### 6.14 Downloads, HTTP authentication, client certificates, Web Push and enterprise policy

- **Downloads.** The request runs in the initiator's partition with its credentials mode; the bytes are written by the partition service into a **broker-granted writable file** (IPC-R1, security scope SH-R9) in the download directory; Mark-of-the-Web and quarantine attributes are applied by the broker at completion (SH-R6). Resumption uses Range requests with a strong validator ([RFC 9110 §14](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110#section-14)); a download survives its tab, keeps its partition service alive, and, if that service restarts, resumes from the last validated offset. A failed download shows its `FailureReason`. **Every download URL is checked against the phishing bundle before the network service connects, and every redirect target before the next request is made**; a malware match writes nothing, and the download record carries the verdict and check time (phishing spec PH-D7).
- **HTTP authentication.** Basic and Digest challenges are parsed in the partition service and shown by the prompt renderer through the consent path (security scope CON-R2), with the requesting origin and the top-level site; prompts for **cross-origin subresources are refused** (a phishing vector), only navigations and same-origin subresources prompt. Proxy 407 challenges prompt per container. Entered credentials stay in the (T, C, F, X) authentication cache for the session, and in the password manager only if the user saves them. Negotiate and NTLM follow NW-D20's allowlist (Section 6.1).
- **Client certificates.** A `CertificateRequest` pauses the handshake; the broker shows the selection through CON-R1, remembers the choice per (T, C, host) if the user asks, and issues the per-handshake signing grant (Section 6.1). Enterprise policy can auto-select per host pattern. The selection is a recorded decision under NW-D2.
- **Web Push** (Stage 2, Stage 1 13.2). The push client is a long-lived connection in the **system service**, opened only while at least one subscription the user granted exists. It is the one exception to NW-33, NW-34 and NW-36, tied to that grant: one connection, server-initiated traffic, no client keepalive beyond what the push protocol requires on the wake grid, and messages routed to the subscription's storage key.
- **Enterprise policy surface.** Policies, applied by the broker per container: DoH mode and resolver; QUIC on or off; ECH on or off; proxy configuration and lock; ambient-authentication allowlist (NW-D20); LNA defaults; added trust anchors and client-certificate auto-selection; HTTPS-First exceptions; content-blocking list set; Web Push allowed. Each policy is visible on `izcal://policy`.

### 6.15 Content blocking

HGBA-2 2.3.0 drops the `adblock` crate, footprint 6.11 specifies the matcher's footprint and compilation, and the coding standard lists `hg-content-block` as tier P; this spec owns the **request-matching semantics**, the matcher, the cosmetic-rule compiler and, since 0.3.2, **Izcal's own lists** (NW-D26): the de facto default lists, EasyList and EasyPrivacy, are GPL-3.0 or CC BY-SA 3.0 ([LA 2.5][LA]), and HGBA-2 2.3.2 lets Izcal ship only lists it builds. Which lists are on in which container, and the settings UI, stay with the shell (footprint 6.11).

- **Rule syntax.** Izcal writes a rule-syntax page for the network-request rules it supports, from the public documentation of the common list formats, recorded as black-box observations where documentation is silent (M-1 part 2): URL patterns and anchors, `$third-party`, `$domain`, resource-type options, exceptions, `$important`, `$redirect` to Izcal's built-in neutral resources, and `$removeparam`. DNR JSON rulesets and Safari content-blocker JSON are imported for user-added lists, from their published documentation (CB-D14). Regex rules are a linear-time subset only: no backreferences or lookaround, bounded repetition, each automaton ≤ 4 KiB†, and anything else is rejected with its reason (CB-D11). Cosmetic rules are compiled separately for renderers (footprint 6.11).
- **Matching semantics (content blocking spec 6.2).** Every request is matched over one typed request context (URL, resource type, method, partition key, initiator, CNAME chain and resolved addresses, navigation flags, service-worker route). The default lists block third-party requests, and first-party requests only for uncloaked trackers, evidenced first-party request shapes and security entries (CB-D6). Exceptions are scoped by trust domain, and one total order decides ties in phases A–C; `restrict` and `permissions` are typed actions, `csp=` adds a policy from an allow-list without reporting directives or host sources, and popups are matched as document requests before the traversable is created (CB-D11). CNAME matching covers every hop, and resolved addresses match reviewed IP-range entries (CB-D9).
- **Partition semantics.** "Third-party" means the request's site differs from the **initiating frame's site** (F); `$domain` matches against both the top-level site (T) and the frame site, as the list formats document; the decision log records which rule matched. The default list set is the same in every named container (CB-D13); users may add lists per container (Stage 1 Section 6).
- **The phishing list is separate (registered 21 October 2026).** The phishing protection spec's URL-reputation list is **Izcal's own list, separate from the content-blocking lists**: it is not user-addable, not a filter-list format, and no user or extension allow entry can override it (phishing spec PH-D2, PH-D8). The two lists never share a compiled file or a match; on overlap the phishing verdict is the navigation check and the Security list is the request check (phishing spec PH-D2, PH-D3).
- **Izcal's lists (NW-D26).** Content blocking serves P1, so the default lists are defined by what a request does, not by taste. The **tracking list** names third-party hosts and paths whose measured behaviour joins the user across sites: identifier-like values stored or read in a third-party context, identifier-like URL parameters that recur across sites, and fingerprinting API use attributed to their scripts; known malware and cryptomining endpoints join it. The **ads list** names ad-serving, auction and ad-measurement endpoints as the crawl observes them. The bundle's lists are Security, Tracking, Ads, Parameters, Redirectors, Cosmetic, Restrict, Exceptions and Anti-fraud, as the content-blocking spec's 6.8 defines them: malware and mining endpoints are in Security, and bot-detection and anti-fraud scripts are published but not blocked (CB-D13). **Cosmetic rules** are mostly generic (hide an element whose requests were blocked, which needs no per-site rule), with hand-written per-site rules only where the corpus shows a broken layout; hiding is paint-only suppression, not layout collapse, so a blocked element stays indistinguishable from a failed load (NW-22).
- **How entries are made.** A lab crawler, which is Izcal itself in recording mode, loads the corpus and a CT-derived sample of sites per region; the decision log already records each request's initiator, partition, cookies and the script behind each API call (Section 6.18). A classifier labels hosts by behaviour across sites; a person reviews every entry before publication; each entry is published with its evidence. CNAME cloaking is answered by `hg-dns`, which sees the CNAME chain, so a first-party name that aliases a listed tracker matches by its canonical name.
- **Breakage and evasion.** A breakage report in the site panel sends, with the user's consent, the scheme and host by default, a previewed and redacted path only if the user includes it, the bundle version, an enumerated settings vector, the entries that fired and the date — never the query or fragment — optionally with a redacted recording started by reloading the page in recording mode; reports go through OHTTP or NW-D17's accepted fallback and are kept ≤ 30 days† (CB-D18). The crawl compares each page with and without blocking; reports are triaged within 7 days† and exceptions are published (NW-54); NW-54's breakage counts interaction-triggered and cosmetic breakage, and its denominator excludes requests matched by Anti-fraud and Restrict entries, reported separately (CB-D18). Path rotation and first-party proxying are answered by behaviour classification where names fail; this is ongoing work, staffed as such.
- **Users' own lists.** A user may add a third-party list by URL, per container. Izcal fetches it only then, compiles it in the same utility process as downloaded third-party text (footprint 6.11), and neither ships nor pre-selects any third-party list. Third-party lists may be used in the lab to measure Izcal's coverage (test-only, OD-L2), never as a source of entries.
- **Command-text patterns (ED-D11).** The lists track also maintains the command-text pattern list that the editing spec's clipboard warning matches (shell invocations such as `powershell`, `pwsh -enc`, `cmd /c`, `mshta`, `curl … | sh`), authored from published prose reports and shipped through the blocking lists' release process. It is not a blocking list and never shares a compiled file with them.
- **Cost.** 3–6 people† ongoing in the lists track (Section 9.3), plus crawler infrastructure; a useful first tracking list about 1 year† after the track starts, with the ads list growing after it.
- **Placement and cost.** Lists are compiled in a utility process into a sealed automaton file that every partition service maps read-only (clean, uncharged pages); each service's matcher state is ≤ 256 KiB† charged; matching allocates nothing per request and meets FP-28's p99 ≤ 20 µs†. The same utility job compiles **cosmetic rules** into per-site CSS subsets that renderers apply as user-origin sheets (the style spec's Section 6.16; ≤ 256 KiB† per site process, FP 6.11) ([P21][RN]). A blocked request is a network error with `FailureReason::ContentBlocked`, never page-distinguishable from other errors (NW-22).

### 6.16 Determinism and recording (NW-D2)

**Simulation.** Under `hg-substrate-sim`, `hg-netsim` replaces everything below `NetTransport`: simulated links (bandwidth, delay, loss, reordering, duplication, MTU, UDP blocked, NAT rebinding, IPv6-only with NAT64), simulated DNS servers, and **simulated peers built from Izcal's own protocol crates in the server role** plus the adversarial servers of `hg-net-oracle` (Section 6.19). Entropy, including `Entropy::crypto()`, is seeded there, so the whole stack (TLS handshakes included) is a function of the seed. This is what the DST farm runs and what DevTools' what-if runs use (DT-D5). Because simulated peers share Izcal's reading of the RFCs, the performance targets that matter are also run in the lab against real stacks (NW-28, NW-29).

**The recorded unit and start state.** A recording of a tab runs that tab's top-level site in a **dedicated, freshly launched partition service** with a recording nonce in its key (n), so the recording starts from a known empty state, uses fresh connections, and holds no other tab's traffic, as DT-D2 does for renderers by starting at process launch. The renderer records its own side, each data-pipe read as an IPC input (Stage 1 3.4), and the two recordings are joined by request ID ([P20][RN]). Reads from state that outlives the recording (the first-party cookie jar in the container service, HSTS, Alt-Svc, the HTTP cache, the authentication cache, Storage Access grants) are recorded as **the values read**, so replay needs no snapshot of those stores; writes to them are re-executed in replay and compared.

**The recording boundary: above decryption.** A production recording logs, per connection:

- **TCP connections:** TLS application-data records after decryption, each with its canonical receive time; the handshake outcome (version, ALPN, certificate chain, verification result and data versions, ECH acceptance).
- **QUIC, per datagram:** arrival time, ECN codepoint and segment-size metadata, and for each packet in the datagram its unprotected header fields (packet type, packet number, key phase, spin bit, DCID) and either its decrypted payload or a **rejected** outcome with its reason (no keys, AEAD failure, stateless-reset match).
- **Values drawn from `Entropy::crypto()`** for the wire (connection IDs, stateless-reset tokens, PATH_CHALLENGE data, the initial DCID, tokens), as the values drawn, never a seed; stateless-reset tokens are a sensitive class.
- **Timer firings and decision points**: race winners, pool and coalescing choices, cache lookups and evictions, DNS answers and their timing, OS network-change events, client-certificate selections, as JS-D15 does for the engine.
- **Outgoing plaintext** as a hash per send.

**No key, traffic secret or key-derivation input is ever recorded.** Recordings are encrypted at rest under SEC-34's key (security scope REC-R2) because they hold plaintext and sensitive tokens; they are off by default and user-initiated (Stage 1 3.4), with credential placeholders and redaction as the DevTools spec requires.

**Replay.** `hg-net-replay` (tier P) replaces the TLS handshake and packet protection with the recorded outcomes and feeds the recorded inputs into the same state machines; HTTP/2 and HTTP/3 framing, QPACK, QUIC streams, loss recovery, congestion control, the cache logic, cookies and Fetch re-execute. Each outgoing send's plaintext hash is compared, so a divergence is detected at the first differing byte. Network replay runs in the replay host (`hg-replay-main`, which links `hg-net-replay`, not `hg-netsim`) as a **per-(T, C) replay process at the network service's tier**, with no network access; it holds the recorded page's partitions, exactly what that page's partition service held, so it needs no new P1 exception.

**Why this boundary.** Recording ciphertext with the crypto entropy would let a recording decrypt everything it captured and would need a seeded `Entropy::crypto()` in a shipping binary, which Q-CS-1 forbids. Recording only Fetch responses would leave every protocol layer out of replay, where most network bugs live. The decryption boundary is the lowest layer that holds no secret.

**Deterministic by construction.** `hg-net` reads no clock but the substrate's, iterates no hash map in a way that affects output, and orders sequences only through the scheduler. Cache freshness, cookie expiry and HSTS lifetimes use the substrate's wall clock, a recorded input. NW-38 checks byte-identical qlog and decision logs across 1,000 simulated loads; NW-37 checks replay against network checkpoints.

### 6.17 Footprint, wakeups and energy

**Memory, itemized by service kind** (charged memory as footprint 6.2 defines it; sealed shared files are clean file-backed pages and not charged):

| Service kind | Idle charged budget† | Lines |
|---|---|---|
| System service | ≤ 4 MiB | Runtime and allocator 1.5, connection state for its few connections 1, receive buffer 0.5, trust-data and update client 1 |
| Container service | ≤ 2 MiB | Runtime 1, first-party cookie jars in memory 0.5, encrypted-DNS connections 0.5 |
| Partition service | ≤ 2 MiB | Runtime 1, matcher state ≤ 0.25, receive buffer 0.25 (grows under load and shrinks after), per-partition state created on first use ≤ 0.5 |
| Partition service, settled after a corpus page load | ≤ 3 MiB | As above, plus live connection state for the page's origins, DNS and Alt-Svc entries, the cache index and the cookie partition, at the footprint spec's settle point |
| Partition service whose documents are all frozen | ≤ 1.25 MiB | Runtime and matcher state; per-partition state and the receive buffer are dropped at freeze |
| Spare network service | ≤ 1.5 MiB | Runtime only, bound to no (T, C) |
| OS-adapter process (per container, on demand) | ≤ 2 MiB idle; exits after 10 s idle | Starts only for an OS resolver fallback, ambient authentication or client-key signing |
| Proxy-resolver utility (per container and route, PAC only) | ≤ 4 MiB | A jitless engine with no DOM, within FP-2's empty-process figure |
| Route process (per routed container) | ≤ 2 MiB idle | WireGuard state and buffers |

With no tabs, the system service and one spare run (≤ 5.5 MiB†), inside FP-1's 8 MiB† network line; the default container's service (≤ 2 MiB†) starts with the first navigation and also fits in that line. Each open top-level site adds one partition service; each further active container adds one container service. The amendments to FP-1, the footprint model and footprint 8.2 are in Section 9.5.

**Against the footprint scenarios** ([RN] Section 3). At FP-S30's settle point, 30 partition services at ≤ 3 MiB† add about 90 MiB to the 8 MiB line, leaving about 166 MiB of FP-S30's 1.25 GiB† for what the model had about 256 MiB of headroom for (the three iframe-heavy pages' extra processes); FP-S30 holds. After 10 minutes idle, 29 frozen services at ≤ 1.25 MiB† and one visible service add about 39 MiB, and FP-6 holds at about 483 MiB of 768. The duplicated runtime (about 1.25 MiB† per service) is isolation overhead: about 2% of the browser for one page and 3.4% at FP-S30; if FP-7's 5%† counted it, renderer isolation would keep only 1.6–3%, so Section 9.5 asks the footprint owner to report it separately (owner decision OD-2). FP-28's single 8 MiB† matcher becomes 256 KiB† per partition service, which passes 8 MiB at 33 open top-level sites. Receive buffers are per service, not per connection ([PT 7.6][PT]). Partition state with no client is dropped lazily, on its next use or when the service next wakes for other work, never by a timer of its own.

**No keepalives for idle connections.** Izcal sends no HTTP/2 PING, QUIC PING or TCP keepalive to keep an idle connection open; idle connections close (QUIC 30 s†, HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2 60 s†), and their close timers are on the global wake grid (footprint 6.8). A liveness PING is allowed only together with a request (Section 6.3). WebSocket and WebTransport sessions of frozen pages stay open without wakeups (Section 6.11).

**No periodic work of Izcal's own.** Trust-data, filter-list and update checks run on a daily† schedule aligned to the wake grid and are deferred while on battery saver, never polled.

**Attribution.** Bytes, connection setups and CPU per (site, frame, initiator) go to the footprint ledger (footprint 6.13); a partition service's CPU is charged to its (T, C).

**Telemetry.** None by default (FP-D9). Network statistics are local and shown on request.

### 6.18 Observability and user experience

**DevTools** (`hg-net-observe`, DT-D5). The network services emit: **qlog** (main schema -14, QUIC and HTTP/3 events -13, pinned) natively from `hg-quic` and `hg-http3`; Izcal's **decision log** (partition key, cache verdict, reuse or coalescing reason, Alt-Svc state, race outcome, service-worker route, dictionary use, content-blocking rule, speculation fate, `FailureReason`) as a published schema that shares qlog's connection and stream identifiers ([GO 2.8][GO]); TLS and PKI facts (version, group, ALPN, chain, SCTs, revocation source and data age, ECH outcome). HAR 1.2 with `_izcal` fields is an export only. The RFC 9850 key log exists only per session in a non-default container behind an explicit, logged act (DT-D5). DT-18 checks that views equal what the stack did.

**Protocol-level shaping per partition.** Because a partition service owns the transport of one (T, C), DevTools can apply a **live link model** (bandwidth, delay, loss, reordering) and protocol faults (block UDP, fail DNS, drop the ECH config, reset a stream) to one partition, labelled in every view, with write capability on that origin (DT-21). The DevTools spec allows protocol faults only in simulation; Section 9.5 proposes allowing the live link model for a granted partition, because it changes timing only for that partition and the user who asked.

**For people browsing.** The "why this did not load" page from `FailureReason` (Section 6.7) gains a **reputation case** with the entry, source, date and the contest action (phishing spec PH-D6, PH-D15); the site panel shows the protocol, whether ECH and encrypted DNS were used, the route, locally trusted or OS-distrusted roots and stale trust data, the **phishing list's age and the last check** (phishing spec PH-D6), and the trust-store disclosure while OS anchors are in use (`about:build` records the mode; shell 6.6); a per-site, per-container **"who received what" view** lists which parties received requests, cookies (names, partitioned or not), referrer origins and which of the user's addresses (route or direct), built from the decision log, held locally in the storage service under the footprint spec's attribution-history rules (FP-D18: 24 h† detail, cleared with history, memory-only in private windows), never synced, exported or sent ([GO 5, 9][GO]). Certificate and other interstitials follow the shell's warning design (UI-9). **Error pages are shell documents**: the broker supplies the typed `FailureReason` and broker-formatted codes, no page-supplied error text is rendered, and the shell's `about:diagnostics` records the mode (NW-D27; shell 9.5).

### 6.19 Security, privacy and consent

- **Two attackers** ([SR, Method notes][SR]): the network (hostile servers, on-path attackers, middleboxes) and a compromised renderer (crafted requests). Every IPC message is parsed by the generated, Kani-checked parser; request context comes from the endpoint (NET-R1); a renderer can request only what Fetch lets its document request.
- **Blast radius.** A compromise of a partition service exposes that (T, C)'s partitions, site T's first-party cookies and the jars of sites holding Storage Access under T, and its cache until the next security epoch; it cannot open sockets the broker would refuse, cannot use another top-level site's LNA grants, cannot obtain NTLM or Kerberos responses for hosts off the allowlist, cannot sign with a client key the user did not select for that host, and never holds cookie-encryption keys at rest (SEC-34). A compromise of a container service exposes that container's first-party jars and its DNS transport.
- **Fuzzing and verification** (NET-R4, SEC-28): per-frame coverage fuzzers for every parser; stateful connection fuzzers that drive whole connections from the server role and assert budgets (memory and CPU bounds, not only "no crash"); differential fuzzing of HTTP/1.1 framing, URL parsing, certificate path building (rustls-webpki and Go's `crypto/x509` as oracles) and TLS handshakes (rustls, BoringSSL, OpenSSL and Go's `crypto/tls` as peers) against other implementations as black-box signals; stateful TLS fuzzing from the server role, and TLS-Attacker's client scenarios ([TLS-Attacker](https://github.com/tls-attacker/TLS-Attacker)) run black-box; Kani proofs of the arithmetic kernels; RFC-derived state-machine models checked periodically against the implementation by model learning ([SR 3][SR]); the adversarial servers of `hg-net-oracle` (HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, HTTP/3, TLS, DNS, ICE and DTLS) built from the RFCs' MUST lists ([PR 10.3][PR]); and an adversarial **compromised-service harness** (IPC-R17) that drives the broker, the container service and the OS-adapter process with every forgery Section 6.1 rules out (NW-16).
- **Agent and automation interception.** WebDriver BiDi's `network.addIntercept` and the CDP Fetch domain are adapters over IIP; Izcal's interception is **scoped to an origin set and a container**, **typed by intent** (observe, mock, block, modify), **badged** on affected tabs, and **audited** (principal, request, partition, action), with bodies, cookies and credentials as sensitive classes needing a grant ([GO 8][GO]); AI off means none of this exists for agents (B10). Extensions (Stage 2) get request interception through the same scoped, audited path. **Phishing checks run before any agent or automation interception and cannot be mocked, modified, or observe-then-allowed by an automation principal** (phishing spec PH-D8).
- **Privacy.** Section 6.8's table; no telemetry; the "who received what" view is local; failure reasons never reach pages; Izcal's own traffic through OHTTP where it carries user-specific requests.

### 6.20 The wire shape (NW-D12)

A browser's TLS ClientHello, HTTP/2 SETTINGS and frame order, and QUIC transport parameters are fingerprinted (JA3, JA4, the Akamai HTTP/2 fingerprint) and used by bot management as stable client identity ([Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/bots/concepts/ja3-ja4-fingerprint/), [PP 4.1–4.3][PP]). With its own TLS stack (NW-D22), every byte of Izcal's ClientHello is Izcal's decision; 0.3.1's dependence on rustls, which declined RFC 8701 GREASE in January 2022 ([rustls issue #357](https://github.com/rustls/rustls/issues/357)), is gone. Izcal's ClientHello: **GREASE** values ([RFC 8701](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8701)) in cipher suites, groups, extensions and versions, so servers keep tolerating unknown values; **extension order permuted per connection** from `Entropy::crypto()`, so no order becomes a stable identifier and servers cannot come to depend on one; key shares X25519MLKEM768 and X25519; AEAD suites only, plus only the TLS 1.2 suites Q-NW-13's measurement justifies; ECH or GREASE ECH. Copying another browser's bytes is impersonation, which Izcal rejects; adding suites a browser does not need in order to match a fingerprint is the same thing.

The UA and any remaining choice are therefore **decided by measurement** (NW-D12): at NW-M1, the lab runs the corpus with Izcal's real handshake under (i) Stage 1 13.2's Chrome-compatible UA and (ii) an honest Izcal UA, and records challenge and block rates per bot-management vendor; a Chrome UA on a non-Chrome handshake is itself a known bot heuristic, so the combination may be worse than either honest choice. The result feeds NW-D12, NW-23 and Stage 1's UA-1. HTTP/2 settings and pseudo-header order, and QUIC transport parameters, stay within the common browser range, which Izcal's own codecs control. The owner plans **early outreach to the major bot-management vendors**, since Ladybird's case shows the vendor side is part of the fix ([PP 4.4][PP]).

### 6.22 Alternatives, and interfaces for later stages

Each decision's options, cost and reversal trigger are in Section 9.4. Rejected outright: an embedded generic HTTP, QUIC or WebRTC library (M-1 and the owner's direction); rustls or any other TLS library, and AWS-LC, ring or BoringSSL as a crypto provider (HGBA-2 2.3.2; Section 7); the platform's networking stack (URLSession, WinHTTP), which would give up partitioning, determinism and owned observability; kernel bypass (HGBA-2 4.7); io_uring (HGBA-2 4.6).

### 9.4 Decisions for the owner

**Owner decision (19 October 2026): every recommendation below is accepted**, with the reconciliation's OD-1 to OD-4 ([RN] Section 6). **NW-D24's trigger:** if the Izcal root program has not passed its credibility gate (NW-53) at the first public release, the release ships on the OS trust stores (an OS interface) and the site panel says so, until Izcal's store passes its gate; the reason is security (an unproven store would vet CAs less carefully than the OS vendors' programs), not schedule. ## 11. Open questions and flagged claims

### 11.1 Open questions

- **Q-NW-1.** (Restated in 0.3.2.) With its own TLS stack, Izcal no longer needs rustls's maintainers to accept GREASE ([issue #357](https://github.com/rustls/rustls/issues/357)). What remains is measurement: does Izcal's ClientHello (GREASE, per-connection extension permutation, AEAD-only suites) draw fewer bot-management challenges than a shape without them, and do mainstream browsers' current captures carry suites or extensions whose absence is itself a signal ([PP 4.1–4.3][PP])? (NW-M0 spike; NW-23 at NW-M1.)
- **Q-NW-2.** On Windows, can the broker pass connected TCP and bound UDP sockets into an AppContainer or LPAC process that lacks `internetClient`, with overlapped I/O and USO/URO working? (NW-M0 spike; answers SEC-D13's Windows question.)
- **Q-NW-3.** (Answered in part by HGBA-2 2.3.2.) Mozilla's CCADB data is not shipped; the CT log lists are allowed as standards data; CT log entries and CA CRLs are CA-published inputs to data Izcal generates, and Mozilla's CRLite data is no longer a fallback (NW-D21). *Closed in 0.3.3 (owner decision, 19 October 2026):* the root program may read CCADB records (audit statements, CP/CPS links, incident reports) as an input to its own reviews, without shipping or copying them as a data set (Section 6.5.2), as recommended: those documents are the CAs' disclosures, and reading them is how every root program reviews CAs.
- **Q-NW-4.** macOS receive cost without GSO and with no supported batching API: is NW-25 reachable, or does macOS need a different send and receive design?
- **Q-NW-5.** The second element of Fetch's network partition key is unsettled (Fetch issue #1035); if the standard settles on a different shape, NW-D7 follows it unless it is weaker.
- **Q-NW-6.** Who operates the OHTTP relay for NW-D17?
- **Q-NW-7.** Where does CHIPS's `Partitioned` attribute, and its ancestor bit, live once layered cookies replaces 6265bis?
- **Q-NW-8.** What order of HSTS, HTTPS records' upgrade, HTTPS-First and mixed-content autoupgrade does the deployed web expect? No specification defines it ([PR 9.5][PR]); Izcal records black-box observations and files a spec issue.
- **Q-NW-9.** Do the limits proposed in [SR R3–R15][SR] hold on a crawl of real sites (header sizes, CONTINUATION counts, QPACK table sizes)?
- **Q-NW-10.** How many partition services does a heavy user's session reach (distinct top-level sites with open tabs), and does the spare policy keep NW-47 at that count?
- **Q-NW-11.** (Answered in 0.3.2.) WireGuard's primitives come from `hg-crypto`: Izcal's own X25519, and the `chacha20poly1305` 0.11 and `blake2` 0.11 crates (MIT OR Apache-2.0, no `subtle`), with HMAC and HKDF over BLAKE2s built in `hg-crypto` from the WireGuard paper's construction (G36).
- **Q-NW-12.** Black-box observations that replace facts 0.3 read from engine source (M-1 part 3, 2.3.1; [log][SE]). Each runs against current Chrome and Firefox stable (Safari where it applies) in the lab and is recorded as a test case plus each browser's observed result: (a) at NW-M0, the network process's sandbox on each OS (`chrome://sandbox`, process token, AppContainer or seccomp status) ([SR 6.1][SR]); (b) at NW-M3, the retry schedule after HTTP/3 to an origin fails repeatedly, which checks 6.6's 5 min† start ([PT 4.4][PT]); (c) at NW-M2, whether two cross-site iframes of different sites under one top-level page share a connection or DNS lookup to one third-party origin, which checks NW-D7's comparison ([PP 1.9][PP]); (d) at NW-M1, the largest HTTP/1.1 response-header block each browser accepts, which checks R3's 256 KB† ([SR 8.1][SR]); (e) at NW-M3, idle lifetimes and liveness PINGs for QUIC, HTTP/1.1 and HTTP/2, as context for NW-33 and NW-34 ([PT 7.1][PT]); (f) at NW-M6, Chrome's upload congestion controller, as context for NW-D9 ([PT 3.1][PT]). None changes a decision unless a result contradicts the first-principles reasoning it checks; such a result is put to the owner.
- **Q-NW-13.** How many of the corpus's HTTPS origins, and of a CT-derived sample, still need TLS 1.2, and how many need a suite outside ECDHE with AEAD (CBC, RSA key exchange) or lack the extended master secret? (NW-M0 measurement, repeated yearly; decides NW-D22's TLS 1.2 scope.)
- **Q-NW-14.** (Closed 21 October 2026.) WebCrypto is owned by the WebCrypto spec (`reports/Izcal WebCrypto.md`, WC-D1): its primitives join `hg-crypto` under the 6.5 amendments, renderers link `hg-crypto` (which is why it sits in L2s), and the audit scope names both halves (NW-52). The DOM spec holds the bindings.
- **Q-NW-15.** How incomplete is an enumerated OS trust store on each OS for the corpus (Windows fills its store on demand), and does NW-D24's development interim need a workaround before the first Izcal store? (NW-M1 measurement. Since 0.3.3 it also governs a first public release that ships on the OS trust stores under NW-D24's trigger: that release needs the gap closed.)
